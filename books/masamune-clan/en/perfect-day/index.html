Perfect Day

Book 1 of Masamune Clan Specials

Some memories are bright enough to blind you. Some silences speak louder than words.

For Raito, a surprise visit from his elusive older brother offers a rare escape from the clinic and the crushing expectations of the clan. A day of archery, festival food, and a family dinner feels like the miracle he’s been waiting for.

It’s the perfect day Raito always dreamed of—but perfection always comes with a price.***

T he knock came just as I was debating whether to fake a fever or a broken leg.

Not that Master Toya would buy it. The old man could probably diagnose me from across town just by how I coughed. But it was the last day of classes before the New Year’s break, and my brain had already checked out.

The house was too quiet, the winter air too cold, and the thought of spending the morning elbow-deep in nasty herbs and snotty patients made me want to crawl back under my futon and die.

The knock came again. Three light taps, evenly spaced.

Not Father. He would have just opened the door without asking.

Not Shiro. She wasn’t here anymore.

“Door’s open,” I called, not bothering to get up from where I was sprawled on the floor, notes and anatomy diagrams spread around me.

The door slid aside.

“Morning, Raito.”

Tadashi stood in the doorway, framed by the pale winter light from the hall. Same short dark hair, same clan uniform under his long coat, same quiet expression. There was a small paper bag in his hand, grease staining the bottom.

For a second my brain just… stalled.

I hadn’t seen him in four days. Or was it five? He had come back sometime last week, I knew that much. I’d woken up one morning to find a new box of sweets on the kitchen counter, a note in his neat handwriting stuck to it.

Don’t skip breakfast. – T.

No sign of him, of course. By the time I’d dragged myself to the clinic, he’d already ghosted off to whatever assignment Father had shoved him into next.

“Tch,” I said, because apparently my first reaction to my brother being alive and in my doorway was annoyance. “You know you’re allowed to act like you live here instead of a stray cat that only comes by to drop dead mice on the mat and disappear, right?”

His lips twitched. “Good morning to you too.”

I sat up, pushing my notes aside. My heart had kicked into this stupid, traitorous fast rhythm the moment I saw him, and now it was trying to punch a hole through my ribs.

“I, uh… brought something back. From the mission.” He cleared his throat. “It’s fresh, I promise.”

“You usually just leave it on the kitchen counter and you’re gone to god knows where by the time I wake up,” I said, narrowing my eyes. “What, did you forget how doors work until today?”

A faint flush colored his cheeks. Tadashi Masamune, ex-heir of the clan and stoic torture champion, actually looked embarrassed.

“I apologize,” he said, bowing his head a little. “I’ve been… very busy.”

There it was—that careful, polite voice he used when he thought he’d done something wrong. I hated how it made my chest go tight.

He lifted his gaze again, hesitant.

“But I’m free today,” he added. “If you feel like… hanging out.”

He said it like he was asking for a favor, not offering one. Like he wasn’t sure I’d want him here.

“And if you have plans, or you’d rather rest, I understand,” he added quickly. “I just thought I should ask.”

I stared at him.

When I was a kid, if Tadashi had even half a day off, I’d staple myself to his side. I’d follow him around, pester him to show me new moves, whine until he let me sleep in his bed because my room felt too cold. I’d cried when he left for that stupid atonement mission. For eighteen months I’d wanted him back more than anything.

Then he finally came home, and I… survived without him.

I’d learned to stand on my own. To walk to school by myself. To treat my own scrapes. To deal with Father’s moods and Shiro’s sharp tongue without him smoothing everything over. I told myself I didn’t need his time or his attention anymore. That I was done begging for it.

And yet.

After the Koga mission, after seeing what they’d done to him, after realizing he hadn’t abandoned us at all… every scrap of attention he gave me had felt like something I’d been starving for without knowing it. Him poking his head into my room. Him falling asleep halfway through listening to me rant about classmates. Him ruffling my hair in the kitchen.

Lately, though, it hadn’t been like that. He still brought snacks. Still left notes I pretended not to care about. But we were just… two people existing inside the same walls. Passing each other in the hall, trading basic info, then veering off into our separate worlds.

Now he was standing here in my doorway, looking like he was bracing to be told to get lost.

“You’re free,” I repeated stupidly.

“Yes.” He adjusted his grip on the bag. His coat sleeve slid up just enough for me to glimpse the edge of a burn scar on his wrist. “For the whole day. No briefings. No errands. I checked twice.”

“Since when do you check?” The words came out more accusing than I meant.

“Since I realized I’ve been a poor older brother.” His voice dropped, losing its teasing edge.

Damn it.

My throat felt thick all of a sudden, so I scoffed and grabbed for my usual shield.

“Wow. You must really be desperate if you’re asking a brat like me to entertain you.”

His smile softened at the edges. “You’re not a brat.”

“I’m literally famous for it.”

“Not to me.”

He said it so simply that I had no idea what to do with it. I dragged a hand through my hair and looked away, pretending to examine the catastrophic mess of papers scattered on the floor.

“I have class,” I muttered.

“Ah.” He straightened a little. I felt, more than saw, the way his shoulders tensed. “Of course. I shouldn’t have come so early. I—”

“But I was just thinking about how much I hate everyone there,” I cut in. “Especially Toya. And school. And medicine. And mornings.”

A beat of silence. Then a breath that sounded suspiciously like a laugh.

“So what I’m hearing,” Tadashi said slowly, “is that you’re considering committing truancy.”

“Big word.”

“I read books when you aren’t looking.”

“Liar.”

He smiled again, the tension in his frame easing by a fraction.

“You don’t have to skip on my account,” he added. “I really will understand if—”

“ Brother .”

He blinked.

I only dragged that word out when I wanted his full attention. He shut up immediately.

“Stop talking like I’m doing you a favor,” I said. “If you’re free, we’re hanging out. That’s all.”

His eyes widened, just a little. A flicker of relief—almost shock—crossed his face before he smoothed it away.

“…All right,” he murmured. “Then I’ll take responsibility and write you an excuse note.”

“What? No.” I grimaced. “Last time you did that, Toya framed it and put it on his wall like it was some kind of royal decree. I’m never living that down.”

“I thought he needed to know why you’d fallen asleep face-first in the supply cabinet.” He sounded entirely too innocent.

“Because you kept me up three nights in a row making me practice sutures.”

“And you passed your practical exam with a perfect score.”

“That’s not the point.”

He chuckled under his breath and finally moved from the doorway. He walked over to my low table, sinking gracefully onto the floor cushion opposite me. He placed the paper bag between us. The smell that escaped when he loosened the top made my stomach growl, traitor that it was.

“What is that?” I leaned forward, sniffing. “It smells… weird. But in a good way.”

“Skewers,” he said. “From a stall by the east harbor. They marinate the meat with citrus and spice, then sear it over hot stones. I thought you’d like it. I stopped by on my way back this morning—caught the first batch off the grill.”

“You brought me breakfast skewers? From the harbor?” My eyes widened. “That’s, like, an hour away.”

“Forty minutes if you avoid the main road,” he corrected absently. Then he paused, realizing he’d just admitted to running halfway across the city before dawn just for snacks. He looked away, clearing his throat to hide the embarrassment. “I, ah, had business nearby last night.”

“Of course you did.” I tried to sound unimpressed, but my chest had gone warm.

An hour through winter wind, just to bring me hot food.

Idiot.

He tore the bag open further to make a makeshift plate, revealing two skewers. The meat glistened, steam curling up. My stomach growled louder.

“Eat first,” he said, gesturing for me to take one. “Then you can decide what you want to do.”

I eyed him, suspicion flickering, but I grabbed a skewer. “You’re not going to make this some weird training day, are you? ‘Hang out’ as in ‘run laps until you vomit’?”

“If that’s what you want.”

“It’s really not.”

“Then no.” His gaze met mine, steady. “Today is for you.”

My resolve actually stumbled.

“Don’t say such things before I’ve had meat,” I muttered, taking a bite so he wouldn’t see the heat creeping up my neck. “It’s illegal.”

“Yes, Raito.”

“You already ate?” I asked, chewing quickly.

“Yes.”

Liar. His face looked like he hadn’t slept, much less eaten, in at least twenty-four hours.

I finished the first bite. The skewer was crisp on the outside, tender inside, sour and spicy and smoky all at once. I made a strangled noise, then glared at him.

“Why are you only bringing this home now? Have you been hiding this from me on every mission?”

His mouth curved. “You were always asleep when I returned.”

“Wake me up.”

“I didn’t want to disturb you.”

“I’d rather be disturbed by food than by Toya’s morning lectures.”

“I’ll note that for next time.”

“‘Next time’ implies you’re not going to disappear for another week,” I said, but my voice came out softer than I intended.

He caught it anyway. His eyes dimmed.

“I’ll… try not to,” he said.

The room went quiet for a second, the air thick with all the things we weren’t saying. I took another bite aggressively to break it.

“Is this the vendor where people line up all the way down the street for these?” I said, pointing the half-eaten skewer at him. “You cut the line using your ninja privilege?”

Tadashi shook his head. “I waited like everyone else.”

“Wow. The mighty Masamune heir reduced to civilian status. Tragic.”

A shadow crossed his face. “I’m not the heir anymore, remember?”

I lowered the skewer, my appetite dampening slightly. “You know that’s not what I meant.”

Tadashi went quiet, eyes lowering to the table.

I hated that look—the one that slipped out whenever the topic of his demotion brushed the edge of conversation. Like it still hurt, and he was desperately trying not to let it show.

“So,” I said, forcing myself to finish the last piece of meat, “what exactly does ‘hang out’ with the great Tadashi Masamune look like? You don’t have a lot of hobbies besides ‘almost dying’ and ‘being dramatic’.”

His brows knotted. “I’m not dramatic.”

“You literally fell into a ravine.”

“There were extenuating circumstances.”

“You got branded with a magic slave tattoo on your chest.”

“That was… a work incident.”

“See? Dramatic.”

He gave up arguing. I watched him out of the corner of my eye as I dropped the empty stick onto the wrapper. The hollows under his eyes were darker than usual. His movements were precise, but there was a faint stiffness to his shoulders that meant something hurt and he didn’t want me to know.

“I thought…” He paused, as if surprised to find himself uncertain. “We could go into town. There’s a new archery range by the east river. I heard they let you customize targets. And there’s a bookstore nearby that I thought you might like. Or maybe we could just walk around the city, see what catches our interest.”

“Unstructured wandering? From you?” I raised an eyebrow. “Who are you and what have you done with my brother?”

“I can be spontaneous.”

“You color-code your mission reports.”

“That’s different. That’s organization, not—” Tadashi stopped, clearly recognizing the trap. “You’re messing with me.”

“Obviously.” I grinned and leaned back on my hands. “Fine. I’ll plan the day. Since you’re hopeless.”

“I’m not—”

“Step one,” I cut in. “We go to the range. I want to test a new arrow mix and Toya banned me from experimenting on his roof after the… incident.”

“That was one time,” he said mildly.

“It was three times.”

He tilted his head, conceding the point. “The range it is, then.”

“Step two, we raid the market before everyone else. I want festival food before the tourists get here and ruin everything.”

“Festival prep might still be underway.”

“Exactly. Fewer people. Less chance of being recognized. More skewers for me.”

He considered that. “Fair.”

“Step three…” I hesitated, feeling suddenly stupid for saying the next part out loud. “You crash in my room again. Like before. When you’re in the mood to talk instead of vanishing like some kind of ghost.”

He went very still.

“Raito,” he said quietly. “You don’t have to—”

“I didn’t say it for you,” I snapped automatically. “I sleep better when you’re snoring next to me, that’s all.”

“I don’t snore,” he protested.

“Like a dying boar.”

He huffed a breath that was almost a laugh, but there was something bright in his eyes that made me look away fast.

“Understood,” he said. “Range, market, and snoring. I’ll do my best.”

“You better.” I shoved the paper wrapper toward him to clean up and jumped to my feet. “Give me five minutes.”

“Take your time,” he said.

But I didn’t. I splashed water on my face at the basin in the corner, yanked on warmer clothes, and strapped my quiver and bow to my back with fingers that wouldn’t stop fidgeting. Through it all, a stupid, giddy buzz threaded through my veins.

Three days before New Year’s, I was skipping class to spend the day with my brother.

Even if I’d tried to pretend I didn’t care, my body had already decided for me.

Best decision I’d made in months.

T he winter sun was weak but bright when we stepped out of the house. Frost clung to the stones of the garden path, glittering like crushed glass.

Our family estate felt bigger and emptier than ever with Shiro and Father gone to the main complex. It looked so wrong without Shiro’s voice cutting through the air or Father’s cold critiques echoing from the training hall.

My chest tightened. I shoved the feeling down and adjusted the strap of my bow case on my shoulder.

Tadashi waited for me by the gate, his coat buttoned to his throat, scarf wound neatly around his neck. He’d swapped his mission boots for lighter walking shoes, as if to prove this was a casual day off.

“You okay?” he asked quietly as I approached.

“Fine.” I hopped down the last step, landing with a crunch on the frosted ground. “Just thinking about how badly I’m going to beat you.”

His mouth twitched. “Confident.”

“Realistic.”

“The range is a twenty-minute walk,” he noted, checking the position of the sun. “If we sprint, we can make it in ten.”

“Race?” I challenged, already shifting my weight.

“You’re carrying equipment,” he pointed out, eyeing my bow case.

“So? I’m still faster.”

“Is that so?”

I didn’t answer. I just bolted.

My breath burned cold in my lungs, the air biting my cheeks. For a blissful stretch of seconds, there was nothing but the sound of my footfalls and the rush of blood in my ears. The city streets were still relatively quiet this early, giving me a clear path.

Then a shadow flickered at my side, and Tadashi slid past me, moving with that effortless, infuriating grace of his. He wasn’t even breathing hard.

“Showoff!” I yelled, pumping my legs harder.

He laughed and slowed just enough for me to catch up. We reached the destination at almost the same time, skidding to a stop near the entrance of the commercial archery grounds. Our breath steamed in the air in white puffs.

He glanced at me, eyes bright, cheeks flushed from the run.

“You’re faster,” he said, straightening his scarf.

“You let me catch up.”

“You’re still faster than last spring.”

I made a noncommittal noise, trying not to look too pleased.

The archery range was tucked into a wide lot by the river, away from the bustling market district. Unlike our clan’s private dojo with its austere traditional targets, this place was built for public entertainment. It boasted moveable targets on rail systems, some shaped like animals or silhouettes, others like colored rings and rotating wheels.

I stopped just inside the entrance.

“Oh,” I said, genuine delight spilling out before I could check it. “Oh, this is… kind of amazing.”

“I thought you’d like it,” Tadashi said softly.

I shot him a look that meant shut up before I say something embarrassing , then marched over to the counter. Tadashi paid for a two-hour lane and a storage locker for later; the girl at the register looked at him a second too long, blushing when he thanked her with automatic politeness.

I made a face as we walked away toward our assigned lane.

“Unbelievable,” I muttered. “You exist and other people get to see you. That’s so unfair.”

Tadashi blinked, handing me the receipt. “What?”

“Nothing. Get a lane, pretty boy.”

We fell into an easy rhythm: me unpacking my bow, him checking the mechanical targets, both of us moving like we’d done this a thousand times. Because we had, just in a different setting.

“New poison mix, you said,” Tadashi said, setting a board at twenty meters. “What does it do?”

“Paralyzes the target’s legs without affecting the lungs or heart,” I said, pulling a small vial from my pouch. The liquid inside gleamed a faint blue. “Useful for capture missions. Less murder, more inconvenient immobility.”

His brow creased slightly. “You’ve been working with Toya on it?”

“Obviously.” I put the vial back in my pouch. “You think I developed a complex neurotoxin between homework assignments?”

“I think you’re capable of more than you give yourself credit for,” he said mildly.

I rolled my eyes to hide the tiny flicker of satisfaction his words sparked.

“Don’t start.” I nocked the arrow and drew, the familiar weight settling into my shoulders. “You’re not allowed to feed my ego and then vanish on me again. It’s disorienting.”

“I’ll keep that in mind,” he said.

I inhaled, feeling the bowstring kiss the corner of my mouth. Exhaled. Released.

The arrow blurred, thudding into the target with a satisfying smack—just off center.

“Tch.” I lowered the bow. “Wind’s different here. River breeze.”

“Not bad,” Tadashi said.

“‘Not bad’ is what you say when it sucks but you don’t want to hurt feelings.”

A flicker of amusement crossed his face. “You’re overthinking it.”

He walked to the board, inspecting the impact. “Half a finger’s length right,” he called back. “But the angle is good. Poison should penetrate cleanly.”

“Should?” I raised a brow. “Do I need to get you a rabbit to shoot?”

“That won’t be necessary.” He returned to my side, eyes scanning the trees beyond the fence, always alert. “We can test the dosage on practice dummies at the clinic later when Toya isn’t looking.”

“If he forgives me for skipping.”

“He will.” Mirth danced in his eyes. “He likes you too much not to.”

“He likes my hands.” I flexed my fingers, smirking. “Says I have good technique.”

“You do.” Tadashi’s gaze lingered on my hands for a beat, then lifted to my face. “I’m proud of you, Raito.”

“Idiot,” I muttered, looking away fast and grabbing another arrow so he wouldn’t see my ears turning red. “Show me how you would do it.”

Tadashi accepted a rental bow from the rack nearby—since he hadn’t brought his own—checked the tension, tested the draw. It was inferior to what he was used to, but in his hands, it looked like a legendary weapon.

He fired three arrows in quick succession.

Three perfect center hits, one on a target that had just started moving.

I stared.

“I hate you,” I said, but there was no heat in it.

“You asked,” Tadashi replied, maddeningly calm.

“You’re supposed to lose on purpose so I feel good about myself.”

“That would be lying.”

“Yeah, to make me happy.”

Tadashi hesitated, lowering the bow.

“I don’t… want to insult you,” he said finally. “By pretending you’re worse than you are. You’re good, Raito. Really good. You don’t need me to pretend to prove that.”

My throat tightened. I swallowed hard and forced a scoff. “Don’t get sappy on me, old man.”

We spent the next hour shooting in comfortable silence. He adjusted my stance now and then, a light touch to my elbow, my back. Corrected my grip. Complimented my form more than I wanted to admit I liked hearing.

I stole glances when he wasn’t looking. The way the winter light caught in his hair. The way his eyes narrowed whenever he scanned the perimeter, as if he half expected the bushes to spit out an army of skeletons. The way his right hand flexed occasionally, fingers brushing over the front of his coat where I knew the Fuun brand burned into his chest lay hidden.

He moved like he was fine. Like all of this—everything the clan had done to him—was just another training weight he’d gotten used to carrying.

Minutes blurred into an hour. We switched to trickier targets—rings that swung unpredictably, small wooden discs that popped up at random intervals.

I pushed myself, power humming faintly under my skin but unused; this was for fun, not killing. Sweat dampened my shirt, breath coming faster, but the steady weight of Tadashi at my side made everything feel… lighter.

At one point, I loosed an arrow at a small disc that had sprung up late, sure I’d missed. Another arrow whistled past mine, striking the disc dead center and shattering it.

“You—” I started, spinning. “That was my target.”

“You hesitated,” Tadashi said, lowering his bow. “An enemy wouldn’t.”

“This isn’t a mission, you freak!”

Tadashi’s eyes crinkled.

“Then next time,” he said, “shoot faster.”

I threw a towel at his head. He caught it one-handed, unbothered.

Just as he was about to correct my posture again, I blurted out:

“Hey.”

He stilled, hand hovering near my shoulder. “Yes?”

“Who’s watching your back on the mission?”

He stiffened, thrown by the sudden change of subject. “For New Year?”

“No, for the mission next summer,” I snapped. “Yes, for New Year.”

He lowered his hand slowly. “The usual team. Shiro. Sadi. Anko. Two Fuma we’ve worked with before.”

“And you’re guarding the princess again.”

“That’s the plan.”

My grip tightened on the bow. “The last time you guarded her, you came back with exhausted power coils and half your ribs cracked.”

His brows drew together. “That was a different assignment. Different conditions.”

“And this time?” I pressed. “Any chance you won’t end up in a torture dungeon?”

He hesitated. It was small, barely a breath’s worth, but I saw it. He saw that I saw it.

“The conditions will be controlled,” he said carefully. “Shiro and Father have been working on the security plan for weeks. The Fuma will be there. It’ll be alright.”

None of that was an actual answer.

“It’s just another mission, Raito,” he added softly.

“That’s the problem,” I muttered. “For you, ‘just another mission’ always means ‘maybe I get dismembered today, who knows, fun surprise’.”

He stared at me for a long moment.

“You’re worried,” he said finally.

“No,” I shot back. “I’m pissed.”

“At what?”

“At Father. At the clan. At this entire stupid system that looks at you and thinks, ‘Wow, what a great punching bag! Let’s hurl him into another death trap and see if he bounces’.”

“Raito.” His tone held a quiet warning now.

I glared at him. “You don’t even deny it. Why do you have to do whatever assignments Father tells you to?”

“Because I’m capable.” His voice sharpened. “And because every mission I complete lessens the burden on the clan.”

“The clan can choke,” I snapped.

“Raito.”

“What?” Heat flooded my veins, a volatile cocktail of too many feelings at once. Anger. Fear. The ghost of my own voice from years ago, screaming at him to die. “I’m not allowed to be mad that my brother keeps volunteering to bleed for people who barely see him as human?”

He flinched, just barely. The line of his mouth tightened.

“I’m not volunteering to die,” he said, his voice steel. “I’m… making use of what I have.”

“What you have is suicidal tendencies and a hero complex.”

“Raito.”

“Don’t ‘Raito’ me, Brother.”

We stood facing each other in the cold lane, breath misting between us. For a second, it felt like the Koga mission and the ravine again—everything raw and exposed.

He looked away first.

“I hear you,” he said. The words came out low, rough. “I do. And I’m… really sorry. For making you worry.”

I hated that apology. Hated how I knew he meant it.

“Then stop giving me reasons to,” I muttered.

He was silent for a long moment. When he lifted his gaze again, something steady had settled there.

“I can’t promise I won’t be in danger,” he said. “But I can promise I’ll do everything I can to come back.”

I frowned. “You already promised that.”

“I know.” His expression was somber. “I’m promising again.”

It wasn’t enough. It would never be enough. But I also knew there was nothing I could say that would drag him off this path. The clan had sunk its hooks too deep.

“Tch.” I turned away, yanking another arrow from my quiver. “You better. Otherwise I’ll find whatever afterlife you end up in and kill you myself.”

His laugh was soft. “That’s a terrifying image.”

“Good.”

We went back to shooting. The sharp edge of the moment dulled, smoothed over by repetition, by the whistle of arrows and the thud of impact. By the time my shoulders started to ache pleasantly, the tension between us had settled into something less likely to explode.

“Range done,” I announced, loosening the string on my bow and starting to pack it into its case. “Now feed me again.”

“As you command,” he said, with a mock bow.

“Don’t say it like that. It’s creepy.”

“Yes, Raito.”

T he market near the lower town was already buzzing by the time we got there, even though the festival was still three days away. Colorful banners hung half-unrolled from stall fronts. Vendors argued good-naturedly over stall placement. The air smelled like grilled fish, sweet rice, smoke, and cold river water.

Tadashi tugged his scarf a little higher as we slipped into the crowd, instinctively shifting his posture. To anyone else, he was just another young man in a simple coat, hands tucked into his sleeves, gaze downcast. Invisible.

To me, he was a walking target. The set of his shoulders screamed “trained fighter,” no matter how much he tried to hide it.

“Relax,” I muttered, bumping his arm with my elbow. “You’re off duty, remember?”

“I am relaxed.”

“You look like you’re about to assassinate the sweet bun vendor.”

He actually glanced at the vendor, then back at me. “I like their sweet buns.”

“Then stop glaring at them.”

“I wasn’t—” He paused, then sighed. “I’ll try.”

We wandered past stalls selling dried fish, pickled vegetables, cheap toys, good luck charms. I stopped at every food cart that smelled remotely interesting. Skewers, roasted chestnuts, some weird fried dough thing that turned out to be disgusting and made Tadashi laugh himself breathless while I choked it down out of pride.

“You’re cruel,” I said, wiping grease from my chin with the back of my hand.

“I warned you it was offered at a suspicious discount.”

“And you still let me eat it.”

“I’m respecting your independence.”

“Jerk.”

His eyes crinkled at the corners. “You’re growing up.”

“Don’t say it like that. Makes me feel like I’m eighty.”

We found another food stall near the market district—one of the ones that sold the sweet dumplings I had loved since childhood. Tadashi bought two servings in paper bowls without being asked, because of course he remembered. He also grabbed two cups of hot tea from the neighboring cart.

We sat on a low wall overlooking the river, legs dangling, eating in comfortable silence. The winter sun was pale but warm on my face. Somewhere downstream, children were playing, their laughter carrying on the breeze.

“Remember when you used to bring me here?” I asked around a mouthful of dumpling. “After training?”

Tadashi nodded. “You’d always try to eat your serving and half of mine.”

“I was a growing boy. I needed the fuel.”

“You were a tiny gremlin who couldn’t finish half a bowl of rice.”

I kicked at Tadashi’s ankle. “Slander. Lies. I was a perfectly normal-sized child.”

Tadashi laughed—a bright, genuine sound that seemed to startle even him—and I felt something warm bloom in my chest. When was the last time I’d heard Tadashi laugh like that? Not the polite chuckle he used with clan members, or the tired half-smile he wore when he was running on no sleep. This was… light. Easy.

Like the old days.

“I missed this,” I heard myself say. The words slipped out before I could stop them.

Tadashi went still beside me. When I risked a glance, my brother’s expression was soft, almost fragile.

“I missed this too,” Tadashi murmured.

We sat in silence for a while. I finished my dumplings and started on the second serving—the one Tadashi had subtly slid toward me without eating more than two bites himself.

“You should eat more,” I said, nudging the bowl back toward my brother. “You’re too skinny.”

“I’m not—”

“You’re literally wasting away. I can see your cheekbones. It’s disturbing.”

Tadashi huffed a laugh and obediently speared a dumpling with his skewer. I watched him chew, cataloging the details I’d missed in recent weeks—the dark circles under Tadashi’s eyes, the new tension in his shoulders. But for now, watching the river, he looked… peaceful.

“Hey,” I said, poking his arm with my empty skewer. “Thanks. For today.”

Tadashi glanced at me, surprised. “You don’t have to thank me.”

“I know. But usually, your idea of ‘spending time together’ involves you supervising while I patch up bleeding trainees. This is…” I gestured vaguely at the dumpling and the view with the bamboo stick. “Actual fun.”

“I’m glad.” He took a sip of his tea, steam curling around his nose. “I wanted to do something… just for us. Not as clan members. Just brothers.”

“Well,” I grumbled, looking away to hide the heat creeping up my neck. “It’s not terrible. Even if I am an obligation you have to entertain.”

“You’re not an obligation, Raito.”

I looked back. His voice had dropped to that quiet, fierce tone he usually saved for protecting me from Father.

“Spending time with you is never a duty,” he said firmly. “It’s the best part of my life.”

I stared at him, my throat suddenly tight. I kicked his ankle lightly to break the intensity.

“You’re so weird sometimes,” I muttered. “So sappy.”

“And you’re deflecting.”

“Maybe.” I grinned. “But you love me anyway.”

“I do,” Tadashi agreed simply, and the sincerity in those two words made my heart clench.

We finished the tea, tossing the empty cups and bowls into a bin nearby, before wandering back into the crowd. We paused at a stall selling decorative fans. Tadashi examined them with genuine interest while the elderly vendor launched into an enthusiastic explanation of her craft.

I watched my brother nod politely… and felt a familiar pang of nostalgia. This was the Tadashi I remembered from childhood—the one who could find something interesting in anything, who treated everyone with equal respect regardless of their status or importance.

“Do you like this one?” Tadashi asked suddenly, holding up a fan painted with cherry blossoms.

“It’s nice,” I said, confused. “Why?”

“It made me think of you. You used to collect fallen cherry blossoms when you were little. You’d press them in books.”

I blinked, surprised that Tadashi remembered such a small detail. “That was years ago.”

“I know. But you loved them.” Tadashi turned to the vendor. “We’ll take this one, please.”

“Brother, you don’t have to—”

“I want to,” Tadashi said simply, already reaching for his coin purse inside his coat.

The vendor wrapped the fan carefully in brown paper, beaming at us. “Such a good older brother! Your younger brother is very lucky.”

I felt my face heat, but I didn’t argue. When Tadashi handed me the wrapped fan, our fingers brushed briefly, and I found myself smiling despite my embarrassment. I tucked the slender package into the inner pocket of my jacket for safekeeping.

“Thank you,” I said quietly.

Tadashi’s answering smile was soft. “You’re welcome.”

We continued through the market, stopping occasionally when something caught our attention. Tadashi seemed determined to look at everything, as if memorizing the colors of the stalls.

“Is there anything else you want, Raito?” he asked suddenly, stopping near a weapons stall. “We have time.”

I narrowed my eyes at him. “Since when are you this generous? Did Father finally approve your expense report?”

Tadashi paused, then gave a vague, tight smile. “Something like that. Consider it a late holiday bonus.”

“Hmm.” I pretended to think hard. “A new bow, three sets of custom arrows, and a lifetime supply of those skewers from this morning.”

He chuckled. “I was thinking something small. Within our budget.”

“Cheapskate.”

We ended up at a small stall selling festival masks. Foxes, oni, dragons, rabbits. I grabbed a fox mask with bright red markings and slapped it against my face.

“How do I look?” I asked, voice muffled.

“Like trouble,” he said dryly.

“So, normal.”

He reached past me and picked up a plain white mask with a single black streak across the eye.

“Too dramatic,” I declared at once.

He looked at it for a long moment. “It’s simple.”

“It’s creepy.”

“It’s practical.” He flipped it over, checking the strap. “Covers enough of the face without limiting visibility.”

“You’re not allowed to turn a fun mask into tactical gear.”

He glanced at me over the top of it, something wry in his eyes. “Habit.”

I stuck the fox mask back. “Get that one.” I pointed to a simple boar mask with a slightly crooked snout. “It matches your snoring.”

“I don’t—”

“Boar.”

He sighed. “If I buy this, will you stop accusing me of snoring?”

“No. But I’ll have visual aid.”

He bought both masks anyway. One fox, one boar.

“For later,” he said, handing me the fox. “When the streets are full and you want to move without being recognized.”

“See?” I said. “Tactical gear.”

He inclined his head. “I learned from the best.”

“Who, me?”

“Of course.”

He tucked the masks into a cloth bag the vendor provided, hooking the strings over his wrist so his hands remained free.

We walked until my legs ached. Tadashi kept the pace slow, always half a step to my right, the way he used to when I was little and more likely to trip over my own feet. His hand hovered near my shoulder whenever the crowd thickened, ready to pull me out of danger even here, in our own territory.

Ninjas, I thought sourly. We didn’t know how to turn it off.

But as the afternoon light slanted golden between the roofs, as festival lanterns began to flicker to life above us, I realized something.

I was… happy.

Not the quick burst of joy I got from landing a perfect shot or acing a test. Not the manic high of running into a fight and surviving by the skin of my teeth.

A quieter thing. A steady hum under my skin.

Just walking. Just eating. Just existing next to him.

No life-or-death crises. No yelling. No tears.

It was… nice.

If anyone had told thirteen-year-old me that I’d someday consider “spending the day running boring errands with Tadashi” the highlight of my week, I would’ve shot them in the knee.

Shows what I knew.
