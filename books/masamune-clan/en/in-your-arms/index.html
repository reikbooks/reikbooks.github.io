In Your Arms

Book 13 of Masamune Clan Series

He only wanted his father’s forgiveness. Stripped of his title and crushed by guilt, Tadashi Masamune follows his father back to their clan on a journey of penance—clinging to the fragile dream of earning his place again. But when their path twists into a nightmare of memory and pain, what price will Tadashi pay to save the man who bound him in silence?

They say dying is easy—it’s the waking that breaks you.

T adashi had learned this truth twice now. Once in Ikaru Mitsurugi’s hands, when darkness became mercy and each breath a betrayal of his body’s desire to stop. And again months later, when his father’s voice dragged him back from the peace of oblivion.

He’d expected fury. Punishment. The cold disappointment that had shadowed him since childhood.

What he hadn’t expected was responsibility .

The King’s shattered mirror. The mountain of debt. The clan’s ruin—all of it his fault, his father said. Tadashi had accepted it without question because that’s what dutiful sons did. Because maybe if he carried this burden, if he bled himself empty paying for his mistakes, his father might finally look at him and see something worth keeping.

Three days. That’s all Shirasu had bought him. Three days to remember how to stand without swaying, to eat without retching, to pretend the weight of his own body didn’t feel like drowning.

Now the three days were gone, and his father was already pushing the horse faster.

Tadashi pressed his forehead against Masada’s back and tried not to think about how far they still had to ride. Tried not to think about the sealed memories that lurked just beyond his reach—phantom pains with no source, fears with no name.

Tried not to think about Shirasu’s words: Live with your choice. If anything happens, don’t come running to me . His brother had looked betrayed. Shirasu had said nothing when he let Tadashi go.

And somehow, that silence hurt most of all.

A wave of nausea clawed its way up Tadashi’s throat. He swallowed hard, fighting the urge to throw up.

His undershirt was drenched in a cold sweat. Darkness clung stubbornly to the edges of his vision, refusing to fade no matter how many times he blinked. A sharp cramp seized his neck and shoulders, worsened by the weight of his pack. His hands trembled as he struggled to keep his grip on Masada’s tunic while the horse thundered on. Every stride sent a fresh jolt of pain through his body.

Tadashi closed his eyes, trying to steady his breathing. How long had they been riding? There had been no breaks, not even a moment’s rest.

He knew Masada was desperate to return to the clan after days away. An unspoken accusation hung in the air—Tadashi was the reason for their delay. Masada didn’t want to waste another second, and Tadashi understood.

He didn’t want to be a burden. He wanted to meet his father’s expectations, but his body felt utterly spent. A dull, throbbing ache radiated down his spine, urging him to curl into a ball. If they kept up this pace, he would surely pass out.

“F-Father,” he whispered, his voice barely audible over the rushing wind.

Masada couldn’t have heard.

Tadashi tugged weakly on his father’s kimono sleeve. Masada didn’t stop immediately, but he slowed the horse.

“What is it?”

Tadashi closed his eyes, his throat tight with pain.

“Father, please… could we stop for a moment?”

Masada cleared his throat.

“Why? Do you need to relieve yourself?”

Tadashi didn’t want to admit he just needed to rest, so he nodded against his father’s back.

“Can you hold on a little longer? There’s a cliff and a river up ahead. We’ll stop there, all right?”

Tadashi bit his lip, heart pounding. He didn’t know how much longer he could last. He took a deep breath, burying the pain and fear.

“Yes, Father,” he choked out, summoning the last of his strength to stay conscious.

When the horse finally halted by the riverbank, Tadashi’s vision was already blurring. His body trembled violently, and he slumped heavily against his father. He swayed as Masada abruptly released his grip.

Masada dismounted swiftly, his eyes scanning their surroundings. He said nothing, but his expression was tense and agitated.

“Hurry, Tadashi. We don’t have much time,” Masada said, his voice low and clipped.

Still in the saddle, Tadashi winced. Swallowing hard, he mustered the last of his energy to dismount unaided. He slid painfully from the horse, collapsing to his knees on the ground. His pack slipped off and landed beside him. For a moment, he could only stay on all fours, gasping for air, fighting the rising nausea and the urge to black out.

Somehow, he forced himself to stand. Staggering to the riverbank, he vomited up the little that was in his stomach. The nausea clawed him, wracking his throat with spasms long after there was nothing left.

It hurt.

Weakness threatened to pull him under. Tadashi gritted his teeth, his fists clenching as ragged breaths sawed from his lungs. With every gasp, the words formed in his mind.

I endure. I endure.

He fought for a full breath, but his chest was too tight. His heart pounded a frantic rhythm against his ribs. His head swam, and he blinked hard against the dark fog threatening to swallow his sight.

Come on, get a grip. Don’t be more trouble. Control your breath. Calm down. Yes, like that.

He wrestled his breathing into a slower rhythm. His hands dug into the coarse, damp grass, anchoring him. The river’s whisper sounded like a taunt, the rustling leaves a warning. The thunder’s low growl vibrated in his bones. A cold wind swept through, carrying the scent of rain. He gulped it down like a drowning man, pushing back the nausea.

When he blinked, his vision sharpened on the angry, bruised clouds devouring the sky. Thick. Dark. Ominous.

Shelter. Now.

A frantic scan found it: shallow alcoves pocking the rocky hillside. They looked cramped, but if they squeezed in, he and his father might just fit.

But what if Masada wanted to press on?

Tadashi’s gut wrenched. He’d never make it. He’d collapse on the road, a dead weight. A final, unforgivable burden.

No. Stop. Don’t go down that path . Just focus on what’s right in front of you. One thing at a time.

With trembling hands, Tadashi scooped water from the river and washed his face. The cold was a shock, washing away the heat and sweat. It cleared his head. For a moment, he felt a bit better.

He could do this. He had to.

This was all for his father.

He took a long breath and stood. Dizziness made him stumble. He closed his eyes, regulating his breathing again. When he felt steady enough to walk without falling, he turned back toward their horse, where Masada was waiting.

Nervously, Tadashi glanced at his father’s face, afraid to see anger or disappointment. As usual, Masada’s expression was unreadable, a closed mask. But his body language screamed impatience. Tadashi swallowed hard.

“I’m sorry to have kept you waiting, Father,” he murmured, bowing his head before settling to Masada’s right.

Masada gestured toward the bento box lying in front of Tadashi.

“Eat,” he commanded, his voice clipped. He placed a water bottle beside the box.

Tadashi opened the lunchbox and stared at its contents. Shirasu had packed soft rice, a fish fillet, a hard-boiled egg, and a sweet, easy-to-swallow snack. His stomach churned, rebelling at the very thought of food, but Masada’s sharp, insistent gaze left no room for refusal. Fighting a fresh wave of nausea, Tadashi forced the meal down as quickly as he could. He couldn’t bear a scolding for being slow.

He washed the food down with a sip from the bottle Masada had given him. A sudden warmth bloomed in his core, soothing the tremors that wracked his body. A warming potion. The small comfort was so startling he looked up, but Masada was already turning away, getting to his feet.

“Finish it,” his father said. “We leave in five minutes.”

Tadashi’s gaze dropped, the brief hope for respite crushed under the heavy fatigue. Of course. There was no room for his weakness. He ignored the tightness in his chest.

“Yes, Father,” he answered, his voice tight as he choked down the last of his meal.

Masada readied their horse while Tadashi hastily washed the lunchbox. But as soon as he stood, a commotion erupted from the undergrowth.

Bandits!

They surged forward, weapons drawn. Tadashi’s heart hammered against his ribs as he grabbed his katana, a cold sweat blooming on his skin. This was the worst possible moment. He was in no condition to fight, but choice was a luxury he didn’t have.

CLANG!

Steel shrieked as he met the first attack. He dodged, parried, his movements fueled by adrenaline alone. The bandit pressed him, his strikes a relentless flurry. Then two more joined in, their blades a whirlwind he could barely track. Tadashi moved on pure reflex, spinning and deflecting, his breath catching in his throat.

An opening! He seized it, bringing his katana down in a vertical slash. The bandit was thrown back, slamming into a tree with a sickening crunch. But the jarring impact reverberated up the steel, exploding into a white-hot agony in his arm and shoulder. His weak muscles screamed, threatening to buckle. Gritting his teeth, he dodged another wild cut, his movements turning clumsy and desperate. He launched a frantic counterattack. One bandit fell. Then another.

More bandits emerged, larger and more skilled, driving Tadashi back. A fist shattered his defense, slamming into his solar plexus and stealing his breath in a burst of white pain. He fought back, but his strength was failing him. His arms shook as he blocked a heavy strike.

A quick glance showed Masada, a whirlwind of skill, holding off his own swarm of attackers. The sight sent a jolt of pure fear through Tadashi, overriding logic. It didn’t matter that his father was a master warrior. His own duty, ingrained from countless missions, was singular: protect the clan leader . His own survival was secondary. He had to protect him. He would die first before he let his father fall. Forcing his weary body to move, he split his focus, parrying his own attackers while constantly tracking the threats converging on his father.

And then he saw it.

A bandit, moving like a shadow, striking from Masada’s blind spot. The realization was a lightning strike. His father, surrounded and pressed by five opponents, was vulnerable. Tadashi didn’t think. He acted.

He threw his own body into the path of the attack.

The enemy’s sword crashed against his katana with staggering force. Tadashi gasped as the impact blasted up his arm, shattering his guard. A brutal kick to his ribs sent him sprawling into Masada’s back. For a terrifying second, pain eclipsed everything. He couldn’t draw air. His vision swam as his knees gave way.

No!

Nausea rising, he gasped, struggling to push himself up. He had messed up. As he met his father’s eyes, Masada’s glare was sharper than his blade.

“What are you doing? Get up!” his father snarled, seizing his arm and yanking him upright.

Before Tadashi could steady himself, his father shoved him forward, straight into the swarm of bandits. Blows rained down, driving him back, back, until the churning river was at his heels.

A bandit’s slash broke through his faltering guard. Tadashi cried out as a searing pain tore through his arm. His katana flew from his numb fingers. A brutal kick landed in his ribs.

A sickening crack.

White-hot agony electrocuted his senses, driving the air from his lungs in a silent gasp. His body convulsed, arms instinctively clutching his side as he collapsed. He tried to rise, to fight, but the bandits hammered him down from all sides.

A punch to the jaw sent him reeling. Another kick lifted him off his feet and hurled him into the river. The water hit him like a wall.

Pain screamed through his ribs. He gasped and choked on river water. The current seized him, dragging him under in a violent tumble. He thrashed, broke the surface for a single, choking breath, and was immediately pulled under again.

Father!

Tadashi tried to scream, but the raging current dragged him under again. Through the roaring water, he faintly heard Masada shouting his name. He clawed for the surface, his bruised ribs screaming, breaking through for a single, desperate gasp before the river reclaimed him.

His strength was gone. His struggles were useless. His chest was on fire, his lungs ready to burst. Pain and terror locked around him like a vise. In that moment, Tadashi knew he wasn’t going to make it.

This was it.

He would die a failure, unable to keep his promise. The debt to the King would go unpaid, crushing the clan with an impossible burden.

Masada’s words echoed in his mind.

All our clan members… innocent people and their children, they will be forced to work as the kingdom’s slaves. They will bear your mistake until they die. Can you imagine their fate, Tadashi?

Tadashi choked on the memory.

He knew. He knew all too well.

His punishment mission had been a mere glimpse of that hell. His back remembered the whip. His body recalled the blows. His stomach still clenched with the ghost of hunger. A deep, soul-crushing exhaustion was his constant shadow.

He had damned them all to it. The knowledge was a fire in his chest, burning him from the inside out. A wave of nausea twisted his gut. The water pressed in, a suffocating weight.

Help.

The silent prayer was for his father, a desperate wish sent into the void. He had no words, only a crushing pressure in his chest.

Please.

He never meant for this. He never wanted anyone to suffer but himself. The fault was his; the price should be his alone.

Father…

Icy river water flooded his throat. His lungs screamed, ready to burst.

I’m so sorry.

Through the murky water, the surface shimmered, impossibly distant.

Please… forgive me.

His last breath escaped in a stream of bubbles. Then everything went dark.

T he rain fell in punishing sheets, lashing the earth without mercy.

Masada fought against the current, muscles burning. The river, swollen by the storm upstream, pulled at him with increasing force.

He despised unpredictability. As a man who lived by meticulous planning and sharp intuition, he rarely miscalculated. He had expected Tadashi’s obedience. He knew his son hadn’t fully recovered, but that was no excuse to delay their return.

Tadashi had always been stubbornly resilient. He would endure.

What Masada hadn’t anticipated was the depth of his son’s weakness. The bandits had been amateurish, their attacks clumsy and easily countered. The boy could fight. Which made his subsequent foolishness all the more inexplicable.

The sequence was clear in Masada’s mind. He hadn’t been looking directly, but his awareness had tracked Tadashi’s position behind him. Their defensive formation was sound. Tadashi wouldn’t have been vulnerable if he hadn’t recklessly altered his stance, creating an opening for the bandit to exploit.

Like a poorly placed stone triggering an avalanche, that single error had shifted the entire battle.

Masada had moved to correct it, cutting down his opponents with ruthless efficiency. He was nearly finished when he heard the unmistakable splash.

Tadashi had gone into the river.

Instinct overrode calculation.

He couldn’t lose his son. Not after the considerable effort expended to find and retrieve him. The boy was essential to settling the clan’s debt.

Rage, cold and sharp, eclipsed all other thought. Masada became a whirlwind of violence, dispatching the remaining bandits without a second glance. He took the most direct path to the water’s edge and plunged in.

He swam with desperate strength. The current was a living thing, trying to tear Tadashi away. He caught a glimpse of his son’s face, pale and terrified, before the water swallowed him whole.

A searing pain lanced through Masada’s chest, sharp enough to steal his breath.

“Tadashi!”

His shout was devoured by the storm. He drew a deep breath and dove.

Masada broke the surface, gasping. Rain hammered his face. With a final, Herculean effort, he hauled Tadashi’s limp body from the water, slinging him over a shoulder. He fought his way to the bank, each step a battle against the rising flood.

He didn’t stop. The water would only get higher. Scanning the hillside through the downpour, he spotted the dark mouth of a cave. Gritting his teeth, he half-carried, half-dragged Tadashi toward it, his own energy reserves dangerously low.

Inside the cave, he dropped Tadashi unceremoniously onto the stone floor. The boy lay utterly still, his skin a deathly bluish-white, bruises already blooming on his jaw. Masada knelt, pressing two fingers to Tadashi’s neck. The pulse was there, but thready and faint.

He sat back, wiping rainwater from his face, forcing his breathing to steady.

Just in time. The boy had nearly drowned.

He took a moment to assess their situation. The storm showed no sign of abating. They were safe for now, but he had no idea how far the current had carried them from their horse and supplies.

Realistically, Masada held little hope. Scavengers like those bandits wouldn’t leave valuable goods behind. His horse was likely already stolen.

But he had to be sure. Without supplies, the journey would become exponentially harder and longer. He had money, but the nearest village was dozens of kilometers away. On foot, with Tadashi in this state, they would be critically delayed. And time was a luxury they did not have.

Masada glanced at the darkening sky. Action was required, regardless of his preference. He rose and erected a simple protective barrier around Tadashi. Without a backward glance, he turned and ran back into the storm.

Fifteen minutes later, disorientation set in. The riverbank was a featureless wilderness. He remembered a high cliff, but every cliff looked the same. Just as doubt crept in, he found a bandit’s corpse on the bank. A landmark.

He pushed on, arriving at the spot where he’d tethered the horse.

It was empty. The silence was a mockery. The bandits were gone, and they had taken everything.

Fury, hot and bitter, surged through him. Masada drove his fist into the trunk of a nearby tree. Nothing was going according to plan.

Tadashi was a liability. Their return would be delayed past the clan elders’ deadline. Shiro would be appointed leader in his absence.

He would be forced to step down—a move that would raise uncomfortable questions and strain their diplomatic standing with the King. Securing future missions would become even more difficult.

Unless… unless the clan delayed the official announcement to the other clans. There was still time before the next inter-clan council. An internal appointment could remain secret for a short while. It was a slim chance, but it existed.

He grunted in frustration. He had been too optimistic, underestimating the complications Tadashi would bring. He should have built more time into the letter to the elders.

Enough. Consequences could be dealt with later. He had to focus on the immediate problem.

He had to get Tadashi on his feet and drag him home.

Back in the cave, the barrier held. Tadashi remained unconscious, his pallor corpse-like. A quick check confirmed he was still alive, but his skin was icy, his body wracked with shivers.

Masada clicked his tongue, annoyed he hadn’t started a fire earlier. He hadn’t planned on staying. From a hidden storage seal, he retrieved a power stone the size of his fist—a valuable black-market acquisition. He quickly activated a seal on it.

Ember .

The stone flared to life, glowing with a dim, warm light that filled the cave. Its heat was comparable to a small campfire. He turned to Tadashi. He’d given him a warming potion earlier, hoping it would suffice. Now, a stamina potion was necessary, but their water bottles—like everything else—were gone.

Suppressing his irritation, Masada shook Tadashi’s shoulder.

“Tadashi. Wake up.”

There was no response but a low groan. Masada exhaled sharply. Drastic measures were required. He placed a hand on Tadashi’s neck and invoked the Ember Seal.

Tadashi’s eyes flew open, wide with agony. A raw, strangled scream tore from his scorched throat. His body convulsed violently. Masada broke the seal. A red, hand-shaped brand marked Tadashi’s skin.

On the floor, Tadashi curled into a fetal position, trembling, tears leaking from his eyes. His gaze was vacant, uncomprehending. Masada grabbed his shoulder and shook him again. Slowly, Tadashi’s awareness returned.

“Tadashi. Get up. We’re leaving now,” Masada commanded, his voice leaving no room for argument.

Recognition and then pain flooded Tadashi’s expression. His eyes glistened as he stared at his father. He winced, mouth opening as if to speak, but only a hoarse, guttural sound emerged. His hand flew to his raw neck. A single tear traced a path through the grime on his cheek. He looked at Masada, then lowered his head in a weak nod.

Masada released him. Tadashi slumped back, gasping, eyes squeezed shut as he fought to muster his strength. Masada sat, watching him, his own mind racing through bleak options.

He had three potential paths to pay the clan’s debt, each fraught with risk.

First, using leverage from another clan’s assets. The Fuma was the biggest ninja clan in the Tsuki kingdom. They were powerful and respected. The King trusted them most. Masada could propose a marriage alliance. He could offer Tadashi to Saki, heiress of the Fuma clan.

But her father, Hattori Fuma, was no fool. He would see the desperation behind the proposal instantly. The Fuma would demand control, seeking to absorb the Masamune clan into their sphere of influence. The Masamune elders would object to it. And Masada would be trading one master for another.

The second option conflicted him most. To put it bluntly, he could sell his son to the kingdom. Officially, Tadashi would become crown property, a slave. His name and clan affiliation would be erased.

It was a clean, brutal solution. Tadashi had been trained for this during his punishment years; he knew the life. The King, sentimental, might accept this as just punishment for the broken mirror. But his pragmatic advisors would likely still demand additional concessions from the clan, making it a gamble. Masada refused to give up his most prized asset for little gain.

The third option was high-risk, high-reward covert missions. Masada could deploy Tadashi on top-secret, deniable operations against any target. The potential financial payoff was immense. With Tadashi’s official status listed as “Killed in Action,” all ties to the clan were severed. The King would never know if they were dealing with a rival kingdom. It offered the highest potential return. He already had a specific mission in mind, one that required precise timing.

Currently, the third option seemed the most viable. But it required a functional agent, not the broken boy shivering on the cave floor.

As if sensing his father’s scrutiny, Tadashi looked up. Their eyes met. Masada’s disappointment was a palpable force in the damp air.

Your failures compound every problem, son.

He didn’t speak the words, but Tadashi flinched as if struck by them. The boy was perceptive; he always had been. His face paled further, and he looked away, shame etched into his features. With visible effort, he began trying to push himself upright.

Masada turned his gaze back to the cave entrance, to the relentless punishment of the storm. He took a long, controlled breath, mastering his frustration.

The rain continued its assault, a constant, drumming reminder of time slipping away.

S hirasu finally understood why heartbreak songs always topped the charts.

A lone musician strummed a melancholy tune on the tavern’s small stage. Shirasu stared into his umpteenth glass of sake, the sharp aroma mingling with woodsmoke from the fireplace. Tucked away in a dim corner, his fingers tightened around the ceramic rim. His mind was a storm.

Masada.

The name was a persistent ache. That bastard was ruining his life all over again. Shirasu raked a hand through his hair, gripping the strands until his scalp stung.

He’d lost all respect for his father years ago. Masada had abandoned him and his mother when they needed him most. Shirasu could have accepted it if his father had died a hero. But he hadn’t.

The coward had run, fully aware he was leaving his wife and son to suffer. That single fact convinced Shirasu that every good memory was a lie. The smiles, the attention—all a carefully constructed mask, a persona adopted to infiltrate the Mitsurugi clan. Masada had never cared about him or Chiyo; they were just tools. The moment his real clan called him home, he vanished without a backward glance.

The betrayal still stung.

Shirasu growled, threw back the sake, and leaned heavily against his chair, arms crossed.

He’d imagined their reunion a thousand times, concocting elaborate scenarios for tearing his father apart. But he’d never anticipated this .

The one thing that could send you soaring, only to slam you into the deepest abyss.

Hope.

The fragile hope that Masada might show a flicker of regret, that he would at least acknowledge the suffering he’d caused. Shirasu would never forgive him, but an admission of guilt would have been… something. Instead, it proved to be a foolish, hollow wish.

Masada didn’t mince words. He hadn’t cared whether Shirasu lived or died. So Shirasu severed their ties. The bastard was no longer his father.

And then he had the nerve to demand Tadashi to obey him and return to the clan.

Shirasu drained his cup, closing his eyes against the fire in his head.

Tadashi…

Shirasu had always been a loner, never expecting a brother. It took him three years to accept Tadashi. He’d been grasping at straws when he asked for help against Ikaru, but Tadashi did it. For reasons Shirasu couldn’t grasp, Tadashi forgave his past cruelty and accepted him completely. He cared for Shirasu’s well-being, treating him with genuine respect and affection.

Tadashi trusted him.

No one had ever risked their life for Shirasu like that. There were no words for what Tadashi had suffered under Ikaru. He protected his family—Masada, his younger siblings, Shirasu—with everything he had. Shirasu carried the memory of every wound, every struggle, every silent tear Tadashi had shed for his sake. It was the first time he understood the true meaning of sacrifice.

When they were finally free of Ikaru, Shirasu didn’t know how to repay him. He only knew he would do anything for his little brother.

Shirasu poured more sake, the liquid sloshing into his cup.

He didn’t know the details of Tadashi’s final confrontation with Ikaru, but it left Tadashi gravely wounded and in a coma. And now, after Shirasu had finally nursed him back to health, Masada immediately wanted to drag him back for some mission?

The sheer audacity! Tadashi could barely stand, let alone fight.

Shirasu had tried to stop him. He’d warned Tadashi that Masada was a manipulative bastard who only wanted to use him.

Tadashi should have seen it. Masada didn’t care about him. He’d always treated him like a servant. He was a monster who didn’t deserve to be called father.

Shirasu knew he’d hurt Tadashi in the past and hadn’t been a good brother, but at least he felt remorse. Unlike Masada, he wanted to atone, to repay his brother’s kindness, to build a new life together as a real family. He would protect him.

But Tadashi chose Masada instead.

Logically, Shirasu understood. Tadashi was bound by duty. He would never refuse a direct request from his clan leader, no matter the cost.

How long had Tadashi seen Masada as his entire world anyway? His whole life. And how long had Tadashi known Shirasu? A mere blink in comparison.

For ten years, Shirasu had been the source of Tadashi’s nightmares. The only reason Tadashi forgave him and now called him brother was to fulfill their mother’s dying wish.

Shirasu understood all this intellectually, but it didn’t stop the crushing weight. The anger, the disappointment, the sting of betrayal—all eclipsed by a burning hatred for Masada, who commanded such loyalty without deserving an ounce of it.

Shirasu gritted his teeth. His thoughts were a tangled mess, each one amplifiying the throbbing in his head.

He felt like he couldn’t breathe.

Had everything he did for Tadashi been for nothing?

He downed the sake in one searing gulp, slammed the cup on the table, and leaned back to stare blankly at the tavern ceiling, trying to banish the bitter memories.

The cuckoo clock chimed midnight. Shirasu still wasn’t drunk enough to face the empty inn room. Seishiro had given him two days to wallow, and he wasn’t going to interrupt yet.

Shirasu wanted to laugh and cry at the same time.

Dammit.

He hadn’t been this torn up over his breakup with Anko. So why did Tadashi leaving feel like a knife to the heart?

Why did he feel like such an asshole for the glare he’d shot Tadashi at the end?

Shirasu wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. Their final encounter replayed in his mind: Tadashi’s pale face, his choked voice when words almost failed him, the guilt swimming in his eyes.

Shirasu clicked his tongue.

Was he jealous of Tadashi’s bond with Masada?

Was some pathetic part of him still hoping for his father’s acknowledgment?

Did he heal Tadashi and want him to stay out of genuine care, or was it just selfish obsession? A way to fill the void in his own life?

What did he actually want?

Shirasu took a long breath and gripped his hair, as if he could physically untangle the thoughts.

No. He hadn’t nursed Tadashi back to health for gratitude or to chain him down. He did it because he genuinely loved his brother.

Instead of resenting Tadashi’s choice, shouldn’t he show that love by supporting him? He had to trust Tadashi, give him the space to follow his own path.

He had to let him go and believe that one day, Tadashi would return. Tadashi wasn’t his property. Shirasu didn’t need to control him or be jealous because he chose Masada.

Maybe what truly pissed him off was how sure Tadashi seemed of his purpose, while Shirasu felt completely adrift.

Lately, he felt like a door off its hinges. His life had revolved around serving Ikaru. After Ikaru’s fall, he spent six months solely focused on Tadashi’s recovery. Now, with Tadashi gone, he had no direction.

Maybe he was just desperately seeking something new to occupy the emptiness. He’d thought building a life with his brother was the answer. But Tadashi already had a life—one dedicated to serving Masada and the clan, however much Shirasu despised it.

He had no right to run his brother’s life. He needed to stop meddling and start dealing with his own.

Ah, hell.

Shirasu slammed his cup down and stood, grabbing his jacket. His head spun. His heart ached.

Seishiro was right.

He couldn’t keep drowning like this. He had to find solid ground.

Back at the inn, Seishiro was staring at his detective board—a chaotic collage of clippings, strings, scribbled notes, maps, and photos with faces crossed out or marked with question marks.

Shirasu closed the door and stood beside him, glancing at the latest puzzle. Outside of running his business, solving cases was Seishiro’s passion—a relic of a childhood dream, perhaps. Sometimes Shirasu envied him. Seishiro always had his own world to get lost in.

“Done feeling sorry for yourself?” Seishiro murmured without looking over.

Shirasu laughed humorlessly. “Shut it.”

Seishiro snorted. “You’ve still got one more day,” he reminded him.

One more day of self-pity.

Shit. How pathetic.

Shirasu grinned, biting his lip. “No need. I’m sick of all this family drama. So, what’s the case?”

Seishiro gave a brief rundown. Several of his men had gone missing. Stranger still, missing persons reports in the area had spiked recently. No connections between the victims. No pattern to the disappearances.

“You think it’s a serial killer?” Shirasu ventured.

Seishiro clicked his tongue. “With this many victims and not a single trace? Doubt it. He’d have to be burying them somewhere, right?”

He paused, eyes widening slightly. Then he traced a line across the scribbled map. An idea had struck.

Shirasu just shook his head and turned away, collapsing onto his bed. Seishiro would wake him if he found a solid lead.

For now, Shirasu would follow him like a lost chick. At least until he could breathe without this constant ache in his chest. Anything to forget about his brother and his father.

Tadashi and Masada could take a nice stroll and have a heart-to-heart for all he cared. They weren’t his problem anymore. He’d disowned Masada. He was no longer part of that family.

Whatever. To hell with them.

He wasn’t the star of this tragic farce. Tonight, he just wanted the mercy of a dreamless sleep.
