The Real You

← Back to Masamune Clan Series [EN]

M asada stood before the ramshackle house hidden at the edge of the forest. The place was a perfect ambush spot—a little too convenient.

“This must be the new enemy’s base,” the standing Tadashi announced, emerging from a brief inspection. “It’s empty, but there are some books inside. I think you should take a look, Father. I’ll check the perimeter.”

Masada nodded, but his feet remained rooted. Finding crucial intelligence in an abandoned house by pure chance? It was too convenient. What sort of trap was this?

With a sigh, he entered, sword gripped tightly in his hand. A dusty table held a stack of books written in a foreign script. A logical lead, but a nagging unease coiled in his gut. He’d been ignoring it for too long.

Something was wrong with Tadashi.

His son was naturally reserved and painfully shy, rarely showing his true feelings. Masada, too, was a man of few words; solitude had been his refuge. Only Chiyo had ever drawn him out. After her death and his ascension to clan leader, he’d learned to measure every word and action, becoming a figure of calculated control.

He’d tried to be more open with his children. Raito chatted easily, and Shiro never hesitated to express her feelings or embrace him. But Tadashi remained an island of formality. Their interactions were always transactional—clan leader to heir—a dynamic Masada himself had created. Tadashi responded with unwavering obedience and politeness, the perfect subordinate.

Since emerging from his son’s subconscious, Masada had been trying to see him differently. He was only now realizing how much he’d missed—the way a simple smile or a pat on the shoulder seemed to be a monumental event for the boy. He hoped Tadashi would begin to open up.

But the Tadashi outside felt… different.

CRASH!

The sound of a struggle shattered his thoughts. He rushed outside and froze.

There were two of them.

One Tadashi was binding the hands and feet of the other, who lay sprawled and unconscious on the ground. The captured boy’s face was a mess of bruises and blood, his skin deathly pale, his body spasmed even in unconsciousness.

“What is the meaning of this?” Masada demanded, his voice dangerously low.

The standing Tadashi turned, wiping sweat from his brow with a satisfied smirk. “He’s the enemy, Father. He copied my face! Some kind of dark magic. We need to turn him over to the King for interrogation immediately.”

Masada’s mind raced. Two identical figures. One was beaten and bound; the other, though injured, moved with unsettling ease. One was his son; the other, an imposter. Which was which?

He glanced at the captive. Unconscious, he couldn’t be identified. He’d have to be interrogated first. Masada looked back at the standing boy. If this was the enemy, he had to play along to discover their plan.

“Very well,” Masada said calmly. “We’ll take him to the clan first. Our security head can question him. We need to know the enemy’s plan before we involve the King.”

Tadashi nodded. “I saw a cart in the shed. I’ll get it.”

As he walked away, Masada knelt beside the captive. He tilted the boy’s chin, examining his face. No mask, no makeup. Just sweat, dirt, and blood. When Masada touched his arm, the boy shuddered and recoiled, a choked groan escaping him. His eyes fluttered open, vacant and dazed. Tears streamed down his cheeks, his face contorted in silent agony.

The muffled whimpers sent a sharp pang through Masada’s chest. The boy’s face, so like Chiyo’s, was a portrait of suffering. Masada looked away, using a handkerchief to gently wipe the blood from his nose.

The boy gasped, his gaze suddenly sharpening as it focused on Masada. Recognition flashed in his eyes. He scanned Masada frantically, checking if he was unharmed, then looked around in panic.

“It’s still usable, Father,” Tadashi called, pushing a cart toward them.

The bound boy began to struggle wildly, making desperate, muffled sounds, his eyes pleading with Masada, filled with raw fear and urgency.

Masada’s heart hammered.

Tadashi arrived and kicked the thrashing boy in the ribs. “Quiet!”

The boy fell silent, curling into a trembling ball. The imposter threw him into the cart and covered him with a tarp. “Come on, Father, it’ll be dark soon.”

Masada stood, his fists clenched so tightly his knuckles were white. He was certain now. The captive was his son. The real Tadashi abhorred unnecessary violence.

He had to wait. If the imposter escaped, its plan would remain a mystery. Why take Tadashi’s face? He had to know the goal.

He followed the cart in silence, his eyes fixed on the false Tadashi’s back. The sky darkened. As they neared a wheat field, a figure stumbled into view, clutching a bloody arm.

“Tadashi!” the man called out, relief evident in his voice. Masada recognized him instantly.

Shirasu.

Shirasu’s eyes widened as he saw Masada, but before he could speak, he collapsed. Masada caught him. Thunder rumbled overhead as the first drops of rain fell. Masada lifted the tarp, placed Shirasu in the cart back-to-back with Tadashi, and covered them again. He pointed to a barn at the field’s edge.

“We’ll take shelter there,” he told the imposter. Together, they pushed the cart into the barn just as the downpour began.

The barn was spacious, with hay bales and grain sacks stacked against the walls. A small resting room was tucked in the far corner.

“Tadashi, move Shirasu into that room,” Masada instructed, retrieving a first-aid kit from a storage box.

The false Tadashi complied. Masada sent him for water while he examined Shirasu. The man’s face was bruised, his lip split, and his ribs and arm were broken. He blinked, half-conscious.

“Where’s Tadashi?” Shirasu rasped, wincing.

“He’s safe. Why do you ask?” Masada replied, glancing toward the main barn.

Before Shirasu could answer, the imposter returned with water. He helped Shirasu drink. Shirasu patted his chest, searching. “Masada, did you take my necklace?”

“What necklace?”

“I didn’t see it when I carried you in,” the false Tadashi said.

“Damn it! I had it when I ran into you. I have to find it,” Shirasu muttered, struggling to rise.

“Sit. I’ll look,” Masada said.

He went to the cart and pulled back the tarp. The real Tadashi had his eyes closed, feigning sleep. Masada spotted a shuriken hidden in his son’s left hand, the blade already sawing through the ropes on his wrists. A faint smile touched Masada’s lips. He let Tadashi work while he picked up Shirasu’s medallion from the cart bed.

Masada handed it to Shirasu, who had just finished his water. Shirasu put the necklace on and began speaking rapidly—about a creature called Dorimu, a dream world, and their need to escape. His breathing grew more ragged with each word.

“The point is, you have to find Dorimu, fast. I’ve scanned your bodies in the real world, and you only have until—”

Shirasu choked, coughing up blood and foam. He collapsed onto the table, staring wide-eyed at the false Tadashi. “Y-you—”

Masada’s eyes darted from Shirasu to the now-empty cup—the only thing the imposter had given him. Poison . No time to react. The imposter stabbed Shirasu in the gut and hurled him into the corner.

“Shirasu!” Masada lunged forward.

Shirasu gasped, clutching the medallion. A black light flared, enveloping him before he vanished. In his last moment, he gritted his teeth and jerked his head toward the imposter, his final message clear.

He is Dorimu. Kill him.

Masada attacked without hesitation. Dorimu smirked, blocking his sword. The creature’s face warped, transforming into a perfect mirror of Masada’s own. Masada faltered for a split second.

It was all the opening Dorimu needed. It struck him with brutal force.

Tadashi’s head throbbed, his mind a maelstrom of pain and fragmented memories. Ikaru. Shirasu. Dorimu. Masada.

Father!

He had to save him.

His hands were free. Panting, he sliced the ropes on his ankles, tore off the gag, and scrambled from the cart. He saw two Masadas locked in a vicious battle.

He was too late. Dorimu had taken his father’s form. Staggering forward, he faced the impossible choice. Which one was real?

One of them saw him. His opponent slashed, and he failed to dodge. A deep gash opened on his right arm. As blood poured forth, this Masada cried out in pain.

“Tadashi! He is Dorimu!” the wounded Masada shouted, blocking a flurry of attacks. His injured arm slowed him. “Help me! Attack him!”

Tadashi looked at the other Masada, who ignored him entirely, his face a mask of furious, single-minded focus. The killing intent was palpable. Tadashi gripped his sword and joined the assault on the silent one.

“Yes! Excellent, son!” the wounded one cheered. Tadashi fought beside him, and the other Masada—Dorimu—began to lose ground.

Tadashi pushed his exhausted body, coordinating his strikes. The wounded Masada countered a powerful blow and kicked Dorimu back. Dorimu lunged again, but Tadashi parried the attack. The impact sent a jolt of pain through his arm.

“Good, Tadashi! You’ve improved so much!”

Tadashi forced Dorimu back. For a split second, their swords locked, and their eyes met.

“Tadashi.”

The voice was quiet. This Masada’s gaze was piercing. He scanned Tadashi quickly, as if assuring himself his son was unharmed, before twisting away to evade another strike.

Tadashi faltered. A strange feeling washed over him.

“Son, keep going! Just a little more!” the wounded Masada shouted, parrying a heavy swing.

Tadashi stood still, his instincts screaming. Something was wrong.

Why was he obeying blindly? He glanced at the silent attacker, so intensely focused—just like his father.

A memory surfaced: his father in his own subconscious, burdened but striving to be better than Daze. His father, who had cared for him in his own quiet way. His father, a man of action, not words. His praise was rare, shown in a glance, a touch, a quiet acknowledgment of duty done.

“Come on, son,” the wounded Masada urged, his voice cracking with feigned desperation.

And then Tadashi knew.

The effusive praise, the overt encouragement in a life-or-death fight—that was the dream of a father he wished he had.

The silent, fierce warrior whose eyes held a flicker of deep, unspoken concern even in the chaos—that was his real father.

Tadashi raised his sword. He timed his move to coincide with Dorimu’s strike against his father. Masada was cornered, but in the final second, their eyes met again. Tadashi gave a small, knowing smile.

I understand, Father.

Tadashi spun, parrying Dorimu’s attack. The creature was stunned but reacted instantly, producing a second blade and plunging it into Tadashi’s stomach. Tadashi didn’t dodge. He pushed forward, slashing the hand that held the sword impaling him. Dorimu screamed, reflexively blocking the real Masada’s swing aimed at its neck. It never saw the second blade in Masada’s other hand plunge deep into its heart. With a smooth, brutal motion, Masada ripped its life away.

Dorimu roared as black smoke poured from its mouth. Its body collapsed, melting into a swirling black portal. Tadashi sagged, agony searing through his stomach. Masada caught him before he could fall, his arms wrapping tightly around him. In one fluid motion, he lifted Tadashi and leaped into the portal.

They plummeted through a vortex of pure darkness. Agony ripped through Tadashi from every direction, wrenching a scream from his throat. A distant glow below swelled into an exit, and they slammed into the barrier, violently jolting back into reality.

Instantly, the white, sticky strands of Dorimu’s cocoon coiled around him, crushing the air from his lungs. He thrashed, choking. Then, a pair of hands tore through the webbing and hauled him out.

Shirasu.

He pulled Tadashi into a fierce embrace, and a wave of healing energy surged through him. The pain ignited into a white-hot inferno, setting every nerve on fire. Tadashi convulsed, too exhausted to even scream. His heart hammered against his ribs, a frantic drum about to burst.

Then, the world went black.
