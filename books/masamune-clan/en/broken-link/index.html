Broken Link

Book 11 of Masamune Clan Series

A brother’s betrayal. A sister’s rage. A kingdom’s fate hanging by a thread.

To empath Shiro Masamune, her older brother Tadashi was a disgrace—his betrayal a wound that never healed. But when he vanishes during a brutal attack that leaves their clan in ruins, Shiro is left with haunting regrets… and a desperate need for answers.

The worst part of being an empath wasn’t the flood of other people’s pain—it was that it never drowned out my own.

My head throbbed as I scanned the crowded hall, fists clenched against the pressure spiraling through the room. The final mission briefing for the New Year Festival had begun, and I was at the center of it all, though not by choice.

Six months ago, this would’ve been Tadashi’s job. If he hadn’t failed. If Father hadn’t demoted him. If I hadn’t been chosen to replace him.

The clan—and the King—had placed their trust in me. No one said it aloud, but everyone knew: if anything went wrong, it would be my fault .

The Masamune would be working with the Fuma to guard the Royal Family. Every coordinator and team lead was here. The air was tight with fatigue and worry. The emotions hit my shields like a winter squall, slicing straight through. Everything all at once—too much, too fast. And this was only the beginning.

The King’s trust rested on us. One mistake, and our clan’s reputation could shatter.

“Any questions?” Elder Mei’s voice cut through the static. “Speak now if you have concerns.”

Uneasy glances. Silence. Just the ticking clock and a hundred shallow breaths.

Moments like this made me wish I didn’t have empathic abilities. Every tremor of anxiety landed like a punch to the gut. I sat frozen, trembling, as if I could detach from the noise—only to find it followed.

Across the room, Tadashi watched me. I looked away. The pounding sharpened behind my eyes.

When Elder Mei finally closed the meeting and the last chair scraped back, I exhaled so hard my ribs creaked.

Sadi caught me before I reached the door. He steered me into our shared office and nudged me into the only chair that didn’t feel like a trap. While I closed my eyes and tried to steady my breathing, he fetched water.

He pressed a glass into my hand. “Here. Drink.”

I drank.

“You were shooting daggers in there,” he said. “Another minute and half the team leads would’ve impaled themselves on principle.”

The joke cracked through my armor. The corner of my mouth twitched. I didn’t reply, just took another sip.

“There. You look cute when you smile, Shiro.”

I choked and nearly sprayed water in his face. He grinned and thumped my back.

“Flirting during a briefing, Sadi? No wonder your reports are shorter than your love life.”

He winked, dropped into his chair, and propped his boots on the desk. Six years older, twice as reckless, and—if rumor was to be believed—three times as charming. For six months he’d flirted with the same undented confidence he brought to a sword fight. I still hadn’t decided if it was endearing or exhausting. He’d never actually asked me out, though. Maybe he knew I’d say no—or maybe Tadashi warned him off.

A rap at the door landed like a thrown pebble. My shields flared; pain spiked behind my eyes a second before the guard announced the visitor. I winced and braced my defenses, already knowing who it was.

The guard opened the door. Tadashi stood behind him. His gaze flicked to Sadi for a heartbeat before locking onto mine.

“Can we talk, Shiro?”

Before I could answer, Sadi cut in. “Don’t loiter like a lost puppy. Come in. Sit.” He waved the guard away and shut the door. “Have fun, sweetie. Try not to draw blood.”

The latch clicked.

Silence settled. I crossed my arms and stared Tadashi down. Exhaustion clung to him—the same worn look from the briefing, the dark circles beneath his eyes. He knew . He’d seen how hard it was for me to hold my shield in a room like that.

“How are you feeling, Shiro?” he asked. A faint smile. His voice low, careful. He knew I hated being interrupted—especially at work.

Yet he did it anyway.

I let out an exasperated breath. Work with Tadashi was unavoidable. Anything beyond that, I refused.

“Skip the small talk. We have work,” I snapped. “What do you want?”

For a beat he just stared, lips parted. Maybe he hadn’t expected me to be so blunt. I glared, willing him to get on with it. My shields wavered and my skull throbbed as if someone had a drill to my temple. I clenched my jaw.

“If there’s nothing else—”

“Wait, Shiro.” He swallowed, then offered a small smile. “Would you let me join you and Father for dinner?”

I frowned. Of course not. “Father and I don’t have time for you—”

“I meant, with Raito too,” he said quickly, wincing.

“It doesn’t matter. You know how busy Father is—”

“I’ll prepare the tea ceremony,” he added, desperate now.

I went still. He’d invoked our most sacred tradition. Refusing a tea ceremony for a family member bound for battle was unthinkable. My glare burned. How dare he use obligation as leverage?

“Please.” His exhale trembled. “It’s been too long since we’ve all been together.”

My breath hitched. Why did he always make me feel like the villain? I turned away with a huff.

“I’ll ask Father,” I said curtly—careful not to agree. With any luck, Father would refuse.

“Anything else?” I snapped.

Tadashi hesitated, then reached into his pocket. “Shiro, I want to give you something,” he murmured. “Someone important gave this to me.”

He placed a necklace on the desk—a delicate silver chain with a white jade pendant set in an intricately carved iron frame. The stone shimmered faintly when his fingers touched it, then dimmed.

A power stone. Rare. Expensive. Capable of storing immense energy. Why was he handing this to me?

“This necklace means a lot to me,” he said. “If you’re willing, I’d like you to wear it during the mission.”

His wistful smile pulled me back years—to my birthday, when he’d had no money but strung leftover beads from a school project into a necklace.

Sorry, this is all I could give you, Shiro , he’d said back then, cheeks burning. I hope you like it anyway .

I didn’t move. The necklace lay between us, chain pooled like liquid moonlight. I kept silent, unwilling to explain why I wouldn’t take it.

We stared at each other. Hope drained from his eyes, leaving a hollow ache. I felt it like a bruise spreading under my skin. He bowed his head, blinking, and his trembling fingers closed around the necklace.

“Ah… sorry if it’s not your style,” he stammered, voice catching.

I bit my lip hard. You didn’t need to be an empath to see he was devastated. Pain, love, raw regret—his feelings cracked my shield and seeped into my chest. My breath hitched.

“Anything else?” My voice wobbled on the last word.

Ignore it. Those aren’t your feelings. Stay strong. You’ll be fine.

He shook his head, pocketed the necklace, and stood—sensing my discomfort.

“Raito and I will come by tomorrow evening… if Father permits. Sorry to bother you, Shiro,” he murmured, bowing low. “Have a good day.”

He slipped out and closed the door.

Sadi stormed back in, scowling. “What’s the rush? I was just about to go terrorize Kagawa’s desk.”

I ignored him, working to steady my breathing.

Sadi always protested when I refused to see Tadashi. He never understood how quickly Tadashi could shatter my balance—just like now. The line between my feelings and his blurred until I couldn’t tell the difference.

I couldn’t work like that. That’s why I kept my distance. Tadashi tried to show he cared with gifts, but I wouldn’t give him false hope. I threw everything away.

Was I selfish?

Tadashi had betrayed my trust. He’d forced me into this role.

Everyone assumed being heir came easily to me. They didn’t see how hard Tadashi had worked for years, how high he’d set the bar.

I’d only been at this for six months. Six grueling months of relentless pressure. Even now I was driving myself to the edge, trying to outperform him just to satisfy Father and the clan.

So no, I didn’t want his apology.

Was that wrong? Didn’t I have the right to be hurt? To be angry?

Maybe someday, when the wounds stopped bleeding, I’d feel differently. Maybe I’d be ready to forgive. But not today.

Not now.

Enough, Shiro. Don’t waste the day thinking about him. Focus. You’ve got work.

I drew a shaky breath, rubbed my face, and forced myself back to the task in front of me.

Unfortunately, Father granted Tadashi’s request. It had been a long time since we’d gathered as a family. After I became clan heir, Father and I moved from our home with Tadashi and Raito to the main hall complex for security—a small, guarded residence that served as both house and retreat.

Tadashi and Raito arrived after sunset. Raito flew into Father’s arms, chattering about everything at once. Tadashi followed at a respectful distance, bowing deeply and offering polite greetings. Father barely glanced at him, giving only a curt nod before turning fully to Raito.

Then Tadashi looked at me. He smiled—soft, tentative—and asked how I was. I answered with a stiff look and silence. His gaze dropped.

While Father and Raito talked, Tadashi quietly helped me set the table on the back porch. He wiped the surface, arranged the tableware, and pitched in with the kitchen work without a word. I avoided his eyes. When the food was ready, we sat down together.

Father praised my cooking. Raito chattered about school and asked what I’d been up to. Father mentioned work and outlined tomorrow’s mission.

Through it all, Tadashi stayed silent. He didn’t try to draw attention or force conversation. He listened with his head bowed, eating slowly, refilling tea, nudging dishes toward Father. A quiet presence allowed to watch and serve but not to speak. I should’ve felt vindicated that the dinner he’d requested held no joy for him.

But I didn’t.

Every so often, his eyes flicked toward me—a silent check. Are you okay?

I pretended not to notice.

After dinner, Tadashi prepared the tea ceremony with meticulous care. We drank in solemn silence. Father led us in prayer, spoke a few words of guidance, and retired. Raito bolted the moment he was dismissed. Tadashi stayed to help me clean. He moved through the kitchen as if unseen. When the last dish was dried and put away, he finally spoke.

“Thank you for the meal, Shiro. It was delicious.”

To most people, they were polite words drilled into us since childhood. But Tadashi always meant them. I felt the sincerity—warm and heavy as sun-warmed stone—tightening my throat. I gave a slight nod.

Silence stretched. I glanced away, bracing for another apology—ready to be the villain again. But Tadashi kept quiet.

When I finally looked back, he was watching me with a quiet intensity. His eyes were full of tenderness, and beneath it, something deeper—sadness, longing, and a feeling I couldn’t name. We stared at each other until the weight of it pressed against my defenses, sending a dull ache through my skull.

“You look exhausted, Shiro. Get some rest tonight, okay?” His voice was rough. He managed a reassuring smile. “Don’t worry about tomorrow’s mission. I’ve got your back.”

For a moment, he seemed about to hug me—but he hesitated, maybe afraid of my reaction. With a quiet goodbye and a bow, he left.

I didn’t say goodbye. I walked him to the gate. I stood there, watching his figure shrink into the night, a prickling unease crawling over my shoulders. I bit my lip, shook my head, and turned back toward the house.

T he New Year Festival began at nine, but Tadashi and I were up before dawn, already stationed in the palace hall. Tadashi stood vigilant at the entrance to Princess Kaguya’s residence, eyes sharp and unblinking. I worked beside Sadi, reviewing patrol reports from every clan agent on duty.

Security for the event was under the Royal Guard’s direct command. Armed soldiers lined the palace approach. As the hour drew near, four Fuma Clan ninja appeared, forming the sovereigns’ personal escort and taking up positions around the King and Queen.

I signaled our clan to escort the princess and prince. At the dais, Sadi took his place beside Prince Yoru, with Anko standing watch behind him. Tadashi secured the front of the stage where the royals would sit. I positioned myself between Princess Kaguya and Prince Yoru.

The ceremony began. Four royal attendants carried in a small platform draped with banners bearing the kingdom’s crest. Upon it sat several ceramic urns brimming with rice and fruit, and at the center stood a massive golden mirror.

The mirror, Mirai, embodied wisdom in our kingdom. The King and Queen rose to perform the sacred rite: he would wash Mirai with holy water, and she would reverently dry it. After the High Priest’s prayer, the mirror was meant to glow, blessing the realm. The crowd watched in hushed awe.

Just as all eyes fixed on Mirai, a bolt of black lightning tore down from the temple roof and slammed into the mirror before the king could lift the ladle. Dark strikes rained down, hammering Mirai again and again. The mirror shuddered, let out a shrill whine that pierced my skull, then pulsed with an eerie black glow.

Chaos erupted. Civilians clutched their heads and collapsed. Guards and clan members with enough power resisted fainting but froze where they stood. Only a few—those with strong spiritual or mental fortitude, myself included—could withstand the assault.

Princess Kaguya slumped in her chair. I caught her before she fell. Prince Yoru listed sideways, barely kept upright as Sadi stood frozen at his side.

My heart pounded as I scanned the scene. Who would dare unleash a mass hypnosis attack?

Mind control was notoriously difficult. The more targets, the more power required. This attack used both sight and sound—twice the demand. Only someone steeped in dark magic could pull this off.

It had to be the Mitsurugi clan.

“Tadashi!” I sent the call like a mental snap.

He flinched—and in the next instant, protective shields burst to life around me, Sadi, and Anko, dulling the mirror’s piercing hum. Its hold on us loosened. Sadi lurched back to awareness just in time to catch Prince Yoru.

Thunder cracked overhead. Jagged streams of black lightning ripped across the sky. The clouds split apart from horizon to horizon, revealing a thick, oppressive darkness behind them. Strange, flying octopus-like creatures materialized—black, tentacled things that hovered as if hemming us in from above.

A black boomerang slammed into Tadashi’s shield with a sharp crack, shattering it. Anko’s golden Sun Shield flared to life in its place. Above us, the flying creatures dove, snatching hypnotized Royal Guards and clan members alike. Their lashing tentacles crackled with deadly electricity, striking everywhere at once. The battlefield dissolved into chaos.

“Shiro, get out of here!” Anko shouted.

I hoisted Princess Kaguya, ready to move, when a black arrow streaked from the temple roof—piercing Anko’s shield and shattering it. I instinctively funneled mental energy to Tadashi, Sadi, and Anko, bolstering them against the hypnosis.

A tall man with pale blond hair and a long white coat appeared in front of us.

“Where are you off to, Anko? What’s the rush?” His lips curled in a smirk.

Anko recoiled. “Shirasu!”

Her twin bladed fans snapped open and whirled toward him. He laughed, vanished, and reappeared behind her, nunchaku blurring.

I stared, paralyzed. This was Shirasu Mitsurugi?

A brutal memory slammed into me. Ten years ago, Tadashi and I were cornered in the city forest, lured there by him—disguised as a school nurse. We barely escaped.

But Mother hadn’t.

Shirasu killed her.

I nearly let go of Princess Kaguya, ready to launch myself at him, when Sadi’s shout struck me like ice.

“Shiro, guard the princess!”

A deafening blast tore through the air, shattering glass. Sadi and I whirled, stunned.

The golden mirror lay toppled on the stage, its surface blackened. Dark energy spiraled up from its shattered face and dissipated. Jagged shards clung to the broken frame; glittering fragments littered the abandoned platform.

Tadashi had destroyed Mirai.

Oh, gods . The mirror was a sacred royal artifact, thousands of years old. Only the High Priest and the Royal Family were allowed to touch it. My head throbbed with the consequences our clan would face.

“Tadashi! What have you done—?”

A black boomerang spun toward Princess Kaguya and Prince Yoru. I yanked them down, flattening them as someone lunged at Sadi from behind. Metal screeched—Sadi’s short sword deflected iron claws aimed at his throat. They grappled, each seeking a killing blow. I recognized the attacker: Seishiro Mitsurugi, the man who led the ring that trafficked Tadashi and me years ago.

A roar split the air. A hulking beast charged me. Instinct took over. I swung my kusarigama in a desperate arc. The wolf-creature barely flinched, then raked its claws across my arm. I raised my weapon to parry—too slow. The brute force sent me sprawling. Fire burned along my wounded arm.

Gasping, I met its eyes—this was the same breed Sadi and Tadashi fought on their joint mission months ago.

A wolf-like monster towered over me, feral yellow eyes gleaming beneath a snarl of bared fangs. It wasn’t alone. Three more circled with it. Two others toyed with the unconscious Princess Kaguya and Prince Yoru, batting them like prey.

My heart lurched. The Princess and Prince —

I lashed out with the chain, slicing clean through one creature’s leg. The Prince and Princess lay nearby, unconscious, their bodies crisscrossed with claw marks. The wolves lunged at me in unison.

I dodged, drew my twin short swords, and recalled Sadi’s report: strike the heart to kill them. Razor claws grazed my throat—until someone kicked the monster in front of me and drove a blade through its heart.

Tadashi.

He ripped his sword free and cut down the remaining wolves, then finished the maimed beast near the princess.

I scrambled to the Princess, checking for breath and pulse. Both she and the Prince were injured but alive—unconscious and in need of care. Tadashi dropped beside me and pressed his palm to the ground.

“Star Shield,” he whispered.

Shimmering blue light rose around me, the princess, and the prince, forming three protective columns. Kneeling close, I heard Tadashi’s labored breathing, the faint whimpers of pain he tried to swallow.

The Star Shield drained its caster mercilessly—tendrils of fire raking through your veins, searing every nerve, burning you alive from within. Few could endure it. I could barely hold one for a minute. Tadashi was bearing three at once.

“Shiro, you need to go— now ,” he gasped, staggering to his feet, his face ghost-pale. He swiped blood from his temple, eyes scanning the battlefield.

With Mirai destroyed, the hypnosis had broken. Guards and clan members, free of its grip, turned their weapons on the dark invaders. I spotted Father and the Fuma clan leader standing shoulder to shoulder, shielding the King and Queen.

A piercing shriek spun me around. One of the flying creatures had coiled its tentacles around Tadashi’s neck and was electrocuting him. I hacked the grotesque limb off. The creature shrieked and vanished in a flash. Tadashi collapsed, choking—but before we could recover, wolves and octopuses swarmed again. Gritting his teeth, Tadashi lifted his sword.

“Get the Princess and Prince to the evacuation point—the Fuma elders reinforced the shield there. I’ll cover you,” he rasped.

I nodded and hauled Princess Kaguya and Prince Yoru over my shoulders. Their weight made me stumble, but I steadied myself with a surge of power. My head pounded and my body shook—the wolf’s venom was already burning through me, fever racing in my veins.

I had to reach shelter before it took me down.

I pushed forward, agonizingly slow. Wave after wave of dark creatures struck, but their attacks skittered off or disintegrated against the Star Shields.

Tadashi fought to maintain the shields while fending off the wolves hounding me. The blue columns flickered under the relentless assault.

I quickened my pace, spotting Fuma clan members up ahead. Before I could reach them, a figure materialized in my path.

Shirasu .

Two flying octopuses at his side dove for Tadashi. Shirasu’s gaze stayed on me, studying the Star Shield around me. He knew he couldn’t touch me as long as it held. He wiped blood from the corner of his mouth and smiled, calm and cold.

“Say goodbye to your brother, Shiro.”

A sword flashed into his hand. He lunged at Tadashi with blinding speed, blades shrieking as they met. I didn’t stop to watch. My duty was to get the Princess and Prince to safety.

Not much farther.

Dark energy pounded the shield.

It trembled but held.

Almost there.

A wolf-beast hurled itself through the barrier. The shield flared, incinerating it instantly. The impact rattled the shield and sent me tumbling. I kept my grip on the Princess and Prince. A guard rushed in, finishing the charred beast. I dragged myself up.

Come on…

Fifty meters from the evacuation point, I shouted for help. Nearby clan members turned, eyes widening when they saw the Prince and Princess. They sprinted toward me. As they reached us, Tadashi’s Star Shield shuddered violently—

—and went out.

Cold washed through me. I spun back.

Tadashi stood on higher ground, clearly visible despite the distance.

Shirasu’s creatures had wrapped their tentacles around Tadashi’s arms, legs, and neck, stretching him taut. Shirasu stabbed him again and again, relentless even after Tadashi sagged, blood pouring from his abdomen. Then Shirasu’s head snapped toward me. He took in the scene in an instant—I was safe, beyond his reach.

He signaled. The octopus-creatures released Tadashi. Shirasu kicked him once, then grabbed his hair and dragged him away like a trophy. He threw me a mocking smirk and vanished.

I charged uphill, fury singing in my blood.

Too late . Only a pool of blood remained, and Tadashi’s sword lay beneath a tree.

With shaking hands, I lifted the blade. It was slick and sticky, every inch smeared with blood.

My stomach twisted.

There are only two ways a Star Shield ends. The caster lowers it willingly—Tadashi never would. He’d protect me with his last breath.

Or it fades on its own. A Star Shield persists as long as its caster lives, a drain even in sleep.

It fades only with death.

I stood, trembling, my brother’s sword clutched tight.

“Don’t worry, Shiro. I’ve got you…”

His vow echoed in my mind, sharp as the blade in my hand.

Something cracked inside me as Tadashi’s blood ran hot down my arm.

He kept his promise—

—to the very end.

The chaos of that day ended only when Shirasu fled. The flying octopus-creatures he’d summoned winked out one by one, vanishing as mysteriously as they’d appeared. At last, the rift in the sky sealed shut.

Sadi told me Seishiro had driven the wolf-beasts into the valley behind the temple before slipping through a portal into the world of darkness. After a brief search, Anko found a small altar on the temple roof—the enemy’s staging point for the dark ritual that struck the Mirai.

Together with the Fuma clan, we cleared the grounds and tended to the wounded, turning cloisters into makeshift infirmaries and laying the dead to rest. The Kingdom declared three days of mourning. Heavy rain fell on the fourth, yet the field before the royal temple still held the iron tang of blood.

On the fifth day, the King summoned the Masamune clan to the royal audience hall. Father, several elders, Sadi, Anko, and I answered the call.

By then, I was half-dead. The wolf-venom had burned through me for three endless days. Every inch of me ached; fever raged; pressure hammered at my skull until it felt ready to split.

My focus was in shards. My emotional defenses, already frayed, gave way beneath the flood of others’ feelings. Waves of raw emotion broke over me, scorching like hot coals against bare skin—inescapable, unbearable.

Without Sadi, I would have gone under. Holding to the steady cadence of his emotions helped me withstand the pain. He stayed close, sheltering me from crowds. In the worst moments, he let me crush his hand in mine—his touch, my anchor.

We walked side by side toward the audience hall. He squeezed my arm—a wordless promise. None of us had slept properly in days. For the first seventy-two hours, I hadn’t closed my eyes at all. After that, Sadi and I took turns keeping watch, scraping together perhaps two hours of sleep between us. My reserves were gone. Body and mind were running on fumes.

The royal audience hall bristled with the King’s elite guard. Silence fell as we entered. I knelt beside Father and shut my eyes for a heartbeat, gathering what little composure I had left. The Masamune leaders already knew what was coming.

Princess Kaguya and Prince Yoru had reached the evacuation point alive, but the wolves’ claws had cut deep. They hovered between life and death, only recently pulled back from the edge. The King’s fury burned white-hot.

We stood on the cusp of a slaughter.

“His Majesty, King Takeru Tsuki, approaches,” the chancellor proclaimed as the monarch entered.

At Father’s signal we bowed low. The royal family and senior officials followed suit. When all had assembled, we rose just enough to kneel with heads lowered.

Heat pressed down like a weight. I clenched my fists as a storm of emotion crashed over me—disappointment, anger, bloodlust, fear, contempt—each surge a blow to the ribs.

My chest tightened. Breath came shallow and ragged. Cold sweat soaked my back. I blinked hard, fighting to steady the world.

“I entrusted the Masamune with the safety of the Princess and the Prince. And what happened? They were nearly killed under your watch!” the King roared, his voice shaking the chamber. Each word struck my skull like a hammer.

“I gave the same duty to the Fuma. They protected the Queen and me flawlessly—shielded us and evacuated us without fail. So tell me—why did they fulfill their duty perfectly while you failed?”

No one spoke. No one looked up. When the sabotage hit, the High Priest—his spiritual power immense—had resisted the hypnosis and sealed the King and Queen in a ward. A Fuma adept in mental control rallied his kin and cut a path to safety.

“You also destroyed the Mirai! Do you have any idea how priceless that artifact was?” the King snarled.

I curled my fingers tight enough to draw blood. The breaking of the Mirai had been the turning point—without it, the hypnosis would have dragged on and more would have died.

Father bowed deeply. “Your Majesty, we beg forgiveness for this grievous failure. Command us how to make amends.”

The King pronounced three punishments. First, we were to pay reparations for the Mirai and for the festival’s cancellation. Second, we were ordered to find the Mitsurugi clan’s hideout and eradicate them. Third—and most cruel—Father would be imprisoned as clan head until the Mitsurugi leader was dead.

The hall seemed to grow hotter still. I drew a slow breath, bracing for a sentence we could barely endure. The fine alone could grind our clan to dust.

As for the Mitsurugi—orders or not, I was already resolved to hunt Shirasu and Seishiro. They had to be high-ranking—perhaps the leader’s right hands. Following them would lead me to the stronghold.

The audience ended. We bowed again, then watched as guards led Father to the palace prison. Elder Mei gathered us in the main hall for a private council, her spine rigid with unspent words. I trailed at the rear, feet dragging. Sadi caught my eye and squeezed my shoulder.

“You need to rest, Shiro. You’re pale,” he murmured, low enough that Elder Mei wouldn’t hear.

“I’m fine,” I lied.

He snorted softly but let it pass, falling into step and taking my hand. After that, it all blurred.

Elder Mei’s strategy slid through my mind like smoke. My thoughts scattered like leaves in wind. Every mention of Tadashi, of Shirasu, of the Mitsurugi lanced my chest. Images of my brother crowded in, tightening around my throat until guilt and regret stole my breath.

He had given his life for me. I remembered the silences I’d enforced, the hard words I’d spoken, the times I’d pushed him away and ignored his pain. However I treated him, he shielded me. He kept me safe.

“Shiro, you and Sadi must leave at once. Do you understand?” Elder Mei’s voice snapped me back.

I swallowed and stared at her, empty-eyed. I should have said yes. My mind went blank; my lips would not move. Elder Mei’s brows drew together. Before the scolding came, Sadi rose and cleared his throat.

“Forgive me, Elder Mei, but Shiro needs rest. Her brother just died. Her father’s imprisoned. She’s still fighting the wolf poison. Let me brief her on the plan. I’ll make sure she understands her task.”

Elder Mei exhaled slowly. “Very well. Rest and recover, Shiro. We will speak again soon.”

Sadi helped me up and guided me out. He draped his haori over my shoulders, warmth seeping into my skin. I pulled it tight, grateful for its shelter.

“Go straight home and sleep,” he said gently. “Don’t be stubborn. Pushing yourself helps no one. The elders need you, but you’re in no shape for a mission.” He hesitated. “Can you make it back alone?”

I bit my lip and nodded. As he returned to the council, I walked away on unsteady legs. I meant to go to the rest house in the main hall complex, but a sudden ache pulled me elsewhere.

Home .

The house I’d left after I was named clan heir. The house Tadashi had kept.

I drifted on autopilot and stopped only when I arrived. I stood before the gate. The front yard was clear of dry leaves; the small patch of grass neatly trimmed. The place was in order, not a sign of neglect. Tadashi had kept it perfect.

I pushed the gate and hurried up to the porch. The lack of the familiar tingle as I crossed the threshold did not surprise me, but it stung. Tadashi’s detector shield was down; the power stones set into the house’s corners cold and lifeless.

No one to charge them now.

I chewed my lip, turned the key, slipped inside, and slumped against the kitchen door.

“ Tadaima . I’m home, brother.”

Silence answered. Raito wasn’t here—likely sleeping at a friend’s.

I breathed in, already mourning the warmth that used to fill these rooms. Now, darkness pressed in. The kitchen and dining room stood empty. The floor gleamed, furniture dustless. Tadashi must have cleaned before the New Year mission. He hated leaving things untidy, even for a few days.

I climbed the stairs and paused on the landing. Three steps more and I opened my bedroom door. I’d expected months of dust, but the floor was as spotless as the dining room below. Tadashi, of course. Raito barely cleaned his own room.

Back in the hall, I hesitated at Tadashi’s door, then forced it open. Darkness swallowed me. I felt my way to the corner and lit the oil lamp.

I had forgotten how cramped it was. Four meters square, a room that had once been a storage closet. No windows, only thin slits of light through high vents—none now, with clouds blotting the night. The air was thick and stale. More cell than bedroom, but Tadashi never complained. He took this room after Mother died, when we moved here.

I lit the lamp on the table and bit my lip at the futon still laid out—the blanket folded neatly atop the pillow, as if waiting. My gaze drifted.

I opened the small wardrobe and my legs went weak. My heart hammered. I stood there a long time, staring at the neat stack of folded clothes.

I gripped the wardrobe handle and breathed in the scent that was purely him. Green tea, leather, cinnamon. The door creaked; I shut it quickly, afraid to lose the smell.

On the right side, his weapons hung in a tidy array—some leaning, others tucked among boxes of mission gear. I pulled down the top box and lifted the lid.

Oh.

I froze. Inside lay the silver necklace with the white pendant he meant to give me. Beside it, every trinket and gift I’d thrown away, and all the childhood keepsakes he’d guarded.

At the bottom were torn scraps of a letter. I knew them at a glance—his first letter to me after I became clan heir. I’d been so furious I’d ripped it and tossed it out. But every word was burned into me.

“Shiro, I never meant to betray you. Please forgive me. I love you, Shiro… I always have.”

He had fished it from the trash and kept it. Pain knifed through my chest; my eyes clamped shut. My head throbbed; my body swayed. I closed the box, carried it from the wardrobe, and slid down to the corner of the futon. Leaning against the wall, I dragged the blanket over me.

“Don’t worry about tomorrow’s mission, Shiro. I’ve got your back.”

I drew a shaky breath and burrowed into his blanket, letting the familiar scent wrap around me like a warm embrace.

A memory surfaced. My head had ached all day. I didn’t yet understand my empathy, much less control it. When the pain came, I always sought Tadashi—clinging to him, anchoring myself in the warmth of his affection.

He never refused me, even if I woke him at midnight or found him feverish. He didn’t know why I trembled in his arms, but he never let anything touch me.

He would stroke my back. “Shh… I’m here, Shiro. It’s going to be okay. You know that, right? I love you.”

I’d heard it too many times. I’d accepted it too many times and never answered. I assumed his love was a given—the duty of an older brother.

Did he know I loved him too?

I’m here, Tadashi. Forgive me for hurting you all this time. I do love you, brother. I always have. You knew that, didn’t you?

Silence braided with the dark, twisting into a longing that swallowed me whole. My chest cinched tight; my eyes burned; salt pricked my tongue; my throat ached with every swallow. His blanket still held his scent. Each breath filled with him. Each breath cut new wounds around all the questions I could no longer ask.

Tears slid soundlessly. My head swam; my chest ached. My feelings were a snarl.

Too late to say goodbye.

Tadashi would never come home again.
