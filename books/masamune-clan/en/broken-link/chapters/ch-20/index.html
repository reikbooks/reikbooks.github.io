Forgiveness

Epilogue B - Shirasu Mitsurugi

Damn.

Sloppy work, Shirasu. Amateur hour.

I snorted, wrenching the faucet handle before thrusting my hands under the stream. Blood swirled down the drain, but some had already dried—stubborn under my nails, clinging no matter how hard I scrubbed.

For the first time, hurting an innocent left me shaken. Tadashi’s face flashed in my mind—the way his expression twisted when I yanked his hair, how the blood seeped from his scalp, splitting his forehead before tracing the bridge of his nose.

It made me want to break him further.

There was satisfaction in peeling open his old wounds, watching him go pale, his eyes glassy. He was the reason Mother became the village’s scapegoat. He was why Ikaru took his rage out on me.

That was my second mistake—getting lost in revenge, savoring Tadashi’s pain like it could balance my own.

I shouldn’t have lost control.

He shut down, refusing to believe a word I said. My plan crumbled, forcing me to reveal too much, promise things I shouldn’t have.

But beneath the urge to hurt him, something else gnawed at me—the desire to help him.

I hated how blindly he trusted Father. I wanted to rip the scales from his eyes, make him see how the Masamune elders had used him, trampled him like dirt.

Pathetic, really. Because I was no better—using him just the same. Offering to save his clan when all I really wanted was for him to save me from Ikaru.

I dragged a hand down my face, water dripping from my chin.

First time a target left me torn between wanting to destroy and protect, drowning me in guilt instead of resolve.

Damn it.

He was driving me insane.

Tadashi’s decision to accept my offer didn’t surprise me. I’d made the terms so sweet, so advantageous, that refusal was impossible.

But his request to visit the grave and learn more about Mother? That caught me off guard.

Of course he’d want to know about his birth mother. Maybe I’d relied too much on her letter to explain everything, sparing me the trouble of answering his questions myself.

Huh. A miscalculation.

Still, I’d promised to take him on the pilgrimage—though it wasn’t without risk. I rarely visited Mother’s grave anymore, and showing up with a stranger would raise suspicions. I’d brought Seishiro once, but an unknown companion? Village gossip would reach Ikaru eventually.

Worse, someone might recognize him as an enemy of my clan. That’s why I insisted he disguise himself. Beyond the Fazto border, he’d become my slave.

Bad luck struck that morning. While resting at an inn near the border, we ran into the last person I wanted to see.

“Lord Seishiro,” the innkeeper greeted.

The coffee in my mouth sprayed out, hitting Tadashi square in the face.

He flinched, but realization dawned when I “accidentally” knocked over the cup, drenching his kimono before it shattered on the floor.

“Forgive me, Master—” he murmured, dropping to his knees in a deep bow.

“Useless! Can’t you do anything right?” I rose and drove my foot into his stomach. “Clean this mess up—now!”

Another kick for good measure, then I rushed to the drink counter. Seishiro arched a brow at my approach. I shrugged, snatching a cleaning rag.

“Someone’s order,” I said dryly, jerking my chin toward Tadashi. Seishiro knew I took side jobs—including delivering purchased slaves. I tossed the rag at Tadashi’s face.

“Shirasu,” Seishiro called, following close behind me.

Damn it. What now?

I turned and planted my hands on my hips, deliberately blocking his view of Tadashi.

I’d disguised him well—shoulder-length wavy extensions, false lashes, darkened brows, hollowed cheeks, a beauty mark at the corner of his lips. The low-cut kimono, the glimpse of the Fuun seal, his submissive posture—anyone would recognize him as a slave at a glance.

But Seishiro… had a gift. He could read people’s strengths and weaknesses just by watching them up close.

“Ikaru was asking about you,” Seishiro murmured, keeping his voice low. No one dared interrupt us, but eavesdropping was practically a sport here.

“I just reported yesterday,” I snapped, itching to kick something. “What does he want now?”

Seishiro lit a cigarette. “The usual.”

My stomach twisted. “Can’t he pick someone else for once?!”

Smoke curled from his grin. “They don’t last long enough.”

Damn it all!

“Tell him I’ll be back the day after tomorrow.”

“Make it this afternoon. Half the base has seen you already—what do you think Ikaru will say?”

He wouldn’t say anything. He’d just carve me up for ignoring his summons, then carve me up again like always.

Ikaru kept an army of dark creatures bound to him. The price? His humanity.

Now he had to feed that hunger: torture, sacrifice, repeat.

Most victims died too fast. He needed someone who could survive—and heal.

Guess who always got volunteered.

“I’ve got a job. Quick one—back by evening.”

Seishiro just shrugged and turned toward the bar.

A golden chance. I seized Tadashi by the hair and dragged him outside—only to find Seishiro’s horse tied right next to mine. Even though I’d switched mounts.

Ugh. Why was my luck always this rotten?

Seishiro caught up in seconds. He grabbed my arm and backed me into a corner.

“What’s your play with that kid?” he demanded when we were out of earshot. I twisted away, keeping myself between him and Tadashi.

“What’re you talking about?” I slapped his hand off.

“Taka. Why bring him here? Want me to drag him to Ikaru myself?” Taka —Tadashi’s alias.

For a heartbeat, I just gaped. “Of course not!” I finally hissed, scrambling for a lie. Caught red-handed smuggling an enemy into the clan.

Seishiro took a slow drag, eyes locked on me. My fingers crept toward the tanto hidden at my back. My pulse hammered at the thought of fighting him.

Seishiro had always been the one ally I trusted not to stab me in the back. We joked, drank, watched each other’s backs. Seven years my senior, he’d been almost like an older brother. But his loyalty to Ikaru was absolute. Right now, I had no idea if he’d help me or slit my throat.

“You’re getting sloppy, Shirasu,” he said at last, exhaling smoke. His gaze flicked toward the inn, where curious eyes lurked. “We need to talk.”

I shut my eyes briefly, relief flooding me. He wasn’t turning me in. For whatever reason, Seishiro was siding with me. Maybe I should just ask for his help outright—I had no backup if this went south.

“When’s your business done?” he asked, sharp eyes on Tadashi.

“Tomorrow night. Day after, latest.”

“Meet me at the usual place once Ikaru’s finished with you.”

His safe house—a remote village in Yami, tucked behind Mount Kazan. I snorted and nodded. Seishiro turned and strolled off like nothing happened.

I sucked in a breath, then yanked my horse’s reins. “Mount up,” I barked at Tadashi. “We’re leaving. Now.”

Fifteen minutes before reaching the village, I stopped in the nearest town at a well-known slave auction house—just another routine stop for anyone tracking my movements. There, I instructed Tadashi to slip away to the restroom and wash off his disguise before teleporting him behind some dense bushes.

“I’ll take you to the cemetery, but I have to head back to base right after,” I said. “You can’t be seen. I’ll hide you in the teleportation dimension—the space between this world and the realm of darkness. From now on, you’ll need to hold onto my Doboro if you want to stay invisible.”

Tadashi didn’t speak—just nodded once, eyes flicking toward the darkness ahead.

I summoned my Doboro and passed one of its tentacles to him. A sharp static shock jolted through him as the creature pulled him into the other dimension. Tadashi flinched but didn’t complain.

Watching him, it hit me—keeping him tethered to the Doboro meant constant electric shocks. Not lethal, but definitely painful.

I’d grown numb to the sting—but I wasn’t exactly normal when it came to pain.

What if he passed out and got caught? That would unravel everything.

I swallowed hard. “Can you handle it?”

Tadashi studied my face for a moment, then offered a faint, reassuring smile. “Don’t worry,” he murmured. “I’m fine.”

For a second, I froze, instinctively wanting to snap that I wasn’t worried about him. I didn’t care if he was fine—only about the mission. Right? That was all.

But I wasn’t about to sound like some kid denying they stole cookies—with crumbs still on their face.

Instead, I just scoffed and told him to come out.

My Doboro drifted behind us like a balloon on a string as we mounted our horses and rode on.

The cemetery on the edge of my mother’s village was eerily quiet. Fufuku was a remote place—few visitors ever came, let alone to its burial ground. Still, I wasn’t taking any chances. I led Tadashi to the cremation ashes repository marked Chiyo Mitsurugi before stopping.

“We’re here,” I murmured.

Tadashi didn’t respond. I resisted the urge to look like a fool craning my neck and talking to empty air, but eventually, I stole a glance. His glassy eyes were fixed on Mother’s nameplate. A trembling hand reached out, fingers brushing the engraved letters with slow reverence.

I bowed my head and offered a quick prayer. Tadashi did the same beside me. That afternoon, a gentle wind whispered through the cemetery trees. Sunlight spilled warmly over us, scattering the shadows that had clung to me for so long.

It was peaceful.

Mother, Tadashi is here with me.

We’re going to fight Ikaru. We’ll probably die soon, so… well. At least you get to see us one last time.

I drew a deep breath and finished my prayer.

“I’ll go ahead,” I whispered to Tadashi. “Tug my Doboro twice when you’re ready—it’ll take you to Mother’s house. Wait for me there.”

He nodded. I walked away quickly, leaving my little brother alone with our mother.

Exhausted, I felt like death warmed over.

I left Ikaru’s place the moment he was done. Nearly two in the morning, and I was on the verge of collapse. I didn’t have the strength to heal my remaining wounds, much less teleport. Trembling, I rode back to the village, every inch of me still aching.

Had Tadashi waited at the house all this time? I’d left him there for over half a day.

I reached the village at sunrise. After grabbing groceries at the market, I slipped in through the back door. Inside, I blinked—the place was spotless. In the dining room, Tadashi sat curled in the corner, clutching one of Doboro’s tentacles, head bowed in sleep.

“Tadashi,” I called.

He startled awake, drawing a shaky breath as he tried to stand. His eyes were puffy, his hair a mess.

“Welcome home,” he murmured. I summoned my Doboro. It let out a loud bellow, yanking its tentacle free from Tadashi before lumbering over to me. I stroked its hide, letting it zap me in protest. After a muttered apology, I released it.

I must not have been clear enough. Once inside, Tadashi didn’t need to keep holding Doboro—no one was watching. And Doboro hated waiting. It must’ve thrown a fit or two.

I carried the groceries to the kitchen and grabbed a knife and cutting board. Carrot and tofu soup sounded perfect—I was starving. Without prompting, Tadashi took the rice and rice cooker. When he rolled up his kimono sleeve to wash the rice, I noticed his wrist, still wrapped from the Doboro’s earlier treatment, looked red and raw like a burn.

Before long, breakfast was ready. As we ate together, Tadashi froze after tasting the soup.

“What?” I frowned.

His face flushed instantly. He blinked rapidly before shaking his head.

“Nothing,” he murmured, voice rough. “Thank you. It’s delicious.”

I snorted.

“Obviously. I’m an amazing cook,” I shot back without thinking—my filter apparently on vacation. “It’s a secret weapon for making girls fall head over heels.”

For a heartbeat, we just stared at each other. Then a small smile curled Tadashi’s lips. Whether from exhaustion or his smile being contagious, I found myself smiling back.

“You can’t cook?” I asked between bites of rice.

Tadashi shook his head. “Raito says it’s either too salty or completely bland.”

Somehow, we ended up chatting through breakfast. I’d never imagined feeling this at ease around my younger brother. After eating, I wiped my face and pressed my fingers to my tired eyes.

“I’m going to read Mother’s diary. You should rest first,” Tadashi said, gathering the dirty plates.

Too drowsy to argue, I trudged to the bedroom. Tadashi had already cleaned the floor, laid out the tatami and futon, and even prepared blankets and sleepwear. I set my weapon and belongings aside, changed, and collapsed onto the futon.

Ah… Pure bliss.

Half-conscious, I gazed toward the dining room—warm, full, and pain-free. If only every day could be like this… My eyes drifted to Tadashi’s distant figure, suddenly overlapping with memories of Mother washing dishes in the kitchen.

Soon, I’d be sending him to hell.

The thought jolted me awake. Was this really worth it? Was I certain about dragging Tadashi into my reckless plan? His chances of killing Ikaru were slim. Maybe cooperating with Father would be smarter.

No. Father would never approve of how I wanted to handle Ikaru. He might send an army, but one thing was certain—Ikaru would make sure I died on the front lines first.

My life or my brother’s?

The answer wasn’t as simple as I’d thought.

The sun cast no shadows when I woke. Tadashi sat hunched in the dining room, arms wrapped around his knees, staring blankly at the ceiling. Mother’s diary lay open on the table. When I approached, he turned and offered a gentle smile that punched straight through my gut.

For a heartbeat, I could’ve sworn I saw Mother smiling at me.

“I’ll make tea,” he said. I nodded silently.

After we drank, Tadashi fidgeted, words caught in his throat. I reached out.

“Your left hand.”

He hesitated before offering it, wincing when I gripped his burned wrist. My healing power flared beneath my fingertips. He rubbed the smooth skin and murmured thanks. I gave him a thin smile.

“Just say it,” I said, running fingers through my already tousled hair. “What do you want to ask?”

He studied me before dropping his gaze.

“What kind of person is Ikaru?” His voice cracked. His fingers twisted in his hakama. “What will… he do to me? What should I do?”

The question left me winded. What tortures would Ikaru devise? What reactions would satisfy him?

Damn it.

Did we really have to have this conversation now?

Bile rose in my throat. I scrubbed my face, wrecking my hair further. I didn’t want to answer, but Tadashi needed to understand for our plan to work.

“He’ll summon me when he wants to hurt you,” I said flatly. Best to just spit it out. “You might not see me. Or he might force you to watch. Depends on his mood. Either way, I’ll be there.”

I glared at my teacup.

Shit. I needed something stronger for this talk.

“I have to stop him before he gives you wounds I can’t heal. He wants you broken, not dead. I’ll patch you up, then he’ll start over. He’ll order me to hurt you. Or make you hurt me.”

I snorted and drained my cup.

“He enjoys breaking minds as much as bodies. Anything that causes pain—he’ll do it, say it. So far he’s kept Father’s identity from me, but with you? He’ll probably reveal everything. Tell you you’re my brother. Spill every ugly truth about Father, Mother, me—just to watch you squirm.”

The teacup rattled as I set it down.

“How does it feel torturing your own brother? That’s what gets him off. He’ll bring up Makoto too. How I killed her. Remind you how I tortured you before killing your mother.”

Tadashi’s expression tightened.

Gods. I pinched the bridge of my nose, fighting a wave of dizziness. Suddenly, I remembered the seal I’d used on Tadashi all those years ago—one of Ikaru’s torture seals, the same kind he’d often used on me. And now it hit me: I’d used it on my own little brother when he was only seven. Ugh. I really needed a drink.

I shook my head sharply, forcing the memories away.

“I didn’t torture Makoto back then. Your mother’s death was quick. But Ikaru will twist the truth if he thinks it’ll break you. He lies as easily as he tells the truth. He messes with your memories, your thoughts. Takes everything that brings you joy and replaces it with pain. He wants to see you despair. After a while, you won’t even know what’s real anymore.”

I paused, studying Tadashi. His face had gone pale, but he said nothing. The silence stretched between us.

I drew a slow breath.

“Tadashi… I don’t know what to say about Makoto. Back then, I didn’t know who she was—didn’t know who you really were. I was just following Ikaru’s orders. Even if I’d known, it wouldn’t have changed anything. His commands are absolute. He can force any of us to do anything.”

The words felt hollow. Inadequate. “You understand, don’t you?”

Tadashi kept his head bowed, silent for so long I couldn’t guess his thoughts.

Finally, he gave a small nod. “I understand,” he whispered, voice rough.

He turned toward the window. Instinctively, I reached out and caught his hand. “If only I hadn’t hurt you,” I murmured.

Tadashi looked up. His gaze held mine, steady, before his lips curved into a sad, quiet smile.

He didn’t speak—just squeezed my hand tightly in return.

I stared down at our clasped fingers, lost in thought.

No apology was spoken. None accepted.

Yet for the first time in years, warmth flickered in my chest.

In the empty room where Mother once held me on her lap—where sunlight now spilled across the table, illuminating the old diary—

Just the two of us.

With the person whose gentle smile reminded me of her, seventeen years after she left us both… I finally understood what forgiveness truly meant.
