Epilogue C - Masada Masamune

The moment Masada stepped back into the room, the sight of Tadashi—pale, pupils slow to focus, perched on the edge of the bed like a ghost barely clinging to the living—sent an unexpected pang through his chest.

Shirasu was nowhere to be seen. His absence was a relief Masada didn’t acknowledge.

“Where’s your brother?” Masada asked, lowering himself onto the chair beside the bed. His knees felt stiff, as if the day had aged him twice.

“He… let me talk to you,” Tadashi said, careful, testing each word as if his tongue still wasn’t sure of its weight. He tried a smile—small, hopeful, the kind that had once made him look so much like his mother. “Are you well, Father? How is the clan?”

The concern in Tadashi’s voice, thin and raspy, scraped against Masada’s raw nerves. How is the clan? As if he hadn’t been the spark that lit the pyre.

Masada studied his son. The sickly pallor, the sheen of cold sweat on his forehead, the faint tremor in his hands clutching the blanket. The fury and despair Masada had momentarily forgotten surged back, cold and choking. He measured his words, letting the silence thicken until Tadashi’s fragile smile finally crumbled.

“Tadashi,” he began, his voice dangerously low, cutting through the quiet. “Have you truly not grasped how much damage you caused by keeping Shirasu’s plan from me?” He let the accusation hang, watching it strike home.

Tadashi blinked and stared at him, blankness washed over his face. He hadn’t considered it. The sheer shortsightedness of it inflamed Masada.

The silence stretched, thick with everything unsaid. Masada could have screamed. Could have shattered the room with his fury. Instead, he let the quiet choke them both.

Then methodically, he laid out the devastation.

He described, in detail, the penalty the King had loosed upon them after the mirror shattered—contracts turned to ash, debts accelerated, allies forced to distance themselves to save their own. The clan decimated, resources bled dry, loyalists starving and dying, elders pushed beyond exhaustion. This wasn’t just crisis; it was slow, agonizing oblivion.

He said it all. He did not say, I thought I’d lost you .

“Months without improvement. Hope lost.” Masada’s voice was flat, stripped of mercy. “While you slept, our world collapsed. Can you even stomach what your decision cost, Tadashi?” He paused, letting the crippling weight of it sink into the sterile air. “The blame rests entirely on your shoulders.”

Tadashi blanched, the colour draining until he matched the bleached sheets. A visible tremor ran through him. He swallowed, throat working around words that refused to form. In the hush, the drip from the basin sounded like a metronome. Slowly, as if the movement caused physical agony, he bowed his head. “I’m sorry, Father,” he choked out, the apology rasping raw as if dragged through his chest.

Masada rubbed a thumb against the sharp bone of his knuckle. He dismissed the apology with a sharp shake of his head. “Spare me your why – why you trusted him over your own clan, why you gambled everything on lunacy. Especially if it was just because you felt… unloved .” He spat the word, seeing Tadashi flinch as if physically struck. “Would you endanger an entire clan over something so petty ?”

Tadashi fought for breath. “Father, that’s not—”

“Why didn’t you come to me? Were you that afraid of punishment?” Masada asked, each question laid like a blade on a table. “Did you truly believe Shirasu’s word outweighed mine? That I had nothing better to counter Ikaru with? Tell me!” His voice hardened, demanding an answer he knew only amplified the guilt.

Silence followed, taut enough to hum. Tadashi’s fingers pinched the blanket until the knuckles blanched. A tremor ran through his forearms. He blinked, once, twice, too fast, as if to keep the room in focus.

Masada forced himself to breathe. To steady.

“I am angry,” he said. “And I am disappointed. Because you are capable of more—I have seen it. You’re eighteen. I will not treat you like a child. You must think like an adult, act with the cold calculus of survival, not the tantrums of a neglected child. Sacrifice. Duty. Act like a Masamune. Your choices weigh lives, regardless of title. This disaster proves it. Look at what you’ve done . Do you understand?”

Tadashi swayed. Sweat beaded at his temples, his breaths shallow. For a sickening moment, Masada thought he might collapse.

“I… understand,” Tadashi said. His voice caught on the word, and he had to push it out a second time. “I understand, Father.” He met Masada’s eyes for a heartbeat—guilt there, and grief, and something small and shattered—then looked down. His fingers dug into the mattress, as if clinging to it could anchor him. “Please. How can I… make it right?”

Masada let the silence stretch, thick with Tadashi’s fear. A long stiffness moved through Tadashi’s shoulders, like a man bracing for a blow. “I won’t lash you,” he stated coldly. “Not broken as you are. Your condition… decide your own penance. But know this: You will reflect, and you will make amends. You helped cause this financial collapse. You will take responsibility for fixing it. The clan’s ruin? That debt is yours to settle.”

Tadashi’s eyes met his, hollow and depthless. The hope that had sparked moments before was extinguished, leaving only ash. He nodded, a jerky, defeated motion.

“Yes, Father.” He breathed shallowly, as if each inhale might shake something loose inside him. “I… I know you want it done quickly. Truly, I—” He bit down on the rest, shame flushing his cheeks. “I don’t… know how.”

“I have a plan.” Masada exhaled tautly. “I’ll need you to carry it out. We move as soon as we can.”

Another shaky nod. “What… what must I do?”

“We’ll discuss it later.” Masada stood, the movement abrupt. The harsh fluorescent light hollowed Tadashi’s features. Masada forced the words out, layered with tested ambivalence. “If you want to go home. If you’d rather stay with Shirasu, I won’t stop you. I’ll release you from the Masamune. You can join any clan that will have you. You walk away. Free.”

Free . The word clanged in the silence.

Tadashi’s breath left him in a rush. He went very still. His head snapped up, his lips parted as if the air had turned to winter. “Y-you… you would cast me out?” The whisper cracked, rough with disbelief and devastation.

“Isn’t it just?” Masada countered relentlessly, turning away slightly, unable to meet the raw agony in his son’s eyes. He kept his tone even; he did not let himself hear what the question did to Tadashi. “Every member of the clan serves. If you refuse, you are free to leave. I won’t stand in your way. But every choice has a price. Decide, and bear it—no matter how severe.”

Silence descended, profound and suffocating. For a while, there was only the lantern’s flame shifting in the glass. The tremor in Tadashi intensified until his whole body vibrated with it. Tadashi’s grip on the blanket loosened, then tightened again, as if he were testing the edge of a pain he wanted to claim. His hands shook. His eyes glistened. But when he finally spoke, the words were quiet and clean, stripped to the bone.

“I’ll go back. With you, Father.”

Masada looked down at the wreckage of his son. Something twisted deep inside him, a pang sharp enough to ignore. He reached out, patting the trembling shoulder, feeling the sharp bone beneath thin cotton. The muscle there flinched, then steadied beneath his palm. It would have been so easy—too easy—to let the touch soften into forgiveness. He kept it measured.

“Good,” he said. “Get ready. We leave after dinner.”

Tadashi nodded. He kept his gaze on the floor until Masada rose. Only then did Masada notice the small, unguarded thing in his son’s expression—the tired glow of a child surprised to be visited, to be chosen, to be wanted—flicker and gutter under the weight of guilt he had just piled upon it. Masada turned away before it could settle in him.

Behind him, Tadashi’s breath hitched—once, quickly smothered. He did not turn back. The clan could not afford it. Neither, he told himself, could he.

Of course, Shirasu railed against the decision.

“He can’t leave until the day after tomorrow at the earliest,” Shirasu said firmly. “He still needs treatment, medication, and proper meals.”

He handed Tadashi some medicine and told him to rest.

“But I’ve been asleep for months,” Tadashi protested, drawing a laugh from Seishiro, who leaned in the doorway. Shirasu shot him a sharp glare.

“Fine, don’t sleep. Can you even make it to the dining room?” Shirasu demanded, hands on hips.

Tadashi tried to stand but couldn’t manage it. Shirasu scooped him up, muttering curses about insane parents who enjoyed tormenting their own children.

Masada shook his head and fell into step beside Seishiro, trailing behind. “Does he always talk like that?”

Seishiro smirked. “No. Just to you.”

Shirasu made tofu soup and grilled fish for dinner. The dining room felt almost surreal. Masada had never imagined sharing a meal with Tadashi, Shirasu, and Seishiro. To his surprise, Shirasu was an exceptional cook—his food carried the same signature flavors as Chiyo’s.

“Just so we’re clear—if those damned elders of yours lay a finger on Tadashi for vanishing on that mission, returning late, or anything else, there’ll be hell to pay,” Shirasu warned as they finished eating. Seishiro poured sake into a small cup. Shirasu downed it in one gulp.

Masada glanced at Tadashi, who had stayed pale and silent the whole time. The boy shifted restlessly, looking ready to faint.

“Understood. I’ll tell the clan that Tadashi escaped Ikaru’s base through a hidden passage but collapsed before reaching the nearest village. A local healer took him in, but he fell into a coma. When he finally woke, the healer questioned him and contacted me at once to retrieve him.”

Masada stayed overnight. The next day, he supervised Tadashi’s therapy with Shirasu. When Tadashi pushed himself too hard, Shirasu lost his temper, kicked Masada out, and banned him from being near Tadashi.

By the following morning, Tadashi could sit and walk unaided, though he tired easily. Over breakfast, Shirasu fussed over him.

“Wash your face and change before you go—grab some clothes from my room. I packed a backpack with a blanket, warm clothes, a thermos, and medicine. There’s food too. If it’s heavy, make your old man carry it. And don’t ride nonstop—take plenty of breaks.” He shook his head. “Remember, you’re still weak. You’ll have to remind that bastard—he won’t care otherwise. That old fool is too dense to send you off in this state…”

Tadashi flushed. Masada could tell he was embarrassed—Shirasu was speaking that rudely in front of their father. “Shirasu…”

“Don’t you dare say my name again! I told you not to go, but you’re stubborn. Live with your choice. If anything happens, don’t come running to me. Beg him for help instead. If you get hurt again, let him take a turn caring for you. Ungrateful brat.”

Masada could see the instant regret on Shirasu’s face. Tadashi stared at him, stunned and pale, as if slapped.

The light in Tadashi’s eyes dimmed. He lowered his head, muttered thanks for the meal, and excused himself to prepare.

Shirasu cursed under his breath as Tadashi left the room. Seishiro offered him a cigarette, but Shirasu waved it away. Without another word, he stormed out, slamming the door behind him.

Seishiro poured tea for Masada. After a while, Masada glanced at the wall clock. What was taking Tadashi so long?

He went to check and found his son slumped against the wardrobe, fast asleep. Tadashi had changed, but his face was drawn with fatigue. It made sense now—he’d seemed weak and drowsy earlier.

Someone snorted behind him. Masada turned to find Shirasu glaring.

“Did you give him a sleeping pill?” Masada asked, irritation edging his voice. They needed to leave now.

“No. Didn’t I tell you he’s still weak? He can’t do anything yet. Honestly, I’m afraid he’ll faint and fall off the horse,” Shirasu said.

“He’ll ride with me. If he gets tired behind me, he can switch to the front.”

Shirasu cursed under his breath. “You’re really… ugh. Why are you so cruel, Masada? What did you say to him yesterday? He looks like he was handed a death sentence.”

Masada shook his head. “I told him the truth.”

Shirasu’s voice cut like a blade. “No. You twisted the truth to hurt him—to threaten him. You played with his mind, made him feel guilty and afraid. You bombarded him with orders without giving him a moment to rest. You attacked him physically, mentally, emotionally—just to force obedience. And this after he was ecstatic that you survived Ikaru’s trap and came all this way to see him.”

Shirasu shook his head. “You’re no better than Ikaru, Masada. You’re enslaving your own son. Damn you to hell—I hope you die screaming.”

Masada said nothing. Shirasu exhaled, then stepped toward Tadashi.

“Remember, I’ll come to your clan,” he said, pinning Masada with a sharp look. “The moment you mistreat him, I’ll take him and disappear to another kingdom.”

Masada only muttered in response.

Shirasu gently shook Tadashi awake. The boy’s eyes flew open, panic tightening his face.

Shirasu whispered until he calmed. After a brief check, Shirasu left the room without another word.

“Ready?” Masada asked.

Tadashi stared at him, pale. He nodded, shouldered his backpack, and followed Masada outside. With a flicker of energy, Shirasu teleported them to where Masada’s horse stood tethered. Seishiro was already waiting, vigilant.

When he said goodbye to Shirasu, Tadashi looked like a condemned man walking to the gallows. He bowed deeply, then said in a choked voice, “Thank you for everything, brother. I’m sorry for always being a burden. I hope… one day I can repay your kindness.”

Shirasu stared at him, face rigid, lips pressed into a thin line. He motioned for Tadashi to go.

Masada gripped his son’s arm and urged him onto the horse. Tadashi settled behind him, clinging tight. With a brief nod to Seishiro and Shirasu, Masada tugged the reins. Without another word, they rode off.
