Epilogue A - Tadashi Masamune

Two days of begging finally wore the administrators down enough to grant me leave—though only because I’d gotten injured on my last mission. The clerk grumbled as he stamped my request, then tossed last week’s pay envelope onto the desk. It slid off and clattered to the floor. Pain spiked through my bruised ribs as I bent to pick it up.

“Be back Monday at five a.m. sharp. We’re short-staffed. Your schedule will be packed for the next three weeks to make up for this leave.”

I nodded my thanks. He waved me off.

When I got home, Raito still hadn’t returned. After a bath, I changed into a black funeral kimono and hakama, tucked my medicine pouch into my robe, and reached for my tanto—

“No need for weapons.”

I spun, the blade tight in my grip.

Shirasu was leaning against the wall behind me, arms crossed. His black-dyed hair was styled differently, and his glasses completed the look. Dressed like me in black, carrying no visible weapons, he could’ve passed for a harmless kindergarten teacher.

“Don’t worry—you’re safe with me on this journey.”

With a reluctant sigh, I returned the tanto to the top shelf of the wardrobe. The motion sent a stab of pain through my ribs, forcing a sharp inhale. I steadied my breathing and closed the door.

“What’s wrong?” Shirasu asked, studying me. Heat rose in my cheeks.

“It’s nothing. I just bruised my ribs yesterday—”

Shirasu seized my arm. I stumbled as he yanked my belt loose and tore open my kimono. He froze when he saw the dark bruises.

“I didn’t get the chance to ask Raito for ointment and bandages,” I muttered.

Shirasu shook his head and pressed his palm to my ribs. “It’s a long trip. I don’t want you passing out and falling off your horse.”

He summoned his healing power without warning. A sharp pain buckled my knees, but his grip kept me upright. I clenched my eyes shut and dug my fingers into his arm until the pain ebbed.

“Thank you,” I said, my voice tight, and let go. He studied me for a moment, then nodded. From his pocket, he produced a small pouch of henna and indigo for hair dye.

“I’ll be waiting in the dining room,” he said. “Don’t dawdle. If your brother comes back before you finish, I’m leaving—and you’ll lose your chance to visit Mother’s grave.”

I nodded. When I finished dyeing, Shirasu motioned for me to sit and handed me the tea he’d prepared. As I drank, he outlined our route and my cover story. Afterward, I wrote a note for Raito and left my pay envelope on the table. I took one bill—just enough for the road.

It wasn’t much, but it would have to do.

“Does your brother know you spend half your paycheck on his allowance and the other half on household expenses?” Shirasu narrowed his eyes. “Stop spoiling him—he’ll never learn to stand on his own. Let him earn his keep. And for household costs, bring it up with Masada. It’s still his house.”

“Mind your own business,” I snapped, face hot.

What would he think if he knew Father had already kicked me out? I only had a few months before I’d be forced to leave. Father cut off my allowance five years ago, when I was under clan punishment. After he and Shiro moved to the main hall, he left no money for Raito—or even daily meals—so I covered everything.

Since my demotion, my mission pay barely covered necessities. I skipped meals to save what little I had and took side jobs just to scrape by.

What would Raito do without me? Could he manage? Surely Father would help him. He wouldn’t let Raito starve.

Would Raito even miss me?

The thought clenched my chest. I pushed it away.

I finished washing the cups and stacked them neatly. Shirasu appeared beside me, his presence quiet and steady. When I wrung the last drop of water from my hands, he brushed my arm. I froze, caught by the intensity of his gaze.

“I’ll take you home—to Mother’s village,” he said, voice steady. “Beyond the Fazto Border, we enter Mitsurugi territory and switch disguises. Stay close and follow my orders, no matter what. Understand, Tadashi?”

No matter what .

The words settled like a stone in my gut. Doubt pierced me. Could I trust him? Was he leading me into a trap?

“If I intended harm to you or Anko, it would be done. You wouldn’t have walked away from the Arihara warehouse,” Shirasu snapped, patience thinning. He turned to the door. “I’m going to Mother’s grave. Coming or not?”

My fists clenched. “I’ll go.”

“Good. The early train leaves soon. Remember our roles?”

I dried my hands hard, as if I could scrub away the turmoil. We were brothers returning home, grieving our mother. The pretense felt brittle, ill-fitting.

You can do this, Tadashi. It’s just acting. Trust him.

After all… he is your brother, isn’t he?

I forced a smile. “Let’s go.”

Shirasu raised a skeptical brow. “Brother.”

I stared, blank.

He rolled his eyes. “Let’s go, Brother .” He waited, a challenge in his gaze.

My head throbbed. “Let’s go… Brother,” I managed, the words scraping my throat.

Shirasu snorted, something unreadable flickering in his eyes. He took my hand, and the world blurred into a dizzy rush. We landed at the intersection outside the complex, where two horses waited, already saddled.

We followed the same path I’d taken after Shiro ran, but heavy rain forced us to stop halfway at an inn on the edge of the Henna Forest. The small village inn was warm, run by a kind-faced couple.

In the cozy eatery, Shirasu ordered a steaming bowl of gyudon and nodded for me to choose.

I hesitated, feeling the weight of my empty purse. “Just milk, please.”

“Gyudon and miso for my younger brother as well,” Shirasu told the innkeeper’s wife. He turned to me with a small smile. “You’re too thin. Eat.”

His warmth stole my breath. He played the caring older brother so convincingly it didn’t feel like an act. Heat rose in my neck. I mumbled my thanks, still unsteady in this role.

Shirasu watched me, gaze lingering. “I know you’re broke. Don’t worry—my treat.” His voice was low, warm. The empty room amplified the steady hammer of rain.

My nerves tightened. If my end was near, I wanted to understand my mother first.

“Brother,” I said, the word catching. “Will you tell me about Mother?”

Surprise flickered across his face. He hesitated, then cleared his throat.

“Mother was the kindest person who ever lived,” he said slowly.

He spoke of her gentleness and patience, her obedience to the clan’s will. Chiyo’s parents had died, and Ikaru’s family raised her before arranging a hasty marriage. But she met Masada during his infiltration of the Mitsurugi Clan.

He told how Mother fell in love, how Father proposed and completed a dangerous mission to win her. How Ikaru refused to accept it, framing Masada and driving them into exile in a remote village. There, Chiyo became the guardian of Jikan Temple. There, in time, she carried Shirasu.

Masada trained him and entered him in the Clan Heir selection. At six and a half, Shirasu passed and was named heir. Two years later, Masada vanished—no explanation, no farewell.

“The infiltration mission your elders gave him was already done. Masada simply returned to your clan. Mother didn’t know his dual identity. She waited for him, heartbroken and faithful,” Shirasu said, voice distant.

I pictured Chiyo waiting for a ghost.

“If you don’t mind, I’d like to hear the rest.”

Shirasu blinked, as if remembering I was there.

“After that, everything accelerated. Masada became Head of the Masamune Clan. Ikaru saw his portrait and recognized him. He summoned Mother and me, demanding answers about Father’s true identity and whereabouts.”

Shirasu let out a bitter breath. “But we knew nothing—only his alias, Fushi Mitsurugi. To us, he was just a merchant. Ikaru sent Mother back to the village but kept me imprisoned, claiming he was training me.

“I couldn’t go home. I heard nothing from Mother. Three years later, Ikaru told me she was pregnant. No one knew who the father was—only that she was slandered and shunned. Her health declined. When she gave birth to you, she left the village and returned alone, weeping, without her child. People assumed the baby died. By then, she was failing. She begged Ikaru’s informant to ask him to let me come home, just for a while.” He paused, voice trembling. “I made it just in time. She died in my arms.”

I closed my eyes, fighting the tightness in my chest. Shirasu wiped his face and went on.

“Two years ago, a woman brought me a package from her parents. Inside was Mother’s diary—everything about you and Father. After her banishment, she must have seen the notice of Masada’s appointment as Masamune Head. Our clan always tracks the others.

“At first, she kept silent. How could she admit her husband was the infiltrator? But later, maybe after hearing rumors about me under Ikaru’s control, she began to hope. If Father was powerful enough, maybe he could protect me. For whatever reason, she sought him out to confirm the truth.

“By then, Masada was engaged to Makoto, their marriage imminent. That night, they were together—and Mother conceived you, his second child. After your birth, she entrusted you to a midwife, who delivered you to Father.

“I only truly believed the diary when I saw your photo in the clan bulletin seven months later. Remember? When you had Riku—one of Ikaru’s concubine’s sons—arrested. You look so much like Mother, Tadashi. Ikaru must have noticed. From then on, he was determined to destroy your clan. He wanted me—Masada’s eldest—to kill Masada and his entire family. He loves that kind of irony. I couldn’t defy him. I couldn’t escape. Once you’re all dead, he’ll kill me too.” His voice sank, heavy with exhaustion, like a man condemned to endless toil.

I drew a long breath, tracing the pattern on the tablecloth. He had acted indifferent, as if my choices meant nothing to him—but after hearing him, I sensed something more. Maybe, just maybe, he was hoping. He never said it, but this partnership was a quiet plea for help.

He thought I could break Ikaru’s hold.

He trusted me.

Our food arrived, breaking the silence. Shirasu urged me to eat, and we did so in quiet companionship. I finished quickly—the miso soup and warm milk chasing away the chill.

Shirasu studied me, his expression a subtle blend of concern and calculation. Was he maintaining our cover, or genuinely worried I looked half-starved? (Fine, I was hungry, but I still possessed enough manners not to devour my food.) When he offered more, I declined. It had been too long since I’d eaten a full meal. Shirasu simply smiled faintly and finished his own with quiet composure.

Back in the room, he handed me a sealed book—Mother’s diary. The same one I’d seen in Chiyo’s desk. The seal warmed in my hands.

“You want to understand her better,” he said softly. “There’s so much I might have missed. Read it yourself.”

My throat tightened. “Thank you.” I tucked it into my bag.

Shirasu lay down on one futon and turned his back to me without a word—a disarming gesture of trust. I watched his still form, wondering. Exhaustion? A test? Or had he simply reached a point of resignation, feeling safe enough to sleep beside an uncertain ally?

I lay down, restless, trying to reconcile it all.

Shirasu had chosen to work with me. Trust was essential, and he was making a deliberate show of it. He’d promised to help, and so far, he had. He knew we were practically strangers and that my past with him made trust hard. He needed me to believe him. He was proving it.

I took a long breath and wiped my face.

Questioning his motives wouldn’t help. Did he see me as family, or was this kindness another tool? I didn’t know. But he’d kept his word so far. Maybe that was enough.

It had to be.

Now it was my turn.

The trip back passed without incident. Shirasu walked with me the whole way. Since Raito wasn’t home, he made tofu soup—maybe remembering how much I liked it. We ate in easy silence; we’d used up our words at Mother’s house. When we finished, Shirasu left without lingering.

I needed time to gather myself.

I crept toward Father’s study, pushed the door open, and froze in the dark. The bloodstained tatami had been replaced. The room was neat, almost meticulous. Nothing broken, nothing out of place—except the thick handkerchief I’d made as a child for Father. He hadn’t used it in years; I only kept it to dust his desk. He’d never notice it was gone.

I closed the door and leaned against it as longing washed over me. I wished Father and my siblings were home, gathered in the family room. I’d make tea and serve it quietly. Even if Father or Shiro ignored me, it wouldn’t matter. I wouldn’t speak—being near them would be enough.

I needed to see them one last time before we parted—to carry one good memory with me.

This was a suicide mission.

I knew I would die there. Father would rage, call me unfilial, unreliable, a coward who fled his duty. That was fine.

It was fine.

What happened to me—what Father and my siblings thought of me—none of it mattered.

Only their safety did.

I would protect them with my last breath.

And after that… after that, I would follow you, Mother.
