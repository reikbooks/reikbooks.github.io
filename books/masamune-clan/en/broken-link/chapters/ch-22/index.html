Epilogue C - Masada Masamune

Six and a half months after the destruction of the Mitsurugi clan headquarters.

I t was close to nine at night when Masada finished with the clan treasurer. They’d gone over the ledgers again—money was running out. If nothing changed, they’d miss the king’s installment payments within a month or two.

The emergency funds were gone. The clan was flat broke—every last coin spent, even with everyone working nonstop and brutal austerity measures in place. Even senior officials and A-rank agents had taken cuts and delayed pay.

Masada had to act. He couldn’t let the clan collapse.

He rose from his desk to go home.

“Shall I make dinner, Father?” Shiro asked. She’d sat in on the meeting.

Masada nodded. They returned to the rest house in the main hall complex. While Shiro cooked, Masada showered and thought about their worsening situation. After they ate, he spoke.

“Shiro, I need Tadashi’s help to solve our money problem. Bring me his letters.”

Shiro flinched in surprise but quickly did as asked. Masada read Tadashi’s final letter aloud to her again.

The first time he’d read it, he’d barely stopped himself from tearing it up in front of her.

He’d just been released from the King’s custody then—furious, ashamed. The clan’s offense was nearly unforgivable. Shiro had saved them by completing the King’s impossible demand—destroying the Mitsurugi clan—but the financial penalties remained. And that, Masada knew with cold certainty, was Tadashi’s fault.

If Tadashi hadn’t shattered the Mirai mirror, the king wouldn’t have flown into a rage. He wouldn’t have lost all trust in them. There would have been no reparations.

If Tadashi had warned Masada about the Mitsurugi attack planned for the New Year festival, the clan would have been ready.

Princess Kaguya and Prince Yoru would have escaped unscathed. The spectators wouldn’t have been hurt. The chaos could have been avoided. The clan wouldn’t have been left to clean up and pay for the damage.

Their name wouldn’t be in ruins. No other clan would dare look down on them. Commoners wouldn’t hesitate to do business. Their fortune wouldn’t have bled away.

How dare Tadashi work with Shirasu behind his back? How could he trust an enemy more than his own father?

Masada’s anger simmered. Betrayal burned in his chest; humiliation twisted like a knife. How could his own son be so reckless? So selfish? So blind?

Masada had long known Tadashi’s loyalty was to his family and friends, not the clan. Unacceptable in a successor.

A future clan head had to gauge every word and action by the clan’s interests. Nothing—no personal desire, no family, no friendship—could come before that.

Somehow, Tadashi never grasped it. Masada had tried everything—stern reprimands, measured punishment—but he never struck him in public, never humiliated him.

He protected Tadashi’s dignity, careful not to invite disrespect. He counseled him in private whenever he could.

Masada gave him chance after chance. Each mistake brought more embarrassment. Tadashi stayed stubborn—unable or unwilling to change. In the end, Masada had to strip him of the heirship and name Shiro instead. It weighed on him, but it was right.

Shiro understood exactly what he required in a successor. Cold-blooded when she needed to be, never letting emotion or self-interest cloud her judgment. She kept perfect control and made decisions with ruthless clarity. Even when her stance would earn enemies, she never wavered. That was the price the clan demanded—and she was willing to pay it.

She never acted behind his back or hid things. She put everything on the table, knowing he needed accurate information to lead.

“Father, Tadashi might still be alive,” Shiro had told him. “I don’t know if Shirasu got clear before the base collapsed, but I saw it—he teleported away with Tadashi and Seishiro. They must have survived.”

Masada had no doubt. Shirasu and Tadashi were alive—he was sure of it.

“I told only you,” Shiro added. “I didn’t want you caught off guard if you ran into Tadashi or Shirasu, and I thought the information might be useful if you needed their help. For now, no one else knows—not even Anko or Sadi.”

Masada nodded, pleased. It was the choice he would have made himself.

“I also left Tadashi’s collaboration with Shirasu out of the mission report,” Shiro said. “I treated it as sensitive. Once revealed, it can’t be taken back. It would have serious consequences for the clan, inside and out.” She paused. “I weighed the clan’s reputation—and what would happen if the King learned that the son, the former heir, of the Masamune leader conspired with the Mitsurugi leader’s right hand.”

She hesitated, watching him. “I think it’s reasonable, especially since you hid Shirasu and Tadashi’s true identities from everyone. If that comes out, people will ask hard questions—questions our enemies could use to undermine your authority.”

A slow smile spread across Masada’s face. Pride swelled at his daughter’s strategic mind. He had concealed their identities for one simple reason—to protect them.

The Masamune clan had little to gain or lose from the revelation, but those two children would become instant targets for anyone who opposed him. Ikaru, for instance—he’d already stolen Shirasu’s life.

“Thank you, Shiro. Excellent analysis. Sound judgment. I’m very pleased.”

Masada had decided to grant Shiro’s request and release Tadashi. Perhaps it was best to let him roam for a while. He would never truly free him from the clan. Tadashi wasn’t as quick as Shiro, but he was diligent. Once he learned a skill, he executed it flawlessly.

He was their top agent. Losing him would be the worst outcome. He was resilient, adaptable—willing to do whatever the clan required. Men like him were rare, and Masada knew their worth.

He could use Tadashi as a secret weapon—unknown to everyone else. A little freedom would only make the boy more grateful, more indebted. What mattered more was the tracking seal Tadashi had given them. With it, Masada could summon him whenever he wished.

Masada sighed and refolded the letter. Seven months had slipped by. Now he had to play his ace. The clan’s finances were failing. There was no other choice.

He had a plan—but he needed Tadashi to execute it. Shiro couldn’t. Sadi couldn’t. For this, only Tadashi would do.

“I’ll go and bring Tadashi back. Cover for me and watch over the clan, Shiro,” Masada said, arranging Shiro’s letters in a box. He wrote a brief note granting the temporary transfer of power to the clan successor, then stamped it with both the official clan seal and the Clan Head’s personal seal.

Shiro accepted the document with trembling hands, fully aware of the weight he was placing on her. If anything happened and he didn’t return, she would become the official head of the Masamune clan. Masada patted her head.

“I believe in you.”

He took the sheet with Tadashi’s tracking seal pattern and activated it. A faint energy trace—drawn from the seal’s creator—glimmered in the air. Tadashi was alive.

Shiro hugged him tightly before stepping back. “Take care, Father. Safe travels.”

Masada nodded and hurried from the great hall to follow the trail.

The tracking seal led him deep into the Kingdom of Yami, to a remote region behind Mount Kazan. The terrain was brutal.

He tied his horse at the mountain’s base and pushed on through dense, wild forest.

After crossing a crocodile-infested ravine veined with quicksand, he scaled sheer cliffs. Fog bit at his lungs; the cold slowed him. As he crested the summit, the skies opened and rain lashed down.

He sprinted across the rocky plateau toward a flicker of light ahead. Nestled inside a cave recess stood the outer wall of a house. Masada gripped his tanto and advanced. There were no wards, but two of Seishiro’s wolf beasts loped out to meet him.

He struck first, drawing his spare blade to fight with both. He wove between their claws—parrying, slipping, spinning—then drove one blade into a beast’s heart. In the same motion, he leaped and plunged the second into the other’s chest. Both fell at once.

Masada wiped the blades clean and sheathed them. He picked the lock at the front door and slipped inside.

Empty—no sign of life. Yet the tracking signal pulsed from within. He moved down the corridor to the last door. He hesitated, then turned the knob and pushed it open.

An oil lamp flickered beside a wide opening carved into the stone wall. Outside, a mist-choked chasm plunged into haze, its bottom lost from sight. Rain drifted through the opening, leaving the table beneath it damp, but the fresh air was welcome. Tadashi lay on a bed in the corner, an IV line taped to his arm.

Masada froze. He sat on the edge of the bed, studying his son’s face. No obvious injuries—no pallor, no wounds—just a quiet, even sleep.

“Tadashi,” Masada said softly. No response. He shook his shoulder and called again, louder. Still nothing. He frowned. Tadashi was usually easy to wake.

He looked again, and the past pressed down on him. Only six months had passed since they’d last met, and already he had almost forgotten how much Tadashi reminded him of Makoto’s death. Every glance at the boy brought a suffocating weight—enough to make Masada avoid his presence.

Of course Tadashi wasn’t guilty.

He hadn’t killed Makoto. But the thought still crushed Masada at times. He couldn’t afford to break down in front of his son, so he buried himself in work and tried to forget. On purpose, he kept Tadashi at arm’s length.

He knew he wasn’t a model father—he had always loved Makoto’s children more. The thought of Shiro being kidnapped by Ikaru back then was unbearable. If that had happened, it would have destroyed them both.

He breathed slowly and took Tadashi’s hand. What had this boy endured under Ikaru? And where was Shirasu now?

The answer came at once.

“What the hell are you doing here?” a voice hissed from the doorway.

Masada looked up—and went still. A ghost from the past stood there, indistinct and undeniable. How many years had it been? Over two decades.

Shirasu. A man now. His eldest son glared at him with pure hatred. The irony wasn’t lost on Masada—he should have been the one hating Shirasu for killing Makoto.

He glanced at the figure beside him—tall, broad-shouldered, glasses. Seishiro. Today was the day for reunions, it seemed.

“I came to check on everyone,” Masada said calmly.

Shirasu scoffed and closed the distance.

“Tadashi’s in a coma. He hasn’t woken since I dragged him out of Ikaru’s base.”

Masada froze. Tadashi had been unconscious the whole time?

“What happened?”

Shirasu’s glare promised violence. “Ikaru tortured him. I wasn’t there at the end, so I don’t know everything. But I know this—he branded Tadashi with the Seal of Obedience. When that bastard died, Tadashi broke free. I healed his wounds, but he still won’t wake up.”

“The Seal of Obedience?” Masada frowned. That might conflict with the speech seal—an unexpected tangle. Still, he could remove the speech seal and repair Tadashi’s energy pathways. After that, he should wake.

“I can wake him,” Masada said. “But I’ll need his help with something.”

Shirasu drew a long breath and gestured to Seishiro. After a brief glance at Masada, Seishiro turned and left.

Without another word, Shirasu grabbed a glass and a bottle of sake and set them hard on the table.
