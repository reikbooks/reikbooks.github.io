Blue

Book 6 of Masamune Clan Series

Some days are never forgotten. Some burdens are never released.

For Tadashi, a rare family outing to a nature reserve promises a welcome escape from the harsh demands of his clan elders. But lurking beneath the wilderness’s peaceful surface is a deadly betrayal. With danger closing in, will Tadashi find the strength to protect his siblings? It’s a day he’ll never forget alright—for reasons he’ll never stop carrying.

“Lower!” I flinched as Elder Low’s cane cracked against my thigh. His face hardened, his voice a thunderous rumble. “Your stance is weak. A flawed foundation will never hold.”

“Yes, Master!” I choked out, nodding jerkily.

He demonstrated the kata again, and I mirrored his movements. My initial attempts were clumsy, each motion feeling awkward and forced. I was on my umpteenth try when Elder Low materialized beside me.

“Your mind is wandering,” he snarled, his cane finding its mark on my already throbbing upper arm. “You’re too slow. At this rate, you’ll be crippled before you can even lift a hand.”

I tried to correct my form, but Elder Low’s dissatisfaction only grew. It was late when he finally called a halt to the training.

“Pathetic,” Elder Low spat. I knelt before him, head bowed, lungs burning. My body trembled uncontrollably. Black spots danced at the edges of my vision, no matter how many times I blinked.

“What’s the matter with you today? Late and unfocused.”

After school, as usual, the elders had assigned me chores—today, it was helping in the communal kitchen. The workload was unusually heavy, making me late for the dojo. Elder Low had already punished me with ten laps around the clan hall. My legs were screaming by the time we started the new kata. I’d felt off all day…was that why—

“You’re drifting again!” The cane slammed into the already bruised flesh of my arm. A cry of pain ripped from my throat.

“I’m sorry, Master,” I rasped, my throat tight. “I’ll try harder.”

I lost track of time while Elder Low berated me. The dining hall and kitchen were dark by the time I stumbled home. I went straight to my room, changed, then practically crawled into my futon, curling up and burying my face in the covers.

God, everything hurts … There wasn’t a single inch of my body that hadn’t felt the sting of Elder Low’s cane.

I lay there for a while, eyes closed, the weight of tomorrow’s make-up math test pressing down on me. I’d completely bombed the last one—clan meeting had run late, leaving me no time to study. This time, I had to pass.

I have to study.

Ugh …my body screamed in protest, every muscle aching, begging for rest.

Come on, Tadashi.

Elder Yun had been disappointed with my declining grades on Saturday, his reprimand stinging more than any cane. I was required to attend extra classes every weekend to improve, or he’d raise the issue with Father and the other elders.

Don’t cause any more trouble.

I took a deep breath, clenching my fists and pulling back the covers. Pain lanced through me as I forced myself upright. I bit back a groan, careful not to wake Mother and my siblings. Slowly, agonizingly, I pushed myself to my feet and retrieved my books. Concentrating felt impossible; my head throbbed.

Better I suffer than Shiro.

Shiro wouldn’t have these problems. She could solve equations in her sleep and master a kata with a single glance.

My duty is different. Being the heir requires sacrifice. Mother wouldn’t be able to handle it if Shiro had been chosen. Things have been better between Mother and Father since Shiro refused and the elders chose me. Could I really let them go back to the way things were?

My jaw tightened. No. Of course not. Stop whining, Tadashi. Be strong.

I blinked back the tears and forced myself to focus on my studies.

I hadn’t been studying long when my door slid open. I frowned, startled. Shiro stood there, holding a cup of tea, a notebook, and a small, cylindrical container.

“Here,” she said, setting the items on my desk. The tea smelled fragrant. I took a sip. It was delicious. The warmth spread through me, soothing my aching chest, and the sweetness settled my empty stomach.

“Mind if I borrow another one of your books?”

Shiro had probably read every book in the house twice. I nodded, my attention drawn to the small container. The label read “Bruise Salve.” My chest tightened. It was the medicine I hadn’t dared ask Mother for, afraid to worry her. But how had Shiro—

I quickly slipped the salve into my pocket as Shiro returned, settling across from me at the kotatsu . She opened her book – a supplementary social studies text from Elder Yun. I hadn’t even cracked it, despite carrying it everywhere. I reached for the notebook. Shiro’s neat notes, explanations, and worked examples of the very math problems I was struggling with filled the pages.

“Try them yourself first,” Shiro said, her hand covering mine on the table. “If you get stuck, just ask. I’ll study with you.”

“Aren’t you tired, Shiro?”

Shiro shook her head. “I can’t sleep. I’d rather be here.”

I squeezed her hand gently. I couldn’t find the words to express my gratitude, just a soft, “Thank you, Shiro.”

The following evening, after dinner, Mother called me over. “Tadashi,” she said, holding out a piece of paper. “Why did you hide this? Shiro found it tucked inside one of your books.”

My heart sank as I unfolded the paper. It was the notice about the academy field trip to the nature reserve. Students could bring a family member. I had hidden it.

I hadn’t meant for Mother to see it. I knew I couldn’t go. I couldn’t miss Elder Yun’s extra classes. My grades were still too low to risk asking Father for permission. Besides, telling the truth would only spark another argument between my parents. Mother resented my clan duties, while Father always prioritized the clan. I swallowed hard.

“Sorry, Mother. I forgot,” I mumbled. Why hadn’t I just ripped it up? Why torture myself with impossible dreams of a family outing? Father was always busy; he wouldn’t have had time anyway.

“Next time, Tadashi,” Mother said gently, “be more careful where you put important notices. Let me know right away.”

I nodded, my stomach twisting as she handed the notice to Father.

“Tadashi wants to go on the academy field trip,” Mother told Father. “Shiro, Raito, and I will go with him tomorrow. Is that alright?”

Oh, Mother…

I winced under Father’s intense gaze. Had Elder Yun already told him about the extra classes? Did he know about my failing grades? If Father refused, Mother would definitely argue with him. It’s better to say I’m not feeling well. That way, he won’t have to forbid me from going.

Before I could correct Mother and refuse, Father nodded.

“Alright,” Father said. “I can’t join you, but I can accompany you to the academy.”

My jaw dropped. Had I heard that right?

Father was letting me go? Really? A warm, buoyant feeling bubbled up inside me. A small part of my mind worried about the extra classes. What if Father didn’t know? What if Elder Yun is furious on Monday? But the rest of me didn’t care. Getting scolded was a future problem. Right now, all that mattered was the field trip tomorrow – with Mother, Shiro, and Raito!

A wide smile spread across my face as I bowed my thanks.

I packed quickly, excitement bubbling inside me, and fell into a deep sleep. Tomorrow promised to be a day I would never forget.

Father accompanied us to the academy that Sunday morning. After kissing Raito’s forehead and Mother’s cheek, he clapped Shiro on the shoulder and gave me a nod.

“Keep an eye on your mother and siblings, Tadashi. I’ll be back for you this afternoon.”

I nodded, watching him until he disappeared. It’s alright. We can go out with Father another time.

Shiro tugged my arm. “Come on, Tadashi. The announcements are up in the courtyard.”

A group of my classmates passed us, joking and laughing. Shiro squeezed my hand.

“Aren’t you going with them?” she asked.

I shook my head. We didn’t have to stay with our class. Some classmates ignored me, mistaking my quietness for arrogance. Others just plain disliked me, quick to taunt or humiliate me whenever they got the chance. The trip would be more enjoyable without them.

“It’s okay, Shiro. I’d rather stay here. We don’t get to do much as a family.”

Shiro shrugged and then turned her attention to the crowd.

“Is that Anko?” she whispered.

I nodded. Anko, our cousin, was a teacher in the academy. From a distance, she looked busy organizing the students. She seemed different than the last time I’d seen her on a mission – sweeter, gentler, more cheerful.

Mother joined us, stopping beside me. Some of the other parents recognized us with respectful nods. A few offered greetings. Mother chatted with them easily.

We set off soon. Raito fidgeted in Mother’s arms, wanting to explore the trees, but she held him firm. I offered to walk with him, and he readily took my hand, Shiro falling into step beside Mother.

The forest path started to climb after a while. Raito grew tired and asked for a piggyback ride. Just before lunch, we reached a riverbank and stopped. Shiro helped Mother spread a mat and unpack our lunch.

Several families were picnicking beneath the trees. Maybe we should have joined them. The memory of Raito nearly drowning still haunted me. My stomach churned as he tugged at my hair, pointing excitedly towards the river. I winced as he yanked out a few strands, my anxiety battling with his innocent enthusiasm. The thought of him near deep water, the possibility of him falling in and being swept away by the current, filled me with dread.

Thankfully, Mother called us over. Raito, spotting his favorite teriyaki chicken, scampered towards her. I sat down beside Shiro.

“We finally got our picnic,” Shiro said, beaming at me. “You wanted one in the city park during the festival, remember?”

Heat rushed to my cheeks. Was it that obvious? I hadn’t voiced the thought to anyone. I offered a small smile and nodded.

Raito finished his rice and whined for more. Shiro swatted his hand away from Mother’s bento box. “Hey!” she scolded. I quickly finished half of my bento before they could start bickering and offered it to Raito.

“Why do you keep feeding him? He’ll turn into a sumo wrestler!” Shiro exclaimed.

“No! You’re fat!” Raito retorted. They took off after each other. Mother chuckled, telling them to settle down and finish eating.

“Are you full, Tadashi?” Mother asked, smiling. I nodded. Once Raito finished, he and Shiro went back to playing. We still had some time to relax before continuing our hike. Mother patted her lap, inviting me to lie down.

I hesitated. What if my classmates saw me? They’d probably tease me for being a mama’s boy. Still, I obeyed. Mother reminisced about how, before Raito was born, I often napped in her lap. The feeling of her fingers carding through my hair was soothing, and my eyelids grew heavy.

“How’s your training with the elders going, Tadashi?” Mother asked softly. “You’ve been getting home late.”

I instinctively held my breath. My body ached, the memory of each training session with Elder Low a fresh bruise. But Shiro had brought me the salve. I couldn’t complain.

I can’t worry her.

“It’s going well, Mother. Elder Low is teaching us a lot of new kata,” I replied quietly.

“And your studies? Your end-of-year exams are coming up soon, aren’t they?”

I nodded, then bit my lip. Should I tell her now? I had to tell her eventually. Might as well do it while she was happy.

“Elder Yun wants me to attend extra classes on the weekends,” I confessed, the words tumbling out.

Mother’s hand stilled in my hair. I winced.

“Just to prepare for the exams, Mother,” I added quickly. Had I said the wrong thing? Maybe I shouldn’t have mentioned it. She hated it when clan duties ate up my free time.

I should have just said I was going to the library or hanging out with a friend.

Mother sighed. “Tadashi, it’s okay. Just tell me the truth. I’ll talk to your father. You seem overwhelmed. Can you handle it all?”

I swallowed. No . The elders piled on more responsibilities every day. I was overwhelmed, but admitting it would only spark another fight between Mother and Father. The thought made my chest tighten. I clenched my fists, forcing a smile.

“Don’t worry, Mother. Everything’s fine.”

Mother studied me for a moment.

“You’re my son, Tadashi. It’s only natural to worry.” She resumed stroking my hair. I buried my face in her lap, breathing slowly, trying to empty my mind, focusing only on the gentle caress of her hand.

Physical affection from my parents was rare these days. I wished I could stay in her embrace like this, just a little longer. Before Mother returned to taking care of Raito, Shiro, and Father. Before I had to be the responsible eldest son, the future clan leader again.

Sunlight filtered through the leaves, warm and bright, like a dream. I closed my eyes, clutching the fabric of Mother’s clothes.

If this is a dream, I don’t want to wake up.

I must have dozed off. Raito’s mischievous grin and the pinch of his small fingers on my nose startled me awake.

“Tadashi, wake up!”

I sat up, blinking. The picnic things were already packed. Shiro stood beside Mother, who was kneeling, ready to roll up the mat. “Come on, Tadashi,” she said. “The others have already left.”

“Tadashi, up!” Raito demanded.

I quickly got up and swung Raito onto my back. We started walking again.

“Why do you always spoil Raito? Make him walk,” Shiro grumbled.

“Get down,” she told Raito. “You’re too big and heavy.” Raito just laughed and tightened his grip around my neck.

Shiro was lecturing Raito when she abruptly stopped, her face paling as she scanned our surroundings. I frowned.

“What is it, Shiro?”

Shiro’s eyes narrowed, flicking towards Mother up ahead. She leaned closer, shaking her head. “Nothing. Just a little dizzy.”

I took her hand and squeezed it. Shiro seemed stunned, staring at me with a dazed expression.

“Want to rest for a bit?” I asked softly.

Shiro blinked, frowning. “No.” Then she called out to Mother, “Mother, let Tadashi carry the picnic basket. You can take Raito. Tadashi looks tired.”

I gaped at her, then turned to Mother as she approached. “Oh, n-no, Mother. I just slept—”

Mother had already set down the basket and taken Raito, holding him since he’d fallen asleep. She continued walking. I bent down, picked up the basket, and nudged Shiro with my elbow.

“Why’d you say that, Shiro?” I asked, a little annoyed. “Now Mother will have to carry extra weight.”

Shiro just shrugged. “It’s fine. Besides, Raito’s asleep. He might fall.” She took my hand again, looping her arm through mine. I sighed and let her. Shiro could get clingy like this sometimes.

Shiro grew increasingly agitated as we walked. Finally, she squeezed my hand hard and whispered, her voice so low it raised the hairs on the back of my neck.

“Tadashi,” she breathed. “There’s an assasin in this forest.”

“An assasin?!”

I squeezed Shiro’s hand tighter and looked around. My class was ahead of us, with only a few stragglers behind.

“What do you mean, Shiro?” I whispered. “How do you know?”

Shiro pressed her lips together, refusing to answer. Her face was pale. Was she serious? She couldn’t be joking about something like this. Shiro had incredibly sharp instincts; several times, she’d known what I wanted before I’d even spoken. But…

“We have to cancel this trip,” she said, her voice strained. “Everyone needs to get out of this forest.”

I frowned.

“Shiro, I can’t just tell Mother or the teachers something like this without any explanation,” I said. “They’ll have questions. They might think I’m playing a prank.”

“Would you rather someone die than risk embarrassing yourself?” Shiro’s voice was sharp.

Heat flooded my cheeks. “No! Of course not. I don’t want anyone to get hurt. It’s just… they can’t dismiss everyone based on a child’s claim without proof—”

“Do they have to wait until someone is attacked?”

I opened my mouth to argue, then hesitated. “They need something concrete. Overhearing a plan, seeing something suspicious…”

“They’ll listen to you , Tadashi. I’m just a kid. If you tell them, they’ll believe you. Just say you overheard something in the bushes.”

“It’s not that simple. What if they ask what I heard? If they investigate and find out I lied, it would disgrace our clan.”

Shiro clicked her tongue and stamped her foot in frustration. “Why are you worried about the clan right now?! This is an emergency! If someone dies, it’s on your head.”

Shiro released my hand and strode ahead. My stomach twisted.

“Shiro, wait…”

“I’m going to ask Mother to take us home,” she called over her shoulder. “It’s up to you to save everyone else. If anything happens, it’s your responsibility.”

I bit my lip, my mind racing. What was I supposed to do?

“M-maybe I should tell Anko to keep a sharper eye out,” I stammered.

Shiro shrugged and quickened her pace. I hurried after her.

“Where are they?” I finally asked, even though I still didn’t know what I would do. I couldn’t possibly handle this alone.

“I don’t know,” she said. “I can only sense their intent, not just anger or the desire to harm, but a cold-blooded intent to kill.”

I glanced at the people around us: an elderly woman with a young girl, three older academy students, a mustached man with a boy from my class, a young man dressed in black, the beginner class teacher, and the school medic—a young man with sandy blond hair and dark eyes.

“Do you think they’re among us?” I asked, eyeing the man in black. He seemed out of place. He didn’t look like a student. Was he chaperoning a younger sibling?

“Depends who the target is,” Shiro replied, her gaze fixed ahead. “But they’ll likely try to isolate them.”

I considered her words. Who would be targeted on a school field trip? A student? Surely not… A teacher? No, it would be easier to attack them elsewhere, another time. Too many witnesses here, though it might be possible if they were alone. Were the parents the targets? Some who were usually hard to reach might be here today. Could there be multiple targets? Gathering them at the same event would certainly make it easier.

But who was important enough to be targeted here ?

Elder Han’s words from last week suddenly echoed in my mind: A leader is the spearhead of their people. Even off the battlefield, the clan leader is the enemy’s first target. Be prepared to be attacked from all sides—physically, or through scrutiny of your every action. You, and your family.

A chilling thought surfaced. Was it possible…? No, I pushed it away.

We caught up to Mother, and Shiro put her plan into action. She pretended to trip, crying out and clutching her ankle.

“I think I sprained it!” she whimpered. “I want to go home.”

“But we haven’t even reached the halfway point,” Mother said, glancing at me. “It would be a shame for Tadashi to miss the rest of the trip.”

I shook my head. “It’s alright, Mother. I’m happy we got to walk together this far.”

“Alright,” Mother said, relief evident in her voice. “I’ll let your teacher know. Take Shiro to the medic. He should have some ointment for the swelling.”

I nodded and helped Shiro, who was putting on a convincing limp, find the medic—a tall young man at the back of the group.

“What’s the matter?” he greeted us, his smile friendly. I explained about Shiro’s ankle, and he helped her sit on a fallen log. He knelt to examine her ankle, and Shiro suddenly yelped.

“Oh! I need to pee!” she exclaimed, hopping on one foot. She grabbed my arm. I stared at her, bewildered.

“Ow… come on! There’s a river over there.”

Shiro pulled me along. Before we reached the river, she abruptly veered off behind some tall bushes, yanking me hard. I stumbled against her.

“Tadashi, the medic is the assasin!” she hissed. “Run! He’s after us!”

Before I could question her, Shiro was sprinting through the undergrowth. I followed, leaping over roots and dodging branches. I glanced back. I couldn’t see him, but I could hear the pursuit closing in.

We’re not going to make it.

“Shiro, I’ll distract him! Find Mother!”

Shiro nodded and sped up. I stopped, my hand trembling as I pulled out a shuriken. Just buy her some time…

The bushes rustled. I threw the shuriken, then ran in the opposite direction.

The man followed me. Good.

Wait… does that mean I’m the target?

I ducked under a low-hanging branch, squeezing through a narrow gap between two trees, hoping he couldn’t follow. But the forest thinned out, and I found myself on the riverbank. In the distance, I saw Shiro almost reaching Mother. They were alone.

Something whizzed past my ear.

I dropped to the ground. A nunchaku whistled through the air where my head had been a moment before. He slammed the chain down again, and I rolled, narrowly avoiding the weighted ends. The blows came fast and furious, giving me no chance to counter.

I spotted an opening, dodged, and used the kata Elder Low had drilled into me, thrusting my fist into his ribs. But I wasn’t fast enough. He stumbled back, but quickly recovered, grabbing my wrist, twisting it, and slamming me to the ground. My arm bent at an agonizing angle.

“Aargh!”

Pain exploded in my arm. I kicked out, connecting with his jaw, and he recoiled, releasing his grip. I scrambled to my feet, cradling my injured arm.

I heard someone shout my name.

Mother?

The momentary distraction was all it took. The whir of the nunchaku and the sickening thud against the back of my head sent me spiraling into darkness.
