Epilogue - Anko Masamune

← Back to Masamune Clan Series [EN]

Uncle Masada’s house was quiet when I arrived. Only raised voices from the dining room broke the stillness. The argument ceased abruptly as I entered. Tadashi and Shiro sat stiffly at the table, two teacups between them. Shiro glared at me, as if I’d interrupted something important. Tadashi, who had been staring at the tabletop with red-rimmed eyes, turned, his face etched with anxiety.

They both rose. “Anko,” Tadashi’s voice trembled. “Where’s Mother?”

I took a deep breath. Gently, I told them about Aunt Makoto, omitting the gruesome details. She had been gravely injured, and… she hadn’t survived.

The news struck them like a physical blow. Tadashi’s face crumpled, and he began to weep silently. Shiro swayed, her face ashen, and collapsed. Tadashi caught her, holding her close. He looked up at me, his cheeks streaked with tears.

“Can… can I see her?”

I shook my head, sitting down across from him. “The clan has already taken her.”

A small voice called out from upstairs—Raito, calling for his mother. Tadashi flinched. “Mother!” Raito cried, his voice thick with tears.

Tadashi laid Shiro gently on the tatami mat and hurried upstairs. I checked on Shiro, then followed. Tadashi sat with Raito in his lap, half-embracing him. “…want Mother,” Raito mumbled sleepily. Tadashi stroked his hair, his hand trembling. “Raito, M-Mother…” Tadashi whispered, his voice choked with tears. He swallowed hard. “Mother is….”

“Tadashi… are you crying?” Raito asked, tilting his head back to look at his brother.

Tadashi shook his head, unable to speak. Raito wrapped his small arms around Tadashi’s neck, his voice fading. “Don’t cry, Tadashi. I love you.”

I watched them for a moment, then quietly closed the door and returned to the dining room to carry Shiro to her room. Later, as I prepared dinner, shouting erupted from Tadashi’s room.

“Why?! Why did you do that?! You left Mother! You coward! You should have died!” Shiro’s voice was raw with grief and rage. Thuds punctuated her words, the sound of something hitting flesh.

“Don’t cry! You have no right! Just apologizing… as if that will bring her back! This is all your fault! You always cause trouble! Father and Mother were always fighting because of you! It was always you, you, you! Even so, I tried to help you… and this is how you repay me?”

I opened the door. Tadashi huddled in the corner, Shiro pummeling him with a pillow. I pulled her back.

“I hate you! You don’t deserve to be a big brother! Don’t talk to me! Don’t come near me! I’m sick of seeing your face!” She stormed out, slamming the door.

I started towards Tadashi, then paused as I heard Uncle Masada’s voice downstairs. I hurried down.

“Thank you for looking after the kids, Anko,” Uncle Masada said, his voice weary. “I’ve contacted Makoto’s family. They’ll arrive later today. Her mother will stay and look after the children.”

I nodded.

“Where’s Tadashi?”

“Father…” Tadashi’s voice was quiet, hesitant. He stood at the bottom of the stairs, his hair disheveled, his face pale and drawn.

“Wait for me in my study, Tadashi. We need to talk.”

Tadashi’s face paled further, but he nodded silently. “Yes, Father,” he murmured.

I excused myself, sensing they needed privacy.

After Aunt Makoto’s funeral, exams at the academy kept me busy. I heard Shiro and Raito had gone to stay with their grandmother in the countryside. Uncle Masada and Tadashi had moved. Tadashi’s teacher mentioned his grades had slipped, though he’d managed to pass.

A week into summer break, Uncle Masada came to see me, asking me to mentor Tadashi’s for the upcoming semester. Tadashi followed, head down, still distraught, his gaze empty, dark circles under his eyes. He bowed respectfully, his soft greeting echoing Aunt Makoto’s gentle voice.

I studied him for a moment, then led him to the dojo. I tested him with standard academy kata. He kept up. Then I attacked, using our clan’s techniques.

He wasn’t ready. My kick sent him sprawling.

Tadashi looked up, stunned, as if I’d stabbed him in the back.

“Get up,” I said curtly. “We’re not finished.”

Something flickered in his expression—the look of a cornered animal. Good. He needs this . He rose, assuming the defensive stance favored by our clan—the one Elder Low always taught first.

I smiled.

Over the next few minutes, I tested him on Elder Low’s basic techniques. The old man only trained the best. I graduated with a broken arm and the satisfaction of a sprained ankle on his part. Pathetic, really, but it still makes me smile.

Now I knew how far Tadashi had progressed. I dodged, delivering a swift kick to his chest. He went down, coughing, unable to rise immediately.

I offered him a hand. He looked up, blood trickling from his split lip, surprised by the gesture. Elder Low would have berated him, maybe even kicked him while he was down.

I grinned, pulling him up. I leaned close, my voice low. “Excellent. Now, use everything you have. Attack me. Imagine I’m the one who killed your mother.”

I stepped back, assuming a defensive stance. Tadashi’s face hardened as he shifted into an attack stance. He lunged, a flurry of kicks and punches. I parried, jabbed, twisted, our bodies locked in a fast-paced exchange.

I gave him no quarter. He had to use everything he had. His fist connected with my arm, pushing me back. I caught his elbow, twisting, sending him to the ground. He didn’t give up. A sweeping kick caught my leg, forcing me to backflip. He slammed into my side, his elbow connecting with my ribs, sending us both crashing down.

I rolled, bringing my knee up hard into his stomach. Grappling… we’ll work on that . I pinned him, my arm locked around his head. One twist … His breath hitched, his throat constricted.

I released him, springing to my feet. He lay there, hands clenched, shoulders shaking, his breath ragged. I was surprised he hadn’t passed out.

I helped him up and bowed slightly.

I dragged him, gasping, over to Uncle Masada. Tadashi’s face was flushed, hair plastered to his forehead, clothes soaked with sweat. He looked down, fearing his father’s reprimand. But Uncle Masada smiled. We’d seen his potential.

“Thank you, Anko,” Uncle Masada said. “We’ll discuss the details later.”

I nodded, clapping Tadashi on the shoulder. “From today on, you’re officially my student. Congratulations!”

He looked up, the dullness in his eyes momentarily gone. Then his gaze dropped again, no answering smile. A flicker of something as he mumbled his thanks, then… blankness.

I exchanged a look with Uncle Masada. An unspoken accusation hung in the air.

He didn’t deny it.

A month into the new school year, Uncle Masada came to my house. I cut him off before he could speak. “I’m not a psychologist. If this is about Tadashi, I can’t help.”

He remained silent as I prepared tea. We drank in silence.

After taking Tadashi on, I tested his academic abilities. Above average compared to his peers, but struggling with the accelerated curriculum. A long, difficult conversation finally revealed why: Shiro had been tutoring him. Without her, he was floundering.

The elders, of course, wouldn’t accept that. They piled on extra classes and assignments. He was staying up all night, training before dawn, no rest, no breaks. No wonder he was exhausted, his grades slipping.

Worse, he’d internalized the label of “stupid,” believing he deserved the punishments.

I’d planned to address this with Uncle Masada at our monthly meeting, but when Tadashi fell asleep at my desk, his kimono shifted, revealing dark bruises. More littered his body. I stormed into the main hall, where Uncle Masada was meeting with Elder Low. I sealed the room and didn’t leave until Uncle Masada agreed to my terms: I had exclusive control of Tadashi’s training, no more Elder Low or Elder Yun, no extra academic work.

Things improved gradually, but Tadashi’s eyes still held that unsettling emptiness.

“You haven’t spoken to Tadashi?” I asked.

He shook his head. “He insists everything is fine.”

I shrugged, not entirely surprised. From my conversations with Tadashi, he seemed terrified of disappointing his father. Maybe he didn’t want to lose his father the way he’d lost his mother. Actually, it was more than that; he’d lost his siblings, too.

Hold on. When Tadashi had looked at Raito, spoken of Shiro’s help… there had been a flicker of light. Maybe… they were the key to bringing Tadashi back.

“How are Raito and Shiro? Why did they move?”

“Shiro’s last letter said Raito’s been having more tantrums and refusing to eat.” Uncle Masada sighed, sipping his tea. “I sent them to the countryside for their safety. The preliminary investigation suggests the attack was planned by another clan. Makoto is dead because of it, and there’s no guarantee they’ll stop. Shiro and Raito would be easy targets if they stayed here.”

I shook my head. “You could have arranged for their protection here. They miss each other. You separated them when they needed each other most.”

Uncle Masada considered this for a moment, then smiled faintly. “You’re right, Anko. Very well, if you think their return will help. It seems I’m increasingly in your debt. I’ll make the arrangements. Will you bring Shiro and Raito back?”

I rolled my eyes. “Of course. Leave it to me.”

“Your father says you don’t want to come home?” I asked Shiro at their grandmother’s house. Raito, asleep in her arms, clung to her even as I tried to take him. We walked towards the harbor.

Shiro pursed her lips. “I’m coming back for Raito,” Shiro said firmly. “He’s fussy even with Grandmother. Tadashi’s busy. He can’t look after him all day.”

“You can’t either. What about school?” I asked, momentarily forgetting her age. She always sounded so much older.

Shiro scoffed. “No, thank you. The kids at school give me a headache. I’ll take the equivalency exam at the academy, so don’t worry.”

Well, if the stories about Shiro’s intelligence were true, it shouldn’t be a problem. I nodded, then glanced at her again.

“Are you still angry at Tadashi?”

Shiro’s expression immediately hardened.

“Mind your own business, Anko,” she said sharply.

I paused, then said softly, “I’m worried you’ll end up like me, Shiro. I was arguing with my brother when he left for the battlefield. He died there. I still regret we didn’t part on good terms.”

Shiro fell silent. She didn’t argue further, just looked away, her brow furrowed.

“You haven’t seen how Tadashi has been since you left. He’s always thinking about you and Raito. He doesn’t dare ask Uncle Masada, but he’s asked me about you. Try to give him a chance.”

During the journey, I explained Uncle Masada’s plan. For their safety, Shiro and Raito would assume new identities. Officially, they would live in a separate house a few blocks behind Uncle Masada’s new residence. In reality, Uncle Masada and Tadashi would live with them. The two houses were connected by a secret underground passage. No one knew about the house, not even the elders.

Uncle Masada had only informed Elder Yun about the new identities, but no one knew their new names or where they would be living. He only trusted me.

Akarui and Yukito Masamune. Shiro and Raito’s new names. Shiro listened to my explanation without comment. We entered their new home through the secret passage near my house, emerging in a storeroom in a high-walled back garden.

The house was small but clean, recently renovated. Tadashi had also placed a subtle, invisible barrier around the property that would alert him if anyone other than Uncle Masada’s children entered.

Like me.

The kitchen door flew open. Tadashi stepped out, holding a tanto. He froze, staring at his siblings, his face pale.

Raito, now awake, wriggled free and ran towards him, shouting, “Tadashi!”

In that moment, something crumbled behind the emptiness in Tadashi’s eyes. He dropped the tanto, knelt, and embraced his little brother fiercely. His eyes closed, his lips curved into a smile, tears streaming down his cheeks.

“Raito…” he whispered over and over. He kissed his brother’s face, then hugged him again.

The image of Subaru, had he lived, flashed through my mind. A pang of sadness squeezed my chest. I looked away, my gaze falling on Shiro. Her mask of coldness had cracked, tears glistening in her eyes. Tadashi opened his eyes and stared directly at her, his gaze filled with love.

He missed them so much.

Shiro approached. Tadashi repeatedly tried to wipe away his tears. He picked up Raito and seemed to swallow with difficulty. His smile wide.

“Shiro…” he said softly, looking down. He looked like he wanted to hug her, but he was afraid she would reject him. His voice was hoarse as he whispered, “Welcome home.”

“Don’t talk to me… I’m sick of seeing your face!” Shiro’s words, overheard that night, echoed in my mind.

Shiro raised a trembling hand and touched Tadashi’s cheek, making him meet her gaze. A long moment passed.

“Brother,” she whispered, “I’m home.” Then she hugged him tightly. Tadashi’s tears flowed freely as he returned the embrace, whispering her name, kissing her forehead.

I looked up, smiling, a silent prayer for Subaru. A weight lifted from my shoulders.

Sunlight bathed the small, quiet garden.

Bright.

Warm.

Under the blue sky that afternoon, a family reunited.
