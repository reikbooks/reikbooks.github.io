Chapter 4

← Back to Masamune Clan Series [EN]

I closed the door and sat across from Nobuga. I’d tied him to the chair myself—legs and torso bound tight, both hands secured flat to the polished surface of his ornate desk. If he squirmed too much, the circulation would cut off. Somehow, he still managed to look smug.

Tadashi was in the next room with Kitoru, leaving me alone with my supposed uncle.

“You’re not dead, Sadi?” Nobuga said, far too casual for someone in restraints. “Well, no surprise there. You Masamune operatives are like cockroaches—hard to squash. I just didn’t expect Akito to send you .”

If his hands weren’t tied down, I could almost see him gesturing to pour tea.

I tapped my fingers on the polished wood, weighing my options. The jewel box was the mission. But the man in front of me—the face that mirrored my father’s—that was the real disruption.

“Why did Father never talk about you?” The question slipped out before I could stop it.

Nobuga stared, startled, then let out a short, harsh chuckle. “My god, of all the things you could ask, you lead with the obvious?” He smirked, leaning forward as much as his bonds allowed. “Looks like Akito failed miserably in your education.”

The words were baseless, thrown by a stranger who knew nothing about me or Father—but they hit a nerve, sharp and unexpected. A familiar heat rose in my chest. I leaned forward, drawing a kunai smoothly from my haori. With deliberate slowness, I pressed the flat of the blade against Nobuga’s right hand, still pinned to the desk.

He flinched, trying to jerk his hand back, but I clamped down on his wrist. He wasn’t going anywhere.

“Listen,” I whispered, voice low and dangerously close to his ear. “I might lack Father’s… finesse, but I’ve never shied away from getting my hands dirty. Like, say, prying your fingernails off—one by one—with this.”

To make the point, I angled the kunai and sliced cleanly across the base of his index fingernail. Blood welled instantly. Nobuga screamed, a raw, choked sound, bucking against the ropes.

“You little devil! What do you want?” he hissed, sweat already beading on his flushed face. His pain tolerance was disappointing.

I stared at him, unimpressed. I should’ve been demanding the location of the damn jewel box—locking down the mission objective. But the sting of his insult, the echo of my father in his face… they twisted my priorities.

“Tell me everything about Father’s family,” I said coldly. “Start from the beginning. I have plenty of time.”

Nobuga glared, venom in his eyes. He wasn’t going to answer.

I simply pressed the kunai harder on his bleeding finger. He broke immediately, words tumbling out in a resentful rush.

His name was Akira. My father’s younger identical twin. They grew up in a family steeped in crime—generations of thieves and fences. Father despised the life and eventually cut all ties, joining the Masamune clan. My grandparents were apparently still alive but had long disowned him. They kept a wary distance, occasionally monitoring each other. But both sides followed a strict code of non-interference.

“But now you’re here, threatening your own blood. Does this mean Akito finally changed his mind? Declared open war on his ancestors?” Akira challenged, searching my face.

I met his gaze flatly.

“Father’s dead. Been a month and a half now,” I said. “The clan elders probably know nothing about your family’s ‘ agreement .’ They just assigned me to retrieve stolen property.”

Akira stilled, the smugness draining from his face. He looked away, biting his lower lip. His pallor deepened.

“So that’s why… all this time I…” He trailed off, then shook his head, cutting the thought short.

After a moment, he visibly composed himself, the mask sliding back into place.

“Well,” he said, voice hoarse but steadying, “seeing as you’re clearly not afraid to walk the darker paths, perhaps I could convince the family to accept you… if you’re dissatisfied with the life Akito chose. If you wish to return to your roots.”

I remained silent, the offer hanging in the air.

Father had no qualms about throwing away his family for his principles.

Would I really become a criminal—for a family I never even knew, just so I’d have someone to return to?

Just as I was processing Akira’s manipulative offer, a sharp knock sounded at the door. Tadashi entered, glancing from me to Akira, his expression questioning. I stood and met him near the doorway.

“Kitoru talked. I have the box,” Tadashi reported quietly. Relief flickered through me—mission accomplished.

“They were planning to sell it to the Mitsurugi clan. Soon.” He paused, casting a thoughtful glance at Akira. “Are you still planning to let them go?”

I considered Akira’s words about non-interference. Capturing him could ignite a feud we didn’t need, especially with the Mitsurugi potentially involved.

Then again, the clan probably wouldn’t weep over eliminating a rotten thief.

I quickly relayed Akira’s claims about the family pact.

“Forget it,” I said at last, exhaling. “Let’s just leave them and hand off the box. This isn’t our problem.”

Tadashi frowned, studying Akira again. Then he gripped my arm, eyes wide with sudden realization. “Wait. You said he’s your father’s twin? Identical twin ?”

I nodded, confused by his intensity.

Tadashi looked almost floored. “Sadi,” he said slowly, voice hushed with significance, “I think… I think he can free you from the slave seal.”

I blinked. “What? How? You told me it was permanent.”

“It is —normally,” Tadashi said, words quickening with urgency. “But I’ve been researching its structure, the theory behind it. There’s a reference in a Mitsurugi text—a book of dark arts. One obscure method to break a master-slave seal involves a specific ritual… performed by someone from the master’s direct bloodline, with a strong mental and physical connection. Like an identical twin.”

“Hold on.” I raised a hand, frowning. “Since when do you own a Mitsurugi book of dark arts? Are you moonlighting?”

Tadashi flushed beet red, avoiding my eyes. “It’s not like that! I… happened to acquire it during a mission,” he mumbled, clearly unconvincing.

I gave him the look—the one that said ‘I know you stole cookies from the jar and are blaming the dog.’ Tadashi sighed, rolling his eyes.

“Okay, fine. I deliberately sought it out,” he admitted, frustration edging his voice. “You have no idea what I had to do—the informants I had to track, bribe, or serve—just to get my hands on that book. But I haven’t used it for anything else. I swear. I was only looking for a way to break your seal.”

Of course. This was so Tadashi —sneaking around with forbidden clan secrets, all while wearing that innocent face. No wonder the elders found him exasperating. When he wanted something badly enough, he operated entirely in the shadows.

Thinking back, his efforts after Father died—stabilizing the volatile seal, risking his own energy—were already more than enough. Anko had confirmed how dangerous it was, how skillful Tadashi’s intervention had been, how sick he’d gotten afterwards from the strain. He could’ve stopped there. No one would’ve blamed him for accepting the seal’s permanence.

Instead, he’d kept going—determined, relentless—fighting a battle I’d already given up on. Not for praise, but because he thought it was right.

“I don’t want you to die just because I might,” Tadashi added quietly, meeting my gaze, his expression deadly serious.

“Ninjas don’t exactly have long life expectancies. The seal… it links our fates too tightly.” He drew a shallow breath. “I want you to live free from it, Sadi.”

Silence settled between us. An unfamiliar tightness rose in my throat.

No one else knew about the seal—not really. No one understood the constant, invisible weight, the feeling of being fundamentally chained. No one cared. It was my burden, inherited from Father. A life sentence. It had hollowed out my hope years ago.

But Tadashi… he saw it. He understood the bitterness, the cage I lived in. And without a word, he’d been working toward freeing me all along.

I nodded curtly, clearing the tightness from my throat. Emotions could wait. “Okay. What do we need to do?”

“Stay here.” Tadashi stepped toward Akira and initiated a brief, tense negotiation.

Akira listened silently, his expression shifting from surprise to a flicker of something unreadable—concern? Calculation?—before settling into a noncommittal shrug. He agreed to perform the ritual. The condition: we let him and Kitoru walk free afterward. Fair enough.

Tadashi beckoned me over and instructed me to lie flat on the carpet in the center of the room. He positioned the still-bound Akira beside me. Then he secured the door and began placing intricate protection seals along the room’s perimeter. As he activated the final seal, a wave of dizziness crashed over me, and everything went black.

When I came to, Akira was lying unconscious beside me on the carpet. Complex symbols glowed faintly in blood, marring the fabric.

Tadashi was leaning heavily against the desk. He was extinguishing the protection seals one by one. His breathing was short and ragged, one hand pressed tight against his chest. His face was deathly pale. His clothes clung to him with sweat. He looked like he’d gone ten rounds with a demon.

“You have a nosebleed,” I croaked, gesturing toward his face.

He nodded silently and wiped the trickle with a handkerchief already stained dark red. I noticed a fresh, deep cut across his palm.

“Is this all your blood?” I asked, gesturing weakly to the floor.

Tadashi shook his head. He held up two fingers, then pointed toward Akira. I saw a makeshift bandage wrapped around Akira’s left hand. So it was a combination of their blood. The heavy, coppery tang in the air made my stomach churn. Thankfully, as the last seal dissolved, the bloody carvings vanished without a trace.

“How do you feel?” Tadashi asked, his voice raspy. The irony wasn’t lost on me. I should’ve been the one asking him that. “The seal… you don’t feel it anymore, right?”

I focused inward. Took a deep breath. Then another. The constant, low-level throb at the base of my spine was gone. The invisible noose that had constricted my life for twenty-two years had simply vanished. For the first time I could remember, I could breathe without reservation. My body felt impossibly light. My head, startlingly clear.

Freedom . And I owed it to Tadashi. Maybe the closest thing to a brother I had.

“I… I can’t sense you anymore,” I murmured, surprised. “Does that mean our telepathic ability is gone?”

Tadashi stared for a moment, then let out a relieved snort. A trace of dryness returned to his voice.

“Good,” he said. “That means your secrets—and your hypothetical girlfriend’s—are safe from my prying thoughts.”

We looked at each other and exchanged a quick laugh.

We left Akira and Kitoru bound but alive, as promised, and headed to the nearest clan safe house. The jewel box was secured and handed off for transport back to the capital.

While Tadashi finally crashed—utterly spent from the ritual and the journey—I stayed up writing the mission report. Heavily edited, of course.

The next day, feeling lighter than I had in years, we started walking toward my mother’s house. It was only a few hours away. As we approached the familiar gate, a strange mix of hope and long-buried anxiety tightened my chest.

I raised my hand and knocked.
