Chapter 5

← Back to Masamune Clan Series [EN]

“ T adaima. I’m home, Mother,” I said quietly, managing a faint smile as the familiar wooden gate swung open.

Her eyes widened. For a moment, she didn’t move. Then warmth overtook surprise, and she wrapped her arms around me—tightly, without hesitation.

Behind me, Tadashi gave a deep bow.

“Sadi… and… Taka?” she asked, blinking. Of course—she’d known him by that name, back when he worked for her. “Is he working with you now?”

I offered a silent nod.

A pleased look crossed her face. “That’s wonderful news. He was such a reliable young man. Always so polite, always worked hard.”

We didn’t say much more. But her smile lingered.

Dinner was simple. Familiar. Each bite settled something inside me—something I hadn’t even realized had come undone. Later, we sat in the living room, the silence between us soft and undemanding. Eventually, I told her about Father’s death.

Her fingers stilled around her teacup. She looked at me for a long time, searching my face. I didn’t tell her how Father had treated me. I couldn’t burden her with that darkness. Her silence felt like understanding. She knew it was difficult for me.

Her hand found mine.

“You’re still my son, Sadi,” she said, voice low. “Always. You don’t have to ask. This is your home too.”

I nodded, my throat too tight for words.

That night, sleep evaded me. I drifted to the veranda outside the guest room, the cool wood under my feet grounding me. Overhead, the stars were scattered thick across the sky, like dust shaken from some divine hand.

I leaned against a post, arms crossed. I should’ve felt full. I did, technically—Mother’s food had been delicious. But the gnawing ache inside me wasn’t hunger.

It was memory.

Small things rose to the surface. Stupid things.

The way Father used to cook when he had the time. Just once in a while, when work hadn’t consumed him. I always liked helping him on those rare nights. There had been a kind of rhythm to it—the clatter of pans, the soft chopping, the quiet focus. We didn’t talk much. But when we sat down and ate, I’d catch the faint curve of his mouth, the way his shoulders relaxed just slightly.

Back then, I thought: maybe this is what a real father is like.

Even now, those memories refused to vanish. No matter how much I wished they would.

They came with thorns. The training grounds—his voice, sharp and cold, barking corrections. The sudden hand grabbing my wrist too hard. The punishments. The silence. The impossible expectations.

The fear.

And yet, there were still moments. His hand catching me when I stumbled. A nod when I got something right. The briefest flash of pride in his eyes before it vanished again.

They shouldn’t matter. But they did.

I hated that they did.

I pressed my palms against my eyes. I couldn’t go back to that house. Not yet. Maybe not ever. Every part of it still smelled like him. The study. The hallway. Even now, I sometimes thought I heard his voice behind me, calling my name.

I used to check on him at night. I never really knew why. Maybe I just needed to make sure he was still there. I’d wake from a dream—sometimes a nightmare, sometimes just a sense—and I’d tiptoe to the study. He was always awake. Lamp burning low. Sometimes he’d snap. Sometimes he’d sigh and tell me to go back to bed. But sometimes, just sometimes, he’d close the book, rise without a word, and walk me back.

Once, he’d even managed a faint smile and a quiet thanks.

I lived for those nights.

It would be so much easier if I could hate him. If I could bury everything in anger and walk away clean.

But I couldn’t.

He was my father… and I had loved him. A part of me still did. Somewhere under all of it, buried in the quiet places that never quite healed.

His kindness and cruelty tangled into something painful. Something in me fractured when he died.

His absence wasn’t silence. It was a deafening roar in that empty house, crushing my bones. I hated myself for the constant loop of memories. Ashamed of this desperate, lingering need for the man who broke me.

He raised me. Trained me. Held me. Hurt me. Protected me. Beat me .

My hands itched to break something. Make him feel the way I had felt for years. I wanted to scream, but the sound died in my throat.

Because he was gone.

There was no one to scream at anymore.

And what scared me most… was the empty space he left behind.

My hands trembled. I gripped my hair hard enough to hurt. I wanted the pain to focus me. Wanted it to cut through the fog in my chest.

A weight settled over my shoulders.

I froze.

A blanket.

I looked up. Tadashi stood beside me, eyes steady.

He didn’t say anything. Just gave my shoulder a firm, quiet squeeze before sitting down.

No words. No questions. No attempt to fix me.

He just… stayed.

The kind of support I imagined a family might give—solid, steady, unassuming.

And somehow, that was enough.

More than enough.

I stared at the stars again. We sat in silence for a long time. Slowly, the frantic tide inside me ebbed, smoothed by his quiet presence.

Eventually, my body gave in. The weight of everything—the grief, the guilt, the exhaustion—pulled me under.

I don’t remember falling asleep. Only that I woke later, warm beneath the same blanket, the guest room dim with early light, and the world, somehow, just a little less unbearable.

The next day passed in a blur of gentle normalcy. I spent time with my mother. Shizuka, my younger sister, dragged Tadashi and me out for a walk through the woods, chattering the whole way. Later, she insisted on a picnic by the river, managing to rope in both Mother and my stepfather. Surrounded by their easy laughter and genuine acceptance, I felt… anchored. Cared for.

Loved .

Time slipped by—both too slow and too fast. Before I knew it, evening had come. My last night here. Tomorrow, I’d return. I sat on the veranda again, watching the moon rise. Tadashi joined me, setting down a small tea service on the low table between us.

A comfortable silence settled over us, broken only by the quiet chorus of crickets. Tadashi poured the steaming green tea into two cups.

“Drink,” he said gently.

I looked down at the dark liquid, breathing in the fragrant steam. Still too hot to sip. But even the hottest tea eventually cooled.

Heat fades to cold.

Maybe everything does, in time.

Even my feelings for Father.

And in that moment… in the quiet, moonlit yard… far from clan duties and mission reports, a month and a half since he died… clarity settled over me.

Father had beaten me. Tortured me. Shackled me with that cursed seal. Bound me with his will like iron chains. I’d hated him for it. A part of me still did, and always would. I was glad he was gone. That the threat, the control, had died with him.

But beneath the hate, there was something else.

My father was the man who had turned away from a life of crime to forge his own path. The man who worked relentlessly, driven by ruthless ambition. The man who’d pushed me—broken me, yes—but in his own warped way, had tried to prepare me. Tried to build something good for me to inherit.

So that I could out-succeed him. And maybe build a better life where my children wouldn’t have to fight the same battles we did.

My father had loved me—in the only way he knew how.

And I… had loved him, too.

I couldn’t fully hate him. I couldn’t wholly love the memory of him. All I could do was accept him. All of him. The man who raised me. The man whose legacy—ugly and flawed and brilliant—shaped who I was.

I drew in a long breath. Let it out slowly.

Let him go.

I was free now. From the seal. From the past. Maybe even ready to forgive .

Maybe one day.

“You look awfully serious,” Tadashi murmured, breaking the silence. “Don’t tell me you already regret losing the telepathy link.”

I blinked, then a slow smile tugged at my mouth. “Maybe a little. It’s a shame, really. Think how much I could’ve annoyed you—day and night—with completely random thoughts.”

Tadashi groaned dramatically and took a cautious sip of tea.

“Hey!” I nudged him. “I could’ve given you dating advice. Saved you from being so awkward around your girlfriend.”

He choked, sputtering into his cup. I laughed and patted his back until the coughing subsided.

“You know,” I went on, “the more I think about it, the more I regret it. We could’ve passed secret commentary during Elder Low’s endless briefings. Made bets on who could keep a straight face the longest.”

Tadashi snorted. “And get latrine duty with a toothbrush when we lost? No, thank you.”

“Hah! Coward. Where’s your sense of adventure?”

He just shook his head, grinning. I sighed, content, and lifted my cup. Still hot, but drinkable. I took a slow sip.

“Anyway… thanks,” I said softly. The words felt too small. Tadashi hadn’t just helped retrieve a box or break a seal. He’d given me space. Quiet support. A safe place to fall apart and put myself back together. An anchor that silenced the noise in my head.

He’d taken care of me. Not out of duty, but because he was being a good friend. And I was deeply grateful.

Thank you for being here.

Thank you for being my friend.

Thank you for saving me, in more ways than one.

Thank you for everything.

I didn’t say it aloud, but when Tadashi met my gaze, I had a feeling he understood anyway.

“You’re welcome, Sadi,” he said. He smiled as he took another sip of tea. “I’ve had a good week indeed.”

Back at the clan compound, routine hit like a tidal wave. My schedule filled in an instant—missions, briefings, endless reports. I barely saw Tadashi. The New Year’s Festival loomed—a major security operation under our clan’s watch, a mission straight from the King. The pressure was brutal.

The night before deployment, I returned to my quarters late and found a letter on my desk. A single stick of colorful cotton candy. And a small, insulated pot of still-warm tea.

Sadi,

I wanted to share the pre-mission tea with you, but it looks like time’s not on our side.

It’s a big day tomorrow. Let’s do our best. If I can’t be there for Shiro, I trust you to protect her.

Forgive me for my shortcomings. Thank you—for everything.

May the spirits watch over you.

—T.

I paused, caught off guard by the gesture.

The pre-battle tea… a ritual usually reserved for close family. A quiet moment to pray, make peace, and accept whatever lay ahead.

I sat down, alone, in the silence of my room. Slowly drank the tea. Let the cotton candy melt on my tongue.

A faint smile tugged at my mouth.

Thank you, brother. For treating me like family.

Outside, the night was warm. The moon, full and luminous. Nothing felt wrong.

But I should’ve known better. Should’ve seen the heaviness behind Tadashi’s quiet eyes. Should’ve read between the lines of his letter—not as formality, but as the farewell it truly was.

If I had realized that was the last message he’d ever send me…

I would’ve torn the compound apart to find him.

And made him sit down and drink that tea with me.
