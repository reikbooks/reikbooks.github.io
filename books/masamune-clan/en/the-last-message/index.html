The Last Message

Book 7 of Masamune Clan Series

A fateful mission. A face from the past. The meaning of unconditional love.

Adrift like a broken kite, Tadashi is lost when the elders strip him of his title as Clan Heir. But destiny brings him face-to-face with someone from the past, who grounds him once more. However, for every hope fulfilled, a heavy price must be paid.

R ain lashed against Tadashi’s back as he stood motionless before his mother’s grave. The storm raged around him, but he was numb to its fury. His gaze fixed on the headstone, watching raindrops cascade over the carved inscription, tracing the familiar curves of her name.

It had been six months since his last visit—six missed pilgrimages, a betrayal of the ritual he’d upheld for years. No matter the storms, no matter the injuries, he’d always found a way to come. But this year, the clan elders had tightened their leash on him, sending him farther and farther from home, until he felt like a stranger to his own life.

Tonight’s visit was a bitter homecoming. Yesterday, they’d released him from the punishment room, stripped of his position as clan heir. His father’s face, carved with disappointment, still burned in his memory. No words had been exchanged—just a quick turn of the head, unable to bear the sight of his eldest son.

Shame, hot and sharp, pierced his chest. The rejection cut deeper than any blade. His fingers curled into his palm. Had his neglect of her memory brought this upon him? If his mother were alive, would she look at him the same way?

Tadashi closed his eyes, his chest tightening as memories of her flooded his mind. Every time he accomplished a task, she would hug him close. He could almost feel her arms around him.

“You must never give up, Tadashi,” she’d whispered, her last words before she passed.

Tadashi sank to his knees, the damp earth soaking through his clothes. His trembling hand traced the carvings on the cold marble headstone.

“I haven’t given up, Mother,” he whispered, but the words felt hollow.

The truth was, he had sacrificed all he had for his duty. He’d never refused a task, never argued, never made excuses. Not a single word of complaint had escaped his lips, even as the weight of their expectations threatened to crush him, each breath a struggle. He was drowning, buried alive beneath the weight of their demands.

And still, it wasn’t enough. Now, they’d discarded him like a broken tool—useless, unwanted. He didn’t know what else he could do to earn his clan’s acceptance. He wasn’t even sure if he had anything left to give.

He’d already given them everything.

“Coward!” Shiro’s voice cut through his thoughts, sharp and furious. “Don’t act like you’re trying to protect me from the clan’s demands!”

Because of his failure, his younger sister now bore the weight of becoming the next clan head. He’d tried to help her, to ease her burden, but she’d thrown his offer back in his face.

“Enough! I don’t want your empty words. You’ve trapped me in this position, but I won’t become like you—someone who brought shame to Father.”

Her words twisted like a knife in his chest. He’d never imagined his family would look at him with such contempt.

“You must be strong, Tadashi,” his mother’s voice whispered again.

The rain intensified, a relentless torrent. Tadashi didn’t wipe the water from his face. Instead, he pressed his forehead against the unyielding stone, seeking strength, seeking solace. But there was nothing. Only a haunting ache.

Darkness. A void.

His heart felt like churned-up graveyard soil, trampled and ruined.

“Protect your siblings, Tadashi,” she’d once said.

He bit his lip until he tasted blood. He tried his best, but they often misunderstood.

“You’re pathetic, you know that?!” Raito, his youngest brother, had screamed during an argument. “I’m not surprised Mother was so disappointed in you!”

He always got everything wrong. He was never good enough—not for the clan, not for Father, not for his siblings, not for Mother.

“I love you, Tadashi,” her embrace, a distant memory, felt like a lifetime ago…

He was worthless. He didn’t deserve her love.

Tadashi’s fingers dug into the wet earth beside her grave. He whispered apologies into the driving rain, knowing they were lost to the wind. The grave remained silent. The rain was deaf to his pleas. The night offered no answers.

He slumped against the headstone, staring into the pitch-black sky. Soaked to the bone, frozen to the core, he remained still and silent as the night bled into a bleak, unforgiving dawn.

A sharp slap jolted him violently back to the present. Back to the clan’s meeting room, two hours after sunrise.

As soon as Tadashi had entered the house, Raito had informed him that their father had summoned him to the main hall. Still numb, he simply nodded, changed out of his rain-soaked clothes, and complied.

Elder Mei was already outlining a new mission when his focus began to drift. Shiro stood beside her, listening. She was the one who had slapped him.

“Tadashi!” Elder Mei’s voice cracked like a whip. “Are you with us?”

He lifted his gaze, struggling to gather his scattered thoughts. He had to concentrate. Elder Mei had shown mercy by allowing him to serve the clan again. This was what he had always wanted, wasn’t it?

To be a dutiful son. To prove his worth. He wouldn’t fail them again.

“Yes, Elder Mei,” he murmured, his voice hoarse and faint. His head throbbed, and his cheek burned where Shiro had struck him.

“You’re not focused,” Shiro snapped. “Elder Mei, he isn’t ready. He needs more time to recover.”

Tadashi shook his head, catching himself swaying slightly. He had endured two full days of intense physical training as ordered. “I’m fine—”

“If you’re not up to it, say so now,” Elder Mei cut in, her voice firm. “The royal family specifically requested you, but if you’re unfit, we can assign another Level A operative.”

“I can do it, Elder Mei. Please, give me this chance. I won’t let you down.”

Elder Mei scrutinized him for a long moment, then finally nodded her assent.

When the briefing concluded, Shiro escorted him to the door. Gripping his arm tightly, she shoved him out. Tadashi winced as his shoulder crashed into a wooden pillar.

“You have a fever,” she hissed, her gaze glacial. “Take some medicine, rest, and eat before you leave. Don’t fail this mission because you’re being stubborn.”

Get yourself together. You have work to do.

Tadashi swallowed hard. “Yes, Shiro,” he whispered, a mix of gratitude, shame, and hurt churning in his stomach. Shiro slammed the door without another word.

The late afternoon sun cast long shadows as Tadashi departed. His mission: retrieve the Celestial Pearl, an ancient relic lost for generations. A fisherman had stumbled upon it two days prior, jutting from the crumbling coastal wall of an ancient temple. Now, the royal family needed it back.

The Mitsurugi Clan, however, had intercepted the fisherman, seizing the Pearl. Their intent: to use it in a ritual at Jikan Temple on the full moon—this coming Friday. Fearful of an impending catastrophe, the royal family had tasked Tadashi with stopping them.

He arrived at the designated location, a border region of the kingdom. Near the temple lay a small, isolated village called Fufuku, long under the control of the Mitsurugi Clan.

From his observations, the village kept to itself, harboring secrets behind closed doors and suspicious glances. Mitsurugi guards maintained regular patrols, their presence growing stronger as Friday approached.

Jikan Temple itself was a ruin, a hollowed-out husk of its former glory. Tadashi had already infiltrated its grounds, finding nothing but the ghost of an altar beneath a gaping hole in the dilapidated roof. Moss choked the stone, and half the walls had crumbled, neglected for ages.

Friday night arrived, cloaked in anticipation. Since noon, Tadashi had been concealed within the sprawling branches of an ancient banyan tree behind the temple. As dusk settled, guards took up positions around the perimeter, their movements precise and practiced.

Then, they came. A procession of figures draped in white robes, their hair as white as bone, their eyes the color of ash. The Mitsurugi Clan leaders had arrived.

Tadashi’s breath caught as he recognized one of them: Seishiro.

Behind him strode a middle-aged man, built like a mountain, his long white hair cascading down his back like a frozen waterfall. Thin, cunning eyes, reminiscent of a seasoned general, scanned their surroundings.

Beside him, a lean young man, likely younger than Seishiro, moved with the coiled energy of a predator. He wore a white jacket over a red turtleneck, dark sunglasses hiding his eyes. His longish hair was pulled back into a single ponytail, bangs obscuring his face. He moved with the alertness of a bodyguard, his gaze a constant, sweeping searchlight.

Upon entering the temple, Seishiro reached inside his jacket and produced a necklace. From it hung a large pearl, the color of cream with a reddish hue. It pulsed with an inner light, a soft, ethereal glow that seemed to breathe.

The Celestial Pearl.

Tadashi’s muscles coiled, ready to strike.

Seishiro placed the pearl in a ceremonial bowl, presenting it to the general. As the older man began to chant, an unnatural stillness descended.

Without warning, lightning split the cloudless sky. The full moon emerged through a sudden parting in the heavens, flooding the temple grounds with silvery light. A vortex of wind materialized, swirling with increasing fury.

Tadashi clung to his perch, muscles taut, bracing himself against the sudden gale.

The lean bodyguard stepped forward, kneeling before the general. Head bowed, he extended his right hand. The ritual knife flashed, opening his palm. Seishiro caught the crimson flow in the bowl with the pearl. As the blood touched it, the pearl flared, an otherworldly light pulsed from within.

Above, the light of the full moon began to shift, gradually bleeding into a deep, unsettling crimson.

Tadashi’s grip tightened on his kusarigama. Now or never!

He triggered the smoke bombs planted around the temple’s perimeter. As thick smoke billowed, obscuring the scene, Tadashi dropped from the tree, a shadow flitting through the chaos. He slipped past Seishiro and snatched the pearl from the bowl.

A grunt of pain escaped his lips.

The general’s kick, swift and brutal, slammed into his solar plexus. The force of it nearly ripped the pearl from his grasp. Tadashi quickly recovered his balance. He weaved through a barrage of strikes, his movements pure instinct.

Punch. Parry. Punch. Dodge. Kick!

The general deflected Tadashi’s counterattack, seized his elbow, and slammed him to the ground.

“AAH!”

A searing agony ripped through Tadashi’s left arm as the general twisted and wrenched it from its socket with a sickening pop. Still, Tadashi refused to let go of the pearl.

Gritting his teeth against the white-hot fire in his arm, he lashed out, kicking the general’s knee as hard as he could. A satisfying crack echoed in the smoky air as the general staggered.

He had to escape. Now!

Tadashi sprang to his feet, hurling his kusarigama through the hole in the roof. The chain wrapped around a sturdy branch as smoke thickened around them.

Escape was within reach—but the general slashed the ritual knife across Tadashi’s forearm.

When his blood dripped onto the pearl, a shockwave exploded. A blinding red light enveloped Tadashi, pulling and stretching him in all directions. Pain, raw and consuming, ripped through his veins.

His scream was swallowed by the roar of the vortex that now raged within the temple. His body twisted upward, helpless as a leaf in a hurricane. The sky above split open, a void of absolute darkness. The wind hurled him into the abyss.

Static electricity crackled. A tangible energy pinched his skin as he passed through an unseen veil in the sky. Then, gravity abruptly yanked him down. He plummeted into a freefall, the world a blur of darkness and rushing wind.

T ossed through the air, Tadashi crashed through the trees like a broken arrow, branches snapping beneath him. The ground rushed up, and his dislocated arm took the full impact, sending a searing, white-hot agony through him that obliterated everything else.

He wasn’t sure what woke him. Consciousness returned in fragments. First, the pain—everywhere, a wail of pain that made even breathing feel like swallowing shards of glass.

It hurts… God, it hurts…

His thoughts scattered like startled birds, refusing to settle, to make sense. There was something he was supposed to do. Something important. But his head throbbed, and the thought remained just out of reach.

A gentle voice seeped into his awareness. “Shh… relax. Can you hear me?”

His vision swam, resolving into the blurry outline of a woman with long white hair, dressed in a black kimono.

“You collapsed in my backyard last night. I’ve done what I could for your wounds.”

Tadashi stared at her, struggling to form coherent thoughts.

“Are you able to speak?”

He hesitated. When he nodded, sharp jolts of pain shot through his body. He bit his lip to stifle the groan, but a strangled sob escaped.

“My name is Chiyo, the caretaker of Jikan Temple. What’s your name?”

Jikan Temple? The name triggered something… Aah! Tadashi squeezed his eyes shut. Jikan… The name left a trail of dread, but he couldn’t remember why.

Was I on a mission? What happened? Panic fluttered in his chest as he tried to piece together the fragmented memories. Did I get caught? Is she an enemy?

Her patient gaze remained on his face, waiting for the answer he’d almost forgotten she’d asked. His name. She wanted his name.

My name is Tadashi. No. If this was an interrogation… “I’m… Taka,” he rasped. The lie scraped against his dry, cracked throat.

Chiyo gently wiped his tears away. “Rest now… you’re safe here.”

The next time Tadashi awoke, fever racked him in violent shivers. Cold radiated through his limbs, though his skin felt aflame. Every joint ached, his muscles spasmed.

Someone gently stroked his forehead, then carefully lifted his chin to help him swallow medicine. The liquid was cool and soothing as it slid down his parched throat.

Mother?

Tadashi blinked, yearning to see his mother’s face. He must have finished training, gotten caught in the rain, fallen ill. Mother always cared for him with such tenderness. He wanted to reach for her hand, but his throbbing arms hung limp, useless. His strength had abandoned him.

He drifted between consciousness and fevered dreams. A gentle presence remained constant, soothing him with soft touches. Once, through bleary eyes, he saw her tidying the room, mopping the wooden floors. Tadashi frowned.

Mother shouldn’t be working so hard… not while carrying Raito.

The name surfaced from deep within his memories. Yes, Mother said they would name his brother Raito. Father had told Tadashi he was old enough to help with the chores, to ease his mother’s burden. But his body felt so heavy, a lead weight pinning him to the bed.

The aroma of tofu soup roused him next, his stomach clenching with hunger. How long since he’d eaten? Ah, but Mother should be resting, not cooking. He could surely manage to eat on his own.

When the woman tried to feed him, Tadashi turned away and tried to push aside the spoon, but she persisted with patient gentleness. As the savory soup touched his tongue and filled his rumbling stomach, tears welled up in his eyes.

How many years since he’d tasted his mother’s soup? Why was he burdening her now, when they’d finally reunited?

Mother, please forgive me…

His heart constricted with grief as another memory surfaced: “Don’t cry! You don’t deserve to cry!” Shiro pummeled him as he curled into himself, defenseless. “Mother died because of you! You killed Mother!”

No. No. NO!

“Mother!”

Tadashi bolted upright, agony shooting through his shoulder and arm. Gentle hands steadied him as he swayed, a soft voice murmuring comfort.

“Mother, I-I’m sorry… I—” The words caught in his throat, choked by the magnitude of his failures. If his mother knew everything… would she ever forgive him? Would she hate him, as his father, Raito, and Shiro had come to hate him? The thought was a cold, suffocating hand around his heart.

The woman tenderly wiped his tears away, her touch achingly familiar. “Why are you apologizing, son?”

This was it—the moment had arrived. Mother wanted to hear him confess all his mistakes. And then what? She would surely despise him too.

Because… Because I’m the reason you’re gone. It was the thought he could never say aloud.

Tadashi lowered his gaze, vision swimming with tears. “Your last wish… that I protect Shiro and Raito… I-I haven’t been able to do it properly.”

The words were a heavy weight, dragging him down. Silence followed, thick and deafening. The woman released her hold, the absence of her touch a fresh wound. Of course. Why would his mother want to embrace him? He hadn’t done his job. He was a failure who didn’t deserve her love. Tadashi bit his lip, trying to suppress the sharp pain.

“Forgive me, Mother,” he pleaded, his voice a mere whisper. He squeezed his eyes shut, bracing for the blow. “Everything… it’s all my fault. I’m so sorry.”

The woman remained silent. Each beat of silence shattered Tadashi’s heart further.

“B-but I won’t give up, Mother… I-I’ll be strong. S-stronger. I’ll keep protecting them, just as you wished…,” he whispered, yearning for her to say something, anything. His voice trailed off as he slowly lifted his gaze, seeking forgiveness. But what he saw made him gasp, his breath catching in his throat.

The woman’s shoulders shook with silent sobs, her arms wrapped tightly around herself as tears streamed down her face. Tadashi froze, horror crashing through him. What had he done? He had made his mother cry!

“M-mother, please don’t cry.” His voice trembled. He reached toward her despite the pain in his shoulder. “I’ll follow your wish, I swear it. Don’t worry—I’ll take care of our family. I’ll try harder, I promise… I-I love you, Mother. Forgive me. Please—”

“Oh, dear one—” The woman reached out gently, her hand cupping his face.

Tadashi struggled to breathe, his lungs constricting. He had said it. He had admitted his failures to his mother, laid bare his shame. And Mother… hadn’t condemned him. She was still here, wasn’t she? She hadn’t abandoned him. She didn’t say that she forgave him, but she also didn’t reject or punish him. That was enough. It was more than he deserved. Perhaps… perhaps everything could be alright.

A long, shuddering breath escaped Tadashi’s lips. A feeling of coolness from her hand seeped into his mind, soothing the jagged edges of his pain.

“Thank you for always doing your best,” she whispered, her voice thick. “I will always love you.”

Consciousness returned like a gentle tide. When Tadashi awoke again, it was as if surfacing from a long, deep sleep. The throbbing in his arm and shoulder persisted. Weakness clung to his limbs, but his fever had broken. The pounding in his head had subsided to a dull ache. He blinked up at the unfamiliar ceiling, the events of the past few days a jumbled mess in his mind.

Had he been hallucinating during his fever? He could have sworn his mother had visited and spoken with him. But that was impossible—she was gone. Just fevered dreams born from his pain, though they had left him with an inexplicable sense of mixed peace and longing. Focus, Tadashi. Don’t get ahead of yourself. Your mission is still unfinished. He drew a shaky breath and pushed aside the lingering emotions.

The events crystallized slowly: retrieving the Celestial Pearl from the Mitsurugi Clan, the chaos that followed, being flung through space to land at Chiyo’s home. The caretaker of Jikan Temple had tended his wounds with a mother’s gentleness—perhaps that explained his dreams.

His chest tightened with guilt. He needed to properly thank and repay her kindness. But first, the pearl. He’d been clutching it when he fell, but where was it now? The Mitsurugi Clan hadn’t found him yet, despite his days of vulnerability. Perhaps Chiyo’s house was far from the temple grounds? The thought sparked hope.

Tadashi took a careful breath and then slowly eased himself upright, biting back a groan as pain flared in his shoulder. Chiyo had done her best with his shoulder, resetting the dislocation and stabilizing it, but the deep ache suggested a fracture in his left arm. The mirror across the room reflected his sorry state—bruises blooming across his skin, wounds wrapped in careful bandages. His stomach churned at the sight.

Gripping the nearby table for support, he forced himself to stand. His legs trembled beneath him and the room swayed. As his vision steadied, he scanned his surroundings, searching for any sign of the pearl’s distinctive glow.

The house was small and cozy. Sunlight filtered through the window, illuminating a quiet village road and distant homes nestled among the trees. The peaceful scene belied Tadashi’s growing unease. On a nearby table, medicine cups and herbal powder jars stood in neat rows beside his empty drinking glass.

A small picture frame caught his eye—a photograph of a young child with a familiar smile. This must be her child’s room , he thought. Yet something felt off. No toys scattered about, no drawings pinned to walls. Just pristine emptiness.

Venturing further, he found himself in a kitchen showing signs of neglect—unwashed dishes piled high, laundry heaped in corners, dust gathering on the floor. A pang of guilt stabbed him. Poor Chiyo, struggling to maintain her home while carrying a child and tending to a wounded stranger.

The master bedroom door stood slightly ajar. Inside, Chiyo slept peacefully. Could she have found the pearl? The thought spurred him to action.

With silent apologies to his generous host, Tadashi began a systematic search—every drawer, every closet, every shadowed corner. Nothing.

But something puzzled him in the study, a room filled with the scent of old paper and dried ink. He frowned at a desk calendar sitting beside a small pot of cactus. The date was seventeen years in the past. Surely a mistake. But nearby newspapers confirmed the same impossible year.

With trembling hands, he opened the desk drawer. A brown diary sealed with delicate ribbon lay within. He refrained from opening it, picking up a sheet of paper lying on top instead, as if someone had hurriedly placed it there. The letter was only half-written, a request for maternity leave addressed to the temple’s administration. The date matched the calendar.

Tadashi slowly closed the drawer. A sudden wave of dizziness swept over him as the pieces clicked into place. The Mitsurugi Clan’s absence suddenly made sense. The Fufuku village was nestled against Jikan Temple’s weathered walls. The Mitsurugi would have launched an extensive search there. Chiyo was nursing him in her child’s room, not in any secret chamber or hidden cellar. He would surely have been discovered. But they hadn’t found him, because he wasn’t there anymore.

The Celestial Pearl hadn’t just moved him through space—it had hurled him through time itself. He had crash landed in the village of Fufuku, seventeen years into the past.

D isoriented and reeling from the shock, Tadashi decided it was best to wait for Chiyo to wake up. She could confirm what year it was. More importantly, she might know where the pearl had gone when she found him. If he was indeed in the past, there was nothing he could do without finding that pearl.

Tadashi forced himself to move. Every step rattled his battered body, sending jolts of pain through his weary frame. The sharp throb in his shoulder threatened to unsteady him, but he gritted his teeth, pushing past the pain. He couldn’t just sit there in a cloud of shock and despair, sponging off a stranger’s kindness. The least he could do was make himself useful to repay her generosity, however small the gesture.

He went to the kitchen. One-handed, he tackled the pile of housework, though each movement sent fresh waves of pain through his injured arm. His one good arm ached from the unaccustomed strain, but he set about tidying the cluttered hut, mindful not to disturb Chiyo or displace anything precious. He swept, dusted, and straightened. By the time he finished, he was drenched in sweat, his breathing shallow and labored, but the space felt noticeably cleaner. Chiyo remained fast asleep, probably exhausted from tending to him.

When the sun hovered just past its peak, cutting warm streaks of light across the walls, a sudden noise from the bedroom jolted him. He’d just finished cooking rice and brewing tea.

Tadashi stepped closer and found Chiyo awake, her eyes flicking wildly around the room as though lost in some waking nightmare. She sat upright, trembling.

“I’m here,” Tadashi said softly, steadying her with a hand on her arm as she lurched unsteadily to her feet. Her skin was pale and clammy beneath his touch. He guided her carefully to the dining area.

Once she was seated comfortably, Tadashi knelt before her, pressing his forehead to the wooden floor. His right hand trembled slightly, his shoulder throbbing in protest. “Lady Chiyo, I owe you my life. May God repay your kindness a thousandfold.”

“Oh, stop that,” Chiyo chided, concern creasing her brow. “You’re white as paper, and here you are, working and bowing. Please, just call me Chiyo.”

Tadashi hesitated, then nodded, keeping his eyes low, his gaze fixed on the floor. “As you wish… Chiyo.”

Steam curled from the cups as Tadashi poured their tea. He felt Chiyo’s gaze linger on the mark that had been seared into his chest last year, a brand that spoke of ownership and servitude.

“Are you—” Chiyo began, her tone careful, almost hesitant. Her expression was guarded. “… a runaway slave?”

Heat crept up his neck as Tadashi lowered his head.

“No. My master sent me to return a relic to the King, but I was ambushed on the way,” he answered softly. Every word tasted bitter on his tongue. Lying came as second nature to him these days, and he’d long since learned how to spin truth into half-truths when cornered.

He hesitated, then pressed on. “Forgive my boldness, Chiyo… but when you found me… was there a pearl necklace? The pearl would have been large, like a marble.”

Chiyo shook her head and pulled out an iron pendant she wore around her neck, showing that no pearl was attached. “You fell from a tree in my garden. It was a full moon, bright enough to see clearly, but I found no pearl.”

She offered to search the grounds and inquire at the market, but returned hours later empty-handed. Tadashi was shocked to see her tear-streaked face, her hair matted with dirt and grime. Someone had thrown rotten eggs on her, the foul stench clinging to her clothes. He apologized for the trouble he was causing her, his heart sinking with guilt.

“It’s not your fault,” she said, her voice brittle. A bitter smile crossed her face. The villagers believed she was pregnant out of wedlock. The father was actually her lawful husband, but one day he just disappeared without a trace.

Two years later, she discovered that he was a spy from another clan. Once his mission ended, he left without a word. Chiyo didn’t want the villagers or the Mitsurugi to know his true identity, so she had remained silent, enduring their scorn and judgment.

“They already took my first child,” she continued, voice dropping to a whisper. “He was only eight when the Mitsurugi soldiers came. If they learned his father was a traitor…” She shuddered. “The things they’d do to him…”

Before he could think better of it, Tadashi asked, “Is your child’s name Seishiro?”

Chiyo’s head snapped up. “No. Do you know Seishiro?” Her eyes narrowed. “He’s with the Mitsurugi forces—only nineteen, but his mother’s quite wealthy. Is he your master?”

Unprepared, Tadashi fumbled for an answer. In seventeen years, Seishiro would be the head of the Mitsurugi Clan’s child trafficking operations, but to suspect Chiyo as his mother… Tadashi swallowed hard. He should’ve thought it through.

“No, he’s not my master. I only know him by reputation,” he said, carefully choosing his words. “His name is… well-known.”

Chiyo’s brow furrowed, her suspicion shifting to concern. She studied him in silence, her gaze softening with quiet understanding.

“What about your family? Where are your parents?” Chiyo asked, her voice soft.

Tadashi hesitated at the question. How much honesty could he afford? How much did he even want to confront?

“My mother is dead,” he said, the words a familiar ache in his heart. “My father and siblings… they survive.”

“Why not run home?” she asked after a pause. Her gaze dropped, almost absently, to the scars littering his body. “Do they know about your… situation? Surely your father would help.”

Tadashi’s throat tightened painfully, and he looked away, unable to meet her gaze. “My master outranks him,” he replied, the lie coming smooth but hollow. “Running would only bring punishment to my family. I couldn’t bear the shame.”

“These scars…” Chiyo gestured. “Your master abused you.”

Tadashi forced a thin smile. “It’s alright. My mistakes earned these. Better me than my siblings.”

Something softened in Chiyo’s expression. “You must love them deeply.”

“More than anything,” he whispered, though the words twisted like knives in his chest. He would give his life for them, even though they might not do the same for him.

“Rest,” Chiyo said finally. “Stay until you’re healed. The pearl can wait.”

Days melted into weeks as Tadashi fell into a gentle rhythm with Chiyo. Her pregnancy had begun taking its toll, and he found purpose in easing her burden. Each morning, he rose before dawn to complete the heavier chores, careful to remain hidden from prying eyes.

The backyard became his sanctuary. Protected by tall hedges, he could work freely—tending the garden, hanging laundry, planting vegetables that would sustain them through winter. Sometimes, he caught himself humming while working, a forgotten habit from childhood.

“You have a nice voice,” Chiyo remarked one day, making him flush. She never pressed when he grew quiet, yet somehow understood the weight he carried. Unlike the judgment in Shiro’s eyes or his father’s stern disappointment, Chiyo’s presence felt like a balm. Chiyo treated him like a son, especially since she missed her first child, who hadn’t been home in four years.

“It feels like forever,” she confessed one evening. “I wonder if he still remembers my face.”

Tadashi’s heart ached. He offered what comfort he could through small gestures: a cup of tea, a gentle massage for her swollen feet, an attentive ear.

They grew closer by the day. Chiyo liked to talk with him. She listened to his words and seemed to understand his unspoken thoughts. In a short time, Tadashi felt as though he could confide anything in her. Well, that wasn’t completely accurate, but he felt truly seen and understood. No one had ever known so much about him. He felt loved by Chiyo with all her heart. He had started to think of Chiyo as his own mother. Being near Chiyo always brought him a sense of peace.

That evening, as autumn’s chill crept through the house, they sat together in the living room. Tadashi folded laundry while Chiyo knitted, the soft click of her needles a soothing rhythm. Without ceremony, she handed him a bundle—a hat, scarf, and gloves, all carefully crafted in deep blue wool.

“So you can stay warm when winter comes,” she said simply, her smile making his throat tight.

Tadashi couldn’t explain the warmth that swelled in his chest. He ran his fingers over the precise stitches, overcome. In his past life, he had rarely received gifts from his family. They usually came with conditions or expectations. But this… this was different. Pure. Undemanding. He didn’t know how he could ever repay her kindness.

Chiyo must have noticed his expression because she reached over to pat his hand.

“You shouldn’t dwell on sad things,” said Chiyo with a soft laugh. “Smile once in a while. I’d love to see you smile.”

Tadashi ducked his head, his cheeks heated. A small, shy smile touched the corners of his lips as he whispered, “Thank you.”

Just as Tadashi’s strength returned—his shoulder healing, bandages removed—fate intervened. Chiyo went into labor.

When she refused a midwife, panic seized him. Following her calm instructions despite trembling hands, he helped deliver the child. The baby emerged tiny and perfect, his first cry piercing the dawn silence.

Chiyo wept as she cradled her son, pressing tender kisses to his forehead. The pure love in her eyes stirred something deep in Tadashi’s chest, making departure impossible. She needed him now more than ever.

For two weeks, he threw himself into caring for both mother and child. He learned to swaddle, to change cloth diapers, to recognize different cries. Sometimes, in quiet moments when he held the sleeping infant, Tadashi felt an unfamiliar peace.

“I’m sorry, Chiyo,” he murmured one evening, bouncing the fussy baby while she tried to eat. “He’s hungry—looking for you.”

Chiyo’s soft laugh warmed the room as she quickly finished her meal. Taking the infant to nurse, she studied Tadashi for a while.

“What was your name before, Taka? Before your master named you.”

The question caught him off-guard. Something in her gentle gaze made lying feel impossible.

“I’m going to name him after you,” she declared, smiling.

Tadashi’s chest tightened. “Please, don’t. My real name…” He swallowed hard. “That name brings no fortune.”

“Your manners, your kindness—your parents raised you well.”

“Thank you, but I’m undeserving,” he whispered, bowing his head.

“I want him to grow up loving his older brother as deeply as you love your siblings,” Chiyo said softly, tears gleaming in her eyes.

Tadashi swallowed hard, burying the ache her words awakened. If only she knew how his siblings viewed him now—not as their protector, but as their family’s greatest shame. Still, he reached out to squeeze her hand and smiled.

“I’m sure he would.”

“Will your master punish you if you don’t find the pearl?” Chiyo asked one evening after dinner, her voice soft against the quiet of the sleeping house.

Tadashi’s hands stilled over the teapot, steam curling between his fingers. The question he’d been avoiding crashed over him like a wave.

Nearly a month had passed—here, in the past. If he managed to find the pearl and return to his time, the clan would surely punish him for his absence. But if he failed… execution awaited not just him, but potentially his entire family. His father. Shiro. Everyone he’d sworn to protect.

He needed the pearl. Needed to confront Seishiro about the time travel ritual—assuming he even knew how it worked. Assuming he’d help rather than kill Tadashi on sight. The chances of success seemed impossibly small. By now, his family could already be—

His chest constricted. Once again, he’d brought nothing but shame and suffering to those he loved. He was truly useless.

Warm fingers wrapped around his trembling hand, pulling him from the spiral of self-loathing. When he looked up, Chiyo’s eyes held such understanding that it nearly broke him. She saw through his silence, through the weight crushing his shoulders.

“Do you want to go home?”

The truth caught in his throat. He wanted to stay, to keep this gentle peace he’d found. But that would be cowardice—running from his responsibilities while others paid the price. His desires didn’t matter. They had never really mattered. Only his duty remained.

Tadashi managed a slight nod, unable to speak past the tightness in his chest.

Chiyo drew a shaky breath. When he met her gaze, tears shimmered in her eyes.

“I will send you home.” Her voice quavered but held firm. “I will send you back to the future.”
