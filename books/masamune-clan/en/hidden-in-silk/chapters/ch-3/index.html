Chapter 3

← Back to Masamune Clan Series [EN]

T he Western ridge smelled of plum blossoms and ink.

Lord Fuku’s estate rose in tiers above the lotus pond district, white walls pristine, roofs curved like unfolding scrolls. Where the Fuma compound crouched like a hawk, all hard lines and training yards, this place seemed to float: manicured pines, raked gravel, koi gliding in still ponds.

Saki adjusted the plain grey kimono Koji had insisted on—no clan colors, nothing to mark her as Fuma.

Pretty. Useless in a fight.

The thought flickered and faded as she followed the servant along the stone path to the main house. The servant slid open a door and bowed low.

“Princess Fuma. Lord Fuku awaits.”

Inside, the receiving room was airy and elegant, tatami unblemished, lacquer gleaming. A calligraphy scroll hung in the alcove: a single, bold character for “Loyalty.”

A man knelt before the low table.

He was older than her father by a few years, perhaps—hair threaded with silver and bound neatly, beard trimmed close. His silk robe was understated but expensive, patterns of cranes and waves woven so fine they were more suggestion than display. His eyes, when he lifted them to her, were reddened at the corners, the skin beneath them shadowed.

“Princess Saki Fuma,” he said, inclining his head. “You have my gratitude for coming.”

Saki bowed deeply, forehead almost to the floor. “It is an honor, Lord Fuku. I regret the circumstances.”

He gestured gracefully. “Sit. We have much to discuss and little time before the household grows curious.”

She folded herself onto the cushion opposite him, hands resting on her knees. A servant poured tea and withdrew, the door whispering shut behind her.

For a moment, they sat in silence, the only sound the faint clink of porcelain as Fuku set his cup down untouched.

“My daughter, Kiku,” he said at last. “You know her situation?”

“Only the basics, my lord.” Saki kept her tone neutral. “Married to General Guhdo last year. Her letters ceased three months ago.”

His jaw tightened at Guhdo’s name. “Yes. An auspicious match, they called it. A powerful general to shield a lower-ranked house. A dutiful daughter to warm his bed.” His mouth curved in something that wasn’t quite a smile. “We tell ourselves these stories so duty tastes less like ash.”

Saki stayed silent. She didn’t need him to explain how noble daughters were spent like coin. He reached to the side and lifted a small lacquered box onto the table. When he slid the lid aside, the faint smell of old ink rose. Inside, letters lay bundled in silk ribbons, neat and precise.

“I kept every one,” he said, voice soft. “She wrote weekly at first. Long, rambling things about the weather, the servants, how drafty the general’s estate is in winter.” His fingers hovered above the top bundle but didn’t quite touch. “Then… they changed.”

He untied the ribbon with care and selected one, unfolding the paper to face her. “May I?”

“Please.”

He cleared his throat.

“ Within painted walls

the crane folds her wings in fear—

tea grows cold untouched.

Laughter hides the wolf’s sharp teeth,

drums drown out a daughter’s plea. ”

The poem hung in the air between them, brittle and bright.

Saki frowned slightly. “That’s…not exactly a housekeeping report.”

Fuku’s mouth twisted. “At first, the poems were light. Seasonal. Plum blossoms and moonlight. But Kiku always loved wordplay. She began…layering.” He slid the letter closer, tapping the characters for crane , wolf , drums . “In our family, the crane is code for a woman bound by duty. The wolf for danger in human form. Drums for war—or rowdy company.”

“So she was frightened,” Saki said quietly. “Of something—or someone—in that house.”

He nodded once. “Guhdo holds frequent feasts. Victorious campaigns, visiting dignitaries, any pretext will do. Kiku wrote of…guests. Men with daimyo crests who should not meet in private. Foreigners with no court sanction. Priests whose sutras smelled of blood instead of incense.”

Saki’s fingers tightened against her knees. Rogues. Traitors. Smugglers. Or worse.

He drew another letter from the stack, this one creased more from rereading.

“ Lanterns sway like hearts

when the wind of whispers blows.

Sake spills like lies.

At the gate, masked shadows bow—

foxes in a tiger’s clothes. ”

“Foxes,” Saki murmured. “Tricksters. Disguised intentions.”

“Precisely.” Fuku’s gaze sharpened. “Kiku was not trained as a spy. But she is observant. She knew enough of politics to be afraid. She stopped describing people’s faces and began describing…their silences. Who sat too close. Who spoke too little.”

“And then the letters stopped.”

His hand tightened on the paper until it trembled. “Three months ago. This was the last.” He passed it to her. The paper was thinner, the brushstrokes rushed.

“ Ink runs thin at dawn.

Night birds circle shuttered rooms.

Footsteps stain the floors.

If petals fall and are swept,

will roots still remember spring? ”

Saki went still.

His voice was rough. “When I inquired, Guhdo sent a message claiming my daughter had…disgraced herself. That she formed an attachment to a lowly musician and ran away.” He spat the words like poison.

“The letter doesn’t sound like someone packing elopement bags,” she said.

Fuku’s head snapped up, eyes blazing. “My daughter is many things—gentle, soft-hearted, too eager to please—but she is not faithless. She married that man because I asked it of her. To secure this house, to win me the general’s favor and, through him, the king’s.” His voice cracked. “She would not betray my trust. She respects promises too deeply.”

Saki looked down at the letter in her hands. Ink runs thin at dawn. Footsteps stain the floors.

Not the words of someone in love with the idea of escape. More like someone bracing for a blow.

“You think Guhdo is lying to cover… what?” she asked carefully. “Her death? Her imprisonment?”

Fuku sagged a little, as if the question had been a weight he’d been holding too long. “I do not know. I see three paths. One, she tried to flee and was caught. Two, she spoke when she should have stayed silent about his…guests. Three”—his throat bobbed—“he tired of her and…disposed of her.”

The room seemed to darken around the edges.

“If it is the first,” Fuku continued, voice barely above a whisper, “she may yet live. If it is the second, she is in danger from forces beyond my reach. If it is the third…” He closed his eyes briefly. “Then I must know. I cannot sit in this fine house and drink my tea while my daughter’s bones are hidden under some brute’s floor.”

Saki thought of her own father, of the way he weighed risk and gain with that distant, calculating gaze. Her clan’s history was written in ink and blood, in contracts and marriages and silent takeovers.

The Fuma absorbed lesser clans by marrying their daughters, bringing new blood and land under their roof. A pleasant story on the surface. Less pleasant, when you’re the daughter. She’d heard the whispers—of course she had. Servants gossiped. Junior clansmen talked when they thought she wasn’t listening.

They said the clan head was waiting for the right price for her. That as the heir, she was worth more—to secure something that truly mattered. Once she was married off, he could name Jiro or Saito as successor, keep leadership in hands that wouldn’t leave the compound.

She hated those whispers. Hated how, sometimes, late at night, they made a terrible kind of sense.

“Lord Fuku,” she said, forcing her attention back to the man in front of her, “if Guhdo has mistreated her—”

Fuku’s composure cracked. His hands clenched into fists on his knees.

“If he has raised a hand to her, if he has locked her away, if he has allowed others to harm her under his roof—bring her back.” His gaze bored into hers, fierce and pleading. “I care nothing for the alliance anymore. Let my house fall, let my name be mud, if that is the price. I will not leave her in a nest of vipers to keep my seat at court.”

Saki swallowed.

A father who would burn his position to rescue his daughter.

Would her own father do the same? Or would he decide her marriage, her alliance, mattered more than her comfort? Than her consent?

She didn’t know. She’d never asked. She wasn’t sure she wanted the answer.

If she let herself be shuffled on the board the same way, agreeing to marry a feared general because her father asked it of her, would she face the same fate one day, too?

She wasn’t ready to confront her father. Clan heads didn’t share their plans with daughters, no matter how skilled with naginata or poison. If she asked, he’d smile that mild smile and say, “All in time, Saki.” And tell her nothing.

So she’d chosen another path: missions. Success. Proving, over and over, that she was more than a pretty name to sign on a marriage contract. That she could lead in the field, not just be led to an altar.

It stung that so many in the clan still doubted her. That her own brothers wrapped their doubt in love and called it protection. But doubt or not, Koji had given her this. Trusted her with something that mattered.

And sitting here, watching Lord Fuku’s careful composure fray, she realized this mission was more than a test of skill.

It was a glimpse of a future she refused to accept.

“This is not an official inquiry,” she said slowly. “If I am discovered, the Fuma will be disavowed. You understand that.”

He nodded. “I do. I ask not for justice through the courts. Only for truth.”

“If Lady Kiku is alive and wishes to leave, I will bring her back,” Saki said. “If she cannot leave, I will learn why. If she is…” The word snagged in her throat. “If she is beyond saving, you will know. I swear it.”

Fuku’s shoulders trembled once, then stilled. He bowed, low enough that his forelock brushed the tatami.

“You have my house’s gratitude, Princess Saki Fuma. And mine as her father.”

Saki rose to her knees and bowed just as deeply in return, forehead pressing against woven straw.

“I will not fail her,” she said quietly.

As she straightened, her eyes caught on the calligraphy in the alcove. Loyalty . Thick, unbroken strokes.

A daughter’s loyalty to her father. A father’s to his daughter.

She wasn’t sure which weighed more. But she knew this much: Kiku had bent herself to duty for her family’s sake. That sacrifice deserved more than to vanish into rumor and convenient lies.

A girl she’d never met had traded freedom for her father’s ambitions. Saki might one day be asked to do the same.

She touched the yellow senbon pouch at her waist, hidden beneath her plain kimono. It hummed with a presence that steadied her.

Not yet , she thought. Not if I can help it.
