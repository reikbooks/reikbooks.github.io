Chapter 1

← Back to Masamune Clan Series [EN]

W hen I woke, I thought for a moment that I was still on Master Kenta’s shop floor.

The first thing I felt was straw under my cheek, rough and damp. Then the stone beneath it, leeching the warmth from my bones. My ribs flared when I tried to breathe too deeply, a sharp, hot line like someone had driven a knife just under the skin and left it there. My back throbbed in long, raw tracks where the whip had kissed it; I didn’t dare move my shoulders.

The second thing I felt was the collar.

It dug into the bruises along my neck, metal biting against abraded skin. Even without a current running through it, the memory of the last shock crawled under my flesh. My muscles trembled of their own accord, little shudders skittering down my arms and legs. Every now and then my fingers seized—phantom aftershocks that made my stomach twist with nausea.

It took a few breaths before the room came into focus.

The same cell. The same ceiling with its hairline crack running from one corner to the other like a faint ink stroke. The same iron bars that smelled of rust and old blood. A pale rectangle of winter light leaked in through the high, barred window, dust drifting lazily through it like snowflakes that had lost their way.

So. I was back in the dungeon.

Master Kenta must have finished with me.

Memory came in pieces—dust motes in the slant of lantern light through the high window of Master Kenta’s carpet shop; the dry, choking smell of old tatami and new straw; carpets rolled and stacked in neat towers; his voice, thin and sharp as a reed flute, complaining that my sweeping was uneven, that I had set the bolts of imported rugs too close together, that I bowed too slowly to customers.

Each complaint followed by the sting and crackle of the collar.

“If you can’t remember simple instructions,” he had said. “I will teach your body to remember.”

By the second day, my knees had been raw from kneeling on the wooden floor. By the third, my hands shook constantly from the zaps, no matter how hard I tried to still them. I had dropped a bolt of carpet, the heavy roll clipping his display stand. The flash of anger on his face had been the last thing I remembered before everything went white and soundless.

Apparently, he had returned me to Master Oni unconscious.

I swallowed, tasting iron and bile. My lips were cracked. My tongue felt thick. Hunger gnawed at the hollow beneath my ribs—dull and familiar. I couldn’t remember when I had last eaten. The last bowl of rice at Master Kenta’s? Had that been yesterday? The day before?

I tried to curl tighter on the straw, but my ribs objected loudly. A cough clawed its way up and tore through me, sending needles of pain through my side. Dots of black crowded the edges of my vision.

Cold.

The dungeon was always cold, but winter made it worse. The air stabbed at exposed skin. My thin, rough-spun shirt clung damply to my back, half-dried sweat and old water. My bare feet were numb where they touched the stone, but numbness was better than the sharp ache that came before it.

I stared up at the ceiling, following the familiar crack with my eyes.

The date floated up from somewhere in my mind, as clear as if it had been inked onto the stone itself.

Thirty-first day of December?

No. I counted backward. I had been at Master Kenta’s for three days. So, the thirtieth day of December.

Last year on this day, I had been home.

The memory came unbidden: the main house warmed by extra braziers, the faint scent of grilled fish and sweet soy hanging in the air, Shiro scolding Raito for trying to steal a mochi before we had even finished setting the trays. Raito laughing, his cheeks already sticky. Shiro’s hair tied up with the red ribbon Mother had always used on festival days, the ribbon now slightly frayed but carefully kept.

I had been late that night, returning from extra drills. Father had been in the main room, speaking with Shiro and Raito. I had slid the fusuma open, bowed low, and apologized for my tardiness.

Father had only given me a brief, tired look and told me to sit.

Raito had pressed his knee against mine under the table. Shiro had pretended not to notice.

It had been warm.

I blinked, and the cell ceiling came back into view. The crack had not moved. My breath rose in a faint mist above my face.

A key scraped in the lock.

I forced my body to stillness. No flinching, no anticipation. That only made it worse when the blow came.

The door slid open with a dry groan. Master Oni’s guard—Suta, I thought his name was—filled the doorway, a thick shape outlined against the dim corridor torchlight. He carried a bucket in one hand and a folded pile of cloth and a coarse brush in the other.

“Sit up,” he grunted.

My muscles had gone stiff while I lay there. Pushing myself upright sent pain lancing through my ribs again. I bit the inside of my cheek and kept my face blank.

Suta didn’t wait. He dumped the water bucket over me.

Water—ice-cold, straight from the well—crashed over me. It stole the breath from my lungs, a thousand needles sinking into my skin. The shock made my back spasm; the whip marks screamed. I gasped without meaning to, teeth clacking together from the force of it.

The water soaked the straw and ran in rivulets across the stone. Steam rose faintly from my skin in the winter air. He stepped forward and scrubbed the brush over my neck, shoulders, and back in rough, efficient strokes, scraping away the grime and the crusted evidence of Master Kenta’s displeasure.

“Stand,” Suta said.

I obeyed, the collar heavy and cold against my throat. Water dripped from my hair, plastering my shirt to my chest. I tried to keep my shoulders square and my head lowered.

He stripped the wet shirt off me in a few rough tugs, not caring when the fabric stuck to scabs and ripped them open again. Fresh heat bloomed along my back. I stared at the wall, counting my breaths.

He tossed a dry garment at me. Rough, slave-standard: thin, faded, sleeves too short. I pulled it on as quickly as my ribs allowed.

Suta stepped behind me and yanked my wrists together. The coarse hemp rope bit into my skin as he wound it tight, knotting it without finesse. My shoulders protested when he pulled my arms too high. Then he put a hand on the back of my neck and pushed.

“Kneel.”

My knees hit the stone. Cold shot up my thighs. I shifted slightly so the worst of the pressure avoided the bruises I knew were already blooming there. He didn’t bother to check; he just looked me over once to make sure I was properly bound and then stepped back.

“Wait,” he said, and left, sliding the door closed.

The dungeon fell silent again. Water dripped steadily from my hair onto the stone between my knees. My hands throbbed where the rope pinched. I focused on the simple things: the rhythm of my heart, the tick of cooling water, the faint, distant hum of wind outside.

The collar hummed as if in answer.

I didn’t know how much time passed. Long enough that the wet patch under my knees turned from freezing to numbing. Long enough for my shivers to slow.

Bootsteps came, different from Suta’s heavy stride—lighter, more measured. The kind that did not hurry for anyone.

The door slid open.

“Raise your head,” Master Oni said.

I obeyed.

Master Oni always looked out of place in the dungeon. His robes were too fine, his hair too carefully oiled and combed back. Today he wore a layered dark blue kimono patterned subtly with cranes, the silk catching what little light there was. His face was smooth and fleshy, his eyes small and sharp behind hooded lids. A faint perfume clung to him—expensive incense, masking the underlying smell of iron and fear.

His gaze traveled over me like a merchant inspecting flawed goods. He clicked his tongue softly.

“Kenta-dono is careless,” he murmured. “Bruised ribs, whip marks too fresh. If he breaks you, it reduces your value.”

“My apologies, Master,” I said, forehead nearly touching the floor again.

Oni stepped closer. His hand—soft and thick—caught my chin and lifted it, turning my face left, then right. I held my eyes half-lowered, careful not to meet his gaze directly. His fingers smelled faintly of ink and camellia oil.

“Clan report today,” he said. “Elder Low will want to see you. You will kneel properly, answer when spoken to, and keep your head bowed. Do not shame my training.”

“Yes, Master.”

He let go. I swayed slightly as the weight left my jaw. Then I heard the faint jingle of metal.

Oni produced a chain leash from his sleeve, the links polished from use. He bent and attached it to the ring on my collar with a practiced click. The metal brushed my skin, cold enough to sting.

The tug on the chain made the collar shift, pressing into a deep bruise along the left side of my neck. I swallowed the sound that tried to escape.

“Up,” he said.

He led me out of the cell, the leash just short enough that I had to stay close behind him, the rope biting my wrists with each step. The corridor smelled of damp stone and old straw. We passed other cells, some occupied, some not. I kept my gaze forward, focused on the swaying of Oni’s sleeves.

At the end of the corridor, Suta slid open the heavy wooden door that led out into the courtyard.

Winter hit like another bucket of water.

The sky was the color of dull steel, clouds pressing low. Thin snow crusted the packed earth, crunching faintly under Oni’s sandals and soaking immediately into my bare feet. My breath plumed white in front of me. The buildings of the training compound huddled together, roofs dusted with frost.

Beyond the walls, I could see the tiled roofs of the Masamune compound proper rising, layered and orderly, banners hanging limp in the still air. The clan crest was barely visible through the haze.

Oni walked quickly; the chain jerked when I fell half a step behind. Each jolt rattled the collar against my throat. I adjusted my stride, forcing my legs to move steadily despite the ache in my back and the stiffness in my knees.

I remembered the oath, the words drilled into me since childhood, and let them march through my mind in time with my steps.

I, Tadashi Masamune, pledge my life, my strength, and my will…

The main hall stood at the heart of the compound, elevated on a broad stone base, its wide eaves sheltering the veranda. Paper lanterns hung unlit under the roof, their faded red shells stirring gently in the breeze. Two guards stood at the entrance, spears upright, eyes sliding over me without recognition.

The disguise seal on the collar worked well. To them, I was just another nameless servant in chains. Only Father, Elder Low, and Master Oni could see me as I was.

Oni guided me up the wooden steps to the veranda. The grain of the wood was rough beneath my numb feet. At the threshold, he paused to slip off his sandals.

“Kneel,” he said, without looking back. I dropped to my knees on the polished wooden floor of the entryway, head bowed. He stepped up into the hall in his tabi socks, waited a moment, then gave the leash a sharp tug. I rose and followed him inside. The air was marginally warmer, smelling faintly of tatami, ink, and incense.

The main hall’s doors slid open.

I kept my gaze on the tatami as we entered. Following the tug of the leash, I moved to the center of the open floor and knelt, as I had been trained. I felt Master Oni settle on his knees a pace behind me, the leash going taut as he took up the slack. He was my trainer, my keeper, and my chain.

Elder Low sat to one side of the raised dais, robes a simple, unadorned gray. His hair, once black, had gone almost entirely white, tied back at the nape of his neck. Lines bracketing his mouth made him look perpetually stern. A stack of bamboo scrolls rested beside him.

Father sat in the center.

He wore formal black robes with the clan crest embroidered in silver thread at the sleeves, his posture straight, hands resting lightly on his thighs. His expression was carved in stone—calm, impassive, eyes hooded. A brazier burned to his left, heat curling faintly into the air.

I bowed until my forehead touched the tatami.

“Tadashi reports,” Elder Low said, his voice carrying easily in the quiet hall. “Sixth month of the atonement mission, end-of-year inspection.”

“Proceed,” Father said.

The word was flat, neither warm nor cold. It might have been addressed to the air.

Master Oni knelt behind me, the leash still taut. “Honored Clan Head, Elder Low,” he said smoothly. “The boy continues to show improvement in obedience and endurance. His pain threshold is… satisfactory. There are some issues with fine motor control under duress, but I will correct them.”

“I see.” Elder Low’s gaze rested on me. “Raise your head, Tadashi.”

I did, only enough to see the mat in front of me.

“How is your condition?” he asked.

“I am able to continue, Elder,” I said. My voice sounded thin in my own ears. “This unworthy one apologizes for his current… unsightly state.”

Oni chuckled quietly behind me, a sound quickly smothered.

Elder Low did not comment. He shifted one of the scrolls. “Head Akito’s household is next on your ledger. He requires your service for the next three days. You are to remain there until the first day of the New Year.” His eyes lifted, final. “Continue your atonement as ordered.”

My lungs forgot how to work for a heartbeat.

Akito.

I lowered my head again, the tatami blurring. “I understand, Elder. It will be done.”

Elder Low set the scroll aside. “Given the nature of the assignment,” he went on, “I will adjust your collar’s recognition seal. Head Akito and his son must be able to identify you correctly.”

My fingers dug unconsciously into my bound palms.

Elder Low reached out, two fingers extended. The power from his hand pulsed across the space like a warm breeze, then condensed around my throat.

The collar flared hot.

I bit down hard as the seal burned, lines of invisible fire etching themselves into metal and skin both. The disguised face the collar projected wavered at the edges of my vision, then settled back over my features, subtly altered. I felt… visible , in a way that made my stomach twist.

“You will behave with absolute obedience,” Elder Low said quietly. “Head Akito has full authority over you for the duration. Do you understand?”

“Yes, Elder.”

“Good.” Elder Low turned slightly toward the dais. “The report is finished, Clan Head.”

I held my breath, my heart hammering against my bruised ribs. I dared a glance upward, just enough to see the hem of Father’s robes, the set of his shoulders. If I lifted my eyes another finger-width, I would see his face.

His attention was elsewhere—on the Elder, the wall, the air itself. Anywhere but on me. He didn’t even acknowledge the space I occupied.

“Proceed,” he said, his voice flat and detached. That was all.

No question about my condition. No reprimand for failing. No acknowledgement at all that the boy kneeling in chains before him shared his blood.

For a moment, the pain in my ribs, my back, my neck—all of it—faded beneath a sharper, cleaner ache. Perhaps, since that day, Father no longer considered me his son.

I touched my forehead to the mat again, hiding my expression there where no one could see it. My throat tightened under the collar.

“Understood,” Elder Low said. “Master Oni, deliver him to Head Akito immediately.”

“As you command.” Oni bowed low, then rose, tugging the leash.

I followed, knees protesting as I stood. The hall slid past in a blur of wood and paper and shadow. I did not look toward the dais again. I did not risk it.

Outside, the winter air slapped my face. Oni did not slow, striding across the courtyard toward the western sector where Akito’s estate lay. Each jolt of the leash made the collar scrape against new-burned skin.

I thought of Father’s words before this mission had begun, when we had sat facing each other like strangers across the tatami after the hearing.

“You will accept the elders’ judgment,” he had said, his voice steady but distant. “You will not ask for lenience. Whatever hardship comes, you will endure it in silence. This is the weight of your failure, Tadashi. If you cannot bear this, you are unfit to bear the Masamune name.”

I had bowed until my forehead touched the floor.

“Yes, Father.”

He had not touched my head. Had not even sighed. He had simply risen and left, the tatami rustling under his steps, leaving me alone with the echo of his disappointment.

Six months later, I still felt that echo more sharply than any whip.

I had not attended the heir evaluation. I had run after Shiro instead, driven by panic, by the memory of Mother’s blood. I had caused trouble for the clan. Shamed Father before the elders. I knew this. I accepted that I needed to atone.

If I endured this properly, if I did not complain, perhaps he would forgive me. Perhaps, when this mission was over, he would look at me again the way he had before everything broke.

But the collar around my neck was heavy, the rope around my wrists cut deeper with each step, and the cold seemed to seep into my very bones. Half a year of being passed from hand to hand, beaten, ignored, used. Some masters had been kind, or at least indifferent. Lately, only the other sort seemed to request me.

Sometimes, late at night in the cell, when the pain wouldn’t let me sleep, I found myself wishing—just a little—that Father would intervene. That he would send someone with a blanket. A proper meal. A word.

He never did.

The leash jerked as Oni turned down a narrower path. The buildings here were larger, the roofs sweeping higher, the walls freshly painted. Akito’s mansion loomed ahead, two stories of dark wood and white plaster, its gate flanked by stone lanterns. The Masamune crest hung over the entrance, stitched in deep red on black.

Both Akito and Sadi hated me.

Akito, who had opposed my appointment as heir, who had pushed for harsher seals and stricter discipline. Sadi, whose eyes had been sharp and cold since the day I was chosen in his place.

I did not want to imagine what they would do with a slave they knew was me.

My feet carried me forward anyway, one step and the next and the next, because the collar would not allow me to stop.

The stone of the gateway threshold was freezing under my toes as Master Oni dragged me across it by the leash. My world had narrowed to the press of metal at my throat, the ache in my ribs, and the hollow in my chest where hope had once sat.

For the first time in my life, as the gate of Akito’s mansion slid shut behind us, I found myself wondering what it would be like not to get up again.

What it would be like to simply… stop. To let go of the oath. Of duty. Of everything.

To break my promise to my mother.

The thought suffocated me more than the collar ever had.

I lowered my head, followed the tug of the chain deeper into the mansion, and tried not to think at all.
