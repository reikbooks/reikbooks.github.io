Exhaustion is survivable. But when his father no longer recognizes him as a son, what reason is left to keep breathing?***

W hen I woke, I thought for a moment that I was still on Master Kenta’s shop floor.

The first thing I felt was straw under my cheek, rough and damp. Then the stone beneath it, leeching the warmth from my bones. My ribs flared when I tried to breathe too deeply, a sharp, hot line like someone had driven a knife just under the skin and left it there. My back throbbed in long, raw tracks where the whip had kissed it; I didn’t dare move my shoulders.

The second thing I felt was the collar.

It dug into the bruises along my neck, metal biting against abraded skin. Even without a current running through it, the memory of the last shock crawled under my flesh. My muscles trembled of their own accord, little shudders skittering down my arms and legs. Every now and then my fingers seized—phantom aftershocks that made my stomach twist with nausea.

It took a few breaths before the room came into focus.

The same cell. The same ceiling with its hairline crack running from one corner to the other like a faint ink stroke. The same iron bars that smelled of rust and old blood. A pale rectangle of winter light leaked in through the high, barred window, dust drifting lazily through it like snowflakes that had lost their way.

So. I was back in the dungeon.

Master Kenta must have finished with me.

Memory came in pieces—dust motes in the slant of lantern light through the high window of Master Kenta’s carpet shop; the dry, choking smell of old tatami and new straw; carpets rolled and stacked in neat towers; his voice, thin and sharp as a reed flute, complaining that my sweeping was uneven, that I had set the bolts of imported rugs too close together, that I bowed too slowly to customers.

Each complaint followed by the sting and crackle of the collar.

“If you can’t remember simple instructions,” he had said. “I will teach your body to remember.”

By the second day, my knees had been raw from kneeling on the wooden floor. By the third, my hands shook constantly from the zaps, no matter how hard I tried to still them. I had dropped a bolt of carpet, the heavy roll clipping his display stand. The flash of anger on his face had been the last thing I remembered before everything went white and soundless.

Apparently, he had returned me to Master Oni unconscious.

I swallowed, tasting iron and bile. My lips were cracked. My tongue felt thick. Hunger gnawed at the hollow beneath my ribs—dull and familiar. I couldn’t remember when I had last eaten. The last bowl of rice at Master Kenta’s? Had that been yesterday? The day before?

I tried to curl tighter on the straw, but my ribs objected loudly. A cough clawed its way up and tore through me, sending needles of pain through my side. Dots of black crowded the edges of my vision.

Cold.

The dungeon was always cold, but winter made it worse. The air stabbed at exposed skin. My thin, rough-spun shirt clung damply to my back, half-dried sweat and old water. My bare feet were numb where they touched the stone, but numbness was better than the sharp ache that came before it.

I stared up at the ceiling, following the familiar crack with my eyes.

The date floated up from somewhere in my mind, as clear as if it had been inked onto the stone itself.

Thirty-first day of December?

No. I counted backward. I had been at Master Kenta’s for three days. So, the thirtieth day of December.

Last year on this day, I had been home.

The memory came unbidden: the main house warmed by extra braziers, the faint scent of grilled fish and sweet soy hanging in the air, Shiro scolding Raito for trying to steal a mochi before we had even finished setting the trays. Raito laughing, his cheeks already sticky. Shiro’s hair tied up with the red ribbon Mother had always used on festival days, the ribbon now slightly frayed but carefully kept.

I had been late that night, returning from extra drills. Father had been in the main room, speaking with Shiro and Raito. I had slid the fusuma open, bowed low, and apologized for my tardiness.

Father had only given me a brief, tired look and told me to sit.

Raito had pressed his knee against mine under the table. Shiro had pretended not to notice.

It had been warm.

I blinked, and the cell ceiling came back into view. The crack had not moved. My breath rose in a faint mist above my face.

A key scraped in the lock.

I forced my body to stillness. No flinching, no anticipation. That only made it worse when the blow came.

The door slid open with a dry groan. Master Oni’s guard—Suta, I thought his name was—filled the doorway, a thick shape outlined against the dim corridor torchlight. He carried a bucket in one hand and a folded pile of cloth and a coarse brush in the other.

“Sit up,” he grunted.

My muscles had gone stiff while I lay there. Pushing myself upright sent pain lancing through my ribs again. I bit the inside of my cheek and kept my face blank.

Suta didn’t wait. He dumped the water bucket over me.

Water—ice-cold, straight from the well—crashed over me. It stole the breath from my lungs, a thousand needles sinking into my skin. The shock made my back spasm; the whip marks screamed. I gasped without meaning to, teeth clacking together from the force of it.

The water soaked the straw and ran in rivulets across the stone. Steam rose faintly from my skin in the winter air. He stepped forward and scrubbed the brush over my neck, shoulders, and back in rough, efficient strokes, scraping away the grime and the crusted evidence of Master Kenta’s displeasure.

“Stand,” Suta said.

I obeyed, the collar heavy and cold against my throat. Water dripped from my hair, plastering my shirt to my chest. I tried to keep my shoulders square and my head lowered.

He stripped the wet shirt off me in a few rough tugs, not caring when the fabric stuck to scabs and ripped them open again. Fresh heat bloomed along my back. I stared at the wall, counting my breaths.

He tossed a dry garment at me. Rough, slave-standard: thin, faded, sleeves too short. I pulled it on as quickly as my ribs allowed.

Suta stepped behind me and yanked my wrists together. The coarse hemp rope bit into my skin as he wound it tight, knotting it without finesse. My shoulders protested when he pulled my arms too high. Then he put a hand on the back of my neck and pushed.

“Kneel.”

My knees hit the stone. Cold shot up my thighs. I shifted slightly so the worst of the pressure avoided the bruises I knew were already blooming there. He didn’t bother to check; he just looked me over once to make sure I was properly bound and then stepped back.

“Wait,” he said, and left, sliding the door closed.

The dungeon fell silent again. Water dripped steadily from my hair onto the stone between my knees. My hands throbbed where the rope pinched. I focused on the simple things: the rhythm of my heart, the tick of cooling water, the faint, distant hum of wind outside.

The collar hummed as if in answer.

I didn’t know how much time passed. Long enough that the wet patch under my knees turned from freezing to numbing. Long enough for my shivers to slow.

Bootsteps came, different from Suta’s heavy stride—lighter, more measured. The kind that did not hurry for anyone.

The door slid open.

“Raise your head,” Master Oni said.

I obeyed.

Master Oni always looked out of place in the dungeon. His robes were too fine, his hair too carefully oiled and combed back. Today he wore a layered dark blue kimono patterned subtly with cranes, the silk catching what little light there was. His face was smooth and fleshy, his eyes small and sharp behind hooded lids. A faint perfume clung to him—expensive incense, masking the underlying smell of iron and fear.

His gaze traveled over me like a merchant inspecting flawed goods. He clicked his tongue softly.

“Kenta-dono is careless,” he murmured. “Bruised ribs, whip marks too fresh. If he breaks you, it reduces your value.”

“My apologies, Master,” I said, forehead nearly touching the floor again.

Oni stepped closer. His hand—soft and thick—caught my chin and lifted it, turning my face left, then right. I held my eyes half-lowered, careful not to meet his gaze directly. His fingers smelled faintly of ink and camellia oil.

“Clan report today,” he said. “Elder Low will want to see you. You will kneel properly, answer when spoken to, and keep your head bowed. Do not shame my training.”

“Yes, Master.”

He let go. I swayed slightly as the weight left my jaw. Then I heard the faint jingle of metal.

Oni produced a chain leash from his sleeve, the links polished from use. He bent and attached it to the ring on my collar with a practiced click. The metal brushed my skin, cold enough to sting.

The tug on the chain made the collar shift, pressing into a deep bruise along the left side of my neck. I swallowed the sound that tried to escape.

“Up,” he said.

He led me out of the cell, the leash just short enough that I had to stay close behind him, the rope biting my wrists with each step. The corridor smelled of damp stone and old straw. We passed other cells, some occupied, some not. I kept my gaze forward, focused on the swaying of Oni’s sleeves.

At the end of the corridor, Suta slid open the heavy wooden door that led out into the courtyard.

Winter hit like another bucket of water.

The sky was the color of dull steel, clouds pressing low. Thin snow crusted the packed earth, crunching faintly under Oni’s sandals and soaking immediately into my bare feet. My breath plumed white in front of me. The buildings of the training compound huddled together, roofs dusted with frost.

Beyond the walls, I could see the tiled roofs of the Masamune compound proper rising, layered and orderly, banners hanging limp in the still air. The clan crest was barely visible through the haze.

Oni walked quickly; the chain jerked when I fell half a step behind. Each jolt rattled the collar against my throat. I adjusted my stride, forcing my legs to move steadily despite the ache in my back and the stiffness in my knees.

I remembered the oath, the words drilled into me since childhood, and let them march through my mind in time with my steps.

I, Tadashi Masamune, pledge my life, my strength, and my will…

The main hall stood at the heart of the compound, elevated on a broad stone base, its wide eaves sheltering the veranda. Paper lanterns hung unlit under the roof, their faded red shells stirring gently in the breeze. Two guards stood at the entrance, spears upright, eyes sliding over me without recognition.

The disguise seal on the collar worked well. To them, I was just another nameless servant in chains. Only Father, Elder Low, and Master Oni could see me as I was.

Oni guided me up the wooden steps to the veranda. The grain of the wood was rough beneath my numb feet. At the threshold, he paused to slip off his sandals.

“Kneel,” he said, without looking back. I dropped to my knees on the polished wooden floor of the entryway, head bowed. He stepped up into the hall in his tabi socks, waited a moment, then gave the leash a sharp tug. I rose and followed him inside. The air was marginally warmer, smelling faintly of tatami, ink, and incense.

The main hall’s doors slid open.

I kept my gaze on the tatami as we entered. Following the tug of the leash, I moved to the center of the open floor and knelt, as I had been trained. I felt Master Oni settle on his knees a pace behind me, the leash going taut as he took up the slack. He was my trainer, my keeper, and my chain.

Elder Low sat to one side of the raised dais, robes a simple, unadorned gray. His hair, once black, had gone almost entirely white, tied back at the nape of his neck. Lines bracketing his mouth made him look perpetually stern. A stack of bamboo scrolls rested beside him.

Father sat in the center.

He wore formal black robes with the clan crest embroidered in silver thread at the sleeves, his posture straight, hands resting lightly on his thighs. His expression was carved in stone—calm, impassive, eyes hooded. A brazier burned to his left, heat curling faintly into the air.

I bowed until my forehead touched the tatami.

“Tadashi reports,” Elder Low said, his voice carrying easily in the quiet hall. “Sixth month of the atonement mission, end-of-year inspection.”

“Proceed,” Father said.

The word was flat, neither warm nor cold. It might have been addressed to the air.

Master Oni knelt behind me, the leash still taut. “Honored Clan Head, Elder Low,” he said smoothly. “The boy continues to show improvement in obedience and endurance. His pain threshold is… satisfactory. There are some issues with fine motor control under duress, but I will correct them.”

“I see.” Elder Low’s gaze rested on me. “Raise your head, Tadashi.”

I did, only enough to see the mat in front of me.

“How is your condition?” he asked.

“I am able to continue, Elder,” I said. My voice sounded thin in my own ears. “This unworthy one apologizes for his current… unsightly state.”

Oni chuckled quietly behind me, a sound quickly smothered.

Elder Low did not comment. He shifted one of the scrolls. “Head Akito’s household is next on your ledger. He requires your service for the next three days. You are to remain there until the first day of the New Year.” His eyes lifted, final. “Continue your atonement as ordered.”

My lungs forgot how to work for a heartbeat.

Akito.

I lowered my head again, the tatami blurring. “I understand, Elder. It will be done.”

Elder Low set the scroll aside. “Given the nature of the assignment,” he went on, “I will adjust your collar’s recognition seal. Head Akito and his son must be able to identify you correctly.”

My fingers dug unconsciously into my bound palms.

Elder Low reached out, two fingers extended. The power from his hand pulsed across the space like a warm breeze, then condensed around my throat.

The collar flared hot.

I bit down hard as the seal burned, lines of invisible fire etching themselves into metal and skin both. The disguised face the collar projected wavered at the edges of my vision, then settled back over my features, subtly altered. I felt… visible , in a way that made my stomach twist.

“You will behave with absolute obedience,” Elder Low said quietly. “Head Akito has full authority over you for the duration. Do you understand?”

“Yes, Elder.”

“Good.” Elder Low turned slightly toward the dais. “The report is finished, Clan Head.”

I held my breath, my heart hammering against my bruised ribs. I dared a glance upward, just enough to see the hem of Father’s robes, the set of his shoulders. If I lifted my eyes another finger-width, I would see his face.

His attention was elsewhere—on the Elder, the wall, the air itself. Anywhere but on me. He didn’t even acknowledge the space I occupied.

“Proceed,” he said, his voice flat and detached. That was all.

No question about my condition. No reprimand for failing. No acknowledgement at all that the boy kneeling in chains before him shared his blood.

For a moment, the pain in my ribs, my back, my neck—all of it—faded beneath a sharper, cleaner ache. Perhaps, since that day, Father no longer considered me his son.

I touched my forehead to the mat again, hiding my expression there where no one could see it. My throat tightened under the collar.

“Understood,” Elder Low said. “Master Oni, deliver him to Head Akito immediately.”

“As you command.” Oni bowed low, then rose, tugging the leash.

I followed, knees protesting as I stood. The hall slid past in a blur of wood and paper and shadow. I did not look toward the dais again. I did not risk it.

Outside, the winter air slapped my face. Oni did not slow, striding across the courtyard toward the western sector where Akito’s estate lay. Each jolt of the leash made the collar scrape against new-burned skin.

I thought of Father’s words before this mission had begun, when we had sat facing each other like strangers across the tatami after the hearing.

“You will accept the elders’ judgment,” he had said, his voice steady but distant. “You will not ask for lenience. Whatever hardship comes, you will endure it in silence. This is the weight of your failure, Tadashi. If you cannot bear this, you are unfit to bear the Masamune name.”

I had bowed until my forehead touched the floor.

“Yes, Father.”

He had not touched my head. Had not even sighed. He had simply risen and left, the tatami rustling under his steps, leaving me alone with the echo of his disappointment.

Six months later, I still felt that echo more sharply than any whip.

I had not attended the heir evaluation. I had run after Shiro instead, driven by panic, by the memory of Mother’s blood. I had caused trouble for the clan. Shamed Father before the elders. I knew this. I accepted that I needed to atone.

If I endured this properly, if I did not complain, perhaps he would forgive me. Perhaps, when this mission was over, he would look at me again the way he had before everything broke.

But the collar around my neck was heavy, the rope around my wrists cut deeper with each step, and the cold seemed to seep into my very bones. Half a year of being passed from hand to hand, beaten, ignored, used. Some masters had been kind, or at least indifferent. Lately, only the other sort seemed to request me.

Sometimes, late at night in the cell, when the pain wouldn’t let me sleep, I found myself wishing—just a little—that Father would intervene. That he would send someone with a blanket. A proper meal. A word.

He never did.

The leash jerked as Oni turned down a narrower path. The buildings here were larger, the roofs sweeping higher, the walls freshly painted. Akito’s mansion loomed ahead, two stories of dark wood and white plaster, its gate flanked by stone lanterns. The Masamune crest hung over the entrance, stitched in deep red on black.

Both Akito and Sadi hated me.

Akito, who had opposed my appointment as heir, who had pushed for harsher seals and stricter discipline. Sadi, whose eyes had been sharp and cold since the day I was chosen in his place.

I did not want to imagine what they would do with a slave they knew was me.

My feet carried me forward anyway, one step and the next and the next, because the collar would not allow me to stop.

The stone of the gateway threshold was freezing under my toes as Master Oni dragged me across it by the leash. My world had narrowed to the press of metal at my throat, the ache in my ribs, and the hollow in my chest where hope had once sat.

For the first time in my life, as the gate of Akito’s mansion slid shut behind us, I found myself wondering what it would be like not to get up again.

What it would be like to simply… stop. To let go of the oath. Of duty. Of everything.

To break my promise to my mother.

The thought suffocated me more than the collar ever had.

I lowered my head, followed the tug of the chain deeper into the mansion, and tried not to think at all.

M aster Oni kept the leash short as he led me down the silent corridor.

Akito’s mansion felt wrong compared to the rest of the compound—too quiet, too hollow. The paper doors were all closed, the lamps unlit, and the air was heavy with dust and something sharper underneath: ink, old sake, and the faint, metallic tang of blood that had been scrubbed but not entirely erased.

We stopped before a set of double doors. Oni gave the chain a sharp tug.

“Kneel,” he said.

I sank onto the polished wood, head bowed, the rope still biting my wrists behind my back. The doors slid open with a clean, oiled whisper.

“Akito-sama,” Oni said smoothly. “I have brought the boy, as requested.”

“You may enter,” came the reply.

Akito Masamune’s voice was not loud. It didn’t need to be. Each word was clipped and precise, as if he were conserving energy for something else.

Oni stepped forward, dragging me the last half-step over the threshold.

Akito sat on a low cushion by a lacquered table, scrolls stacked neatly to one side. His robes were immaculate—dark brown with a subtle geometric pattern, a lighter haori thrown over his shoulders. His hair was tied back with a plain cord. His face, as always, looked carved from fine stone: long, narrow, with sharp cheekbones and thin lips that never quite relaxed.

His eyes, when they settled on me, were colder than the winter outside.

“So,” he said, inspecting me. “This is the… asset Elder Low spoke of.”

“Just so,” Oni said. “This slave is currently six months into his atonement mission. Pain tolerance and obedience are high. The collar ensures compliance.”

Akito’s gaze lingered on the metal at my throat. “Explain.”

Oni’s tone shifted into his merchant’s cadence, proud and smooth. “The collar carries several seals, Akito-sama. A disguise seal masking his identity from all but designated viewers—yourself, Elder Low, Clan Head Masada, and me. A restraint seal that delivers electrical punishment on command. There are also conditional bindings: if he attempts to flee or directly disobey a clear order from his current master, the collar will activate automatically.”

Akito extended one hand. “Show me.”

“Of course.”

Oni moved behind me. I heard the soft rustle of his sleeve, then felt his fingers brush the side of the collar. Power flared, cold and quick.

The zap hit like a swarm of hornets burrowing into my muscles.

My back arched despite the bindings, a strangled breath ripping from my lungs before I could stop it. Every nerve lit up in a bright, buzzing agony that numbed the very edges of itself. My fingers clenched uselessly against the rope; my vision went white at the edges.

Then it was gone, leaving behind a trembling, hollow ache and the taste of bile in my mouth.

“Moderate level,” Oni said, as if describing the weather. “He can withstand significantly higher.”

“Hmm.” Akito’s eyes had not left my face. I could feel his scrutiny, clinical and unblinking. “May I?”

“Please.”

I heard movement as Oni stepped aside. Akito rose, the soft brush of his hakama against the tatami somehow sharper than the sound of the earlier shock.

He came to stand directly before me.

“Look up,” he said.

I obeyed, lifting my gaze to his throat, careful not to meet his eyes directly.

He reached down, fingers cool and dry as they curled under my jaw, tilting my head to one side. His touch was not rough like Master Oni’s or Master Kenta’s. It was detached, the way a physician might examine a new tool.

“The marks on his neck?” he asked.

“From prior punishments,” Oni replied. “The collar leaves no permanent scars if managed correctly.”

“Mm.” Akito’s thumb pressed lightly along the bruise. Pain bloomed, small and sharp.

His fingers slipped from my jaw to the collar itself. Power gathered—denser and more refined than Oni’s.

The shock that followed was smaller than Oni’s demonstration, but more focused, like a needle of lightning driven straight into the base of my skull. My vision flared, then snapped tight. My stomach lurched; my shoulders jerked against the bindings.

I didn’t make a sound.

Akito watched closely, then let go. “Responsive,” he said. “Your craftsmanship is… adequate, Oni.”

“You honor me, Akito-sama,” Oni said, bowing. I could almost hear the smile in his voice.

Akito returned to his cushion, smooth and unhurried. “You may leave him,” he said. “I will note any deficiencies and report them directly to Clan Head Masada and Elder Low.”

“I am at your service,” Oni murmured. He gave the leash one last sharp tug—more a reminder than a necessity—then dropped it, bowed toward Akito, and swept out of the room.

The doors slid shut.

Silence pressed in, thick as damp wool.

I stayed kneeling, head bowed, feeling the faint tremors still rippling through my muscles. The leash lay slack against my chest, the chain coiled on the tatami.

“Slave,” Akito said.

The word cut deeper the shocks.

“Yes, Master,” I answered.

“You will address me as ‘Master’ at all times,” he said. “You have no other name in this house. You will work in silence. You are not to utter any sound unless I or Sadi give you explicit permission. You will not speak, you will not question, you will not answer anyone—not the neighbors, not any visitor, not even the elders—unless I order you to. Do you understand?”

“Yes, Master.”

The blow came faster than I could brace.

His hand cracked across my face, the force of the slap snapping my head to the side. My teeth clicked painfully together; copper flooded my mouth where I’d bitten my cheek.

“Rule one,” Akito said, voice still calm. “You will obey precisely. When I say you will not utter any sound unless I grant permission, that includes answering questions. You will wait until I say: ‘Speak.’ The fact that you failed such a simple instruction…” He let the sentence trail off with a soft, derisive exhale. “Master Oni’s training is clearly overrated.”

I lowered my forehead to the floor. Blood tickled at the corner of my lip. “This unworthy one—”

The collar flared, a quick, stinging zap. Not enough to make me convulse, just enough to cut the words off in my throat. My breath hitched.

Akito’s silence was heavy, and when he spoke again, a thread of irritation had woven into his calm. “Every mistake you make here,” he said, “every failure, every tardy task, I will record. And at the end of your assignment, I will deliver that report personally to Masada.”

The name landed like a stone in my gut.

He let the implications hang between us for a beat.

“Do you understand,” he said again, “Slave?”

I met the tatami with my forehead once, a small, controlled bow.

He waited, clearly expecting more. When nothing came, the faint crease in his brow smoothed.

“Good. At least you can learn.”

Paper rustled. He picked up a bamboo slip, running his eyes down it.

“Due to a recent breach of security, I dismissed the servants several months ago,” he said. “Their replacements have not yet been selected. This house is… beneath my standards.” A faint curl of disgust touched his lips. “You will correct that.”

He began to read.

“Entry hall: sweep, scrub the threshold stones, polish the door runners. Main corridor: dust the beams, wipe all shōji frames, remove any cobwebs. Guest rooms: air the futons, beat the bedding, wipe the tatami with vinegar water, polish the metal fittings. Storage rooms: inventory loose items, arrange them properly, sweep. Kitchen: scour the hearth, clean the pots, scrub the floor. Washroom: clean the tub, replenish firewood and water. Garden paths: clear snow and debris from the stepping stones. Stables: no, those are empty for now.”

The list went on. Each task stacked on the last until it felt less like chores and more like a mountain.

A whole household’s worth of work, and only one pair of hands.

He rolled the slip closed. “All of it is to be completed before dawn,” he said. “If I find a single area neglected, I will assume willful disobedience.”

My ribs ached with each breath. My arms throbbed from where the rope had held them too long. I had not truly rested since Master Kenta’s shop. The thought of scrubbing floors and carrying water through the day made my vision sway.

But I pressed my forehead more firmly to the floor.

“As for boundaries,” Akito continued, rising, “you are forbidden to enter my study or my private room unless I call you or if there is an emergency. If I find you there uninvited, I will consider it a violation of house security.”

He stepped around me. I heard the faint click of something against the collar, felt his power gather again.

“One more thing,” he murmured.

The seal he laid onto the collar this time felt different—smaller, more intricate. Threads of energy sank into the metal, then into my skin, like a net of fine wires embedding themselves.

“You will not fall asleep on the job,” he said. “This auxiliary seal will deliver low-grade shocks at irregular intervals. A reminder. Motivation.” The corner of his mouth lifted fractionally. “You should be grateful. It will make you more efficient.”

I forced my jaw to unclench and simply bowed again, silently. Akito made a soft, satisfied sound. “I have a meeting in town. I expect the work to be done when I return.”

He took a step, then paused. “When you finish, you will wait in the kitchen. Kneeling, in silence. Do not move from there unless called.”

His sandals whispered over the tatami. The door slid open, then closed.

For a moment I stayed kneeling, eyes closed, counting my breaths until the echo of his presence faded from the room. Then, I pushed myself slowly to my feet.

My wrists burned where the rope had been. I flexed my fingers, testing them, before reaching for the loose end of the binding. Suta had left it intentionally easy to undo; they wanted work from me, after all, not a trussed ornament.

The rope fell away. Pins and needles shot up my forearms.

I found the cleaning tools stacked haphazardly in a side cupboard off the kitchen. The disarray told me how long the house had gone without staff: buckets nested crookedly, brooms with broken bristles, rags stiff with old grease.

I started with the entry hall.

Water was heavy. The bucket’s wooden handle bit into my fingers as I carried it—again and again—from the well in the rear courtyard. Each trip sent a fresh ache through my ribs. Each time I bent to scrub, my back burned where the whip marks split and wept under the thin cloth of my shirt.

The new seal in the collar did its work.

Just as my movements began to dull, just as my mind floated toward exhaustion, a small, sharp shock would nip at my throat or the base of my skull. Not enough to make me cry out. Enough to jolt my heart and steal the comfort of numbness.

I moved from stones to tatami, from wood to paper frames. Dust clung to my clothes, my hair, and the inside of my nose. My hands reddened, the skin softening and then roughening as the water leeched the oils away.

Outside, the winter sky darkened from steel to ink.

At some point my world narrowed to the next stroke of the rag, the next squeak of the bucket, the next faint, unpredictable snap of current along my nerves. My body shook even when the collar was quiet.

By the time I put the last bucket away and wrung out the final rag, my arms felt like they belonged to someone else. My knees threatened to fold.

The house looked different, though. The entry stones gleamed faintly. The corridors smelled of vinegar and wet wood instead of dust. The kitchen hearth was scraped clean and stacked with fresh wood. It was not perfect—no single person could do the work of an entire staff in one day—but it was as close as I could get without collapsing.

I shuffled to the center of the kitchen and sank to my knees.

The cold from the floor seeped up through my thin clothes. My back throbbed with each breath. My head felt light, detached, as if it might float away if not for the weight of the collar.

I clasped my hands loosely in my lap and lowered my gaze to the clean, worn boards.

The collar nipped me once when my eyes slipped closed. I gritted my teeth and focused on the surrounding sounds instead: the faint crack and hiss of embers dying in the stove, the whisper of wind outside the shutters, the distant drip of water in the pipes.

Time blurred.

At some point, the quiet snapped.

The front door slid open hard enough that I heard it from the kitchen—a sharp thud of wood against its frame. Cold air and noise tumbled in together.

Bootsteps. Two sets, uneven. One heavy, controlled. The other… dragging.

A muffled curse. Someone hit a wall with a dull thud. A pained sound, bitten off.

Every muscle in my body went tight.

Order: Wait in the kitchen. Do not move unless called.

The command was a cold weight in my mind, reinforced by the primary seal on my collar. It judged intent. To disobey was to invite a shock that would drop me where I stood.

Clan training: A disturbance at a clan official’s residence after dark is a potential threat.

I swallowed. My heart hammered, each beat sending a throb through my ribs. The collar lay cold and still against my neck.

If it was a thief, a spy, an assassin, and I did nothing…

If it was Akito returning with trouble, and I left my post…

My hands shook. I pressed them flat to my thighs until the tremors steadied.

Akito had not forbidden me from leaving the kitchen, I told myself. He had forbidden me from moving unless called. But if there was an emergency or an immediate threat to clan security, the higher duty was to investigate.

A body hit the floor down the corridor with a meaty thump. This time, the sound that followed was not quite stifled: a hoarse, familiar voice, rough with pain.

“—no… please—”

Sadi.

The decision made itself.

I rose, every movement careful, and padded toward the sound.

The collar did not react at first. Halfway down the dark corridor, it gave me a small, warning sting. I paused, muscles twitching.

The seal’s logic in my collar was absolute, but it wasn’t omniscient. It read my conscious purpose. So I closed my eyes and shaped my thoughts. Emergency. Threat to the household. Duty to the clan . I repeated it like a mantra, burying my fear beneath the cold, clean imperative of protocol. Then I moved again. Another few steps. No shock. I took a shaky breath.

The light in Akito’s study leaked through a narrow gap where the shōji was not fully closed. I slipped to the side of the door, bare feet silent on the boards, and peered through the narrow wedge.

It wasn’t an intruder.

Akito stood in the center of the room, his haori off, sleeves tied back. His hair had come slightly loose from its cord. Sadi knelt on the floor before him, slumped, one arm wrapped around his ribs. His mission blacks were torn and stained dark over one shoulder and thigh. Blood had soaked through the cloth. His face was bruised, his lip split, and one eye was already swelling shut.

“So,” Akito said, his voice very soft. “You failed.”

Sadi coughed, a wet, painful sound. “Target… escaped. I can still—”

“You can do nothing,” Akito snapped. It was the first real heat I’d heard in his voice all day. “You compromised the operation. You exposed yourself. You allowed witnesses to live. Do you understand how much effort went into arranging that contact?”

“I—I’ll fix it,” Sadi said quickly, curling in on himself as if expecting another blow. “Give me another chance, Father. I’ll go back out tonight. I won’t come home until—”

The kick caught him under the ribs, hard enough to lift him from the floor.

He folded around it with a strangled wheeze. I flinched in the shadows, my own ribs sparking with sympathetic pain.

Akito paced, hands clasped behind his back. “You are Masada’s deputy heir,” he said. “You bear my blood. Yet you fail at the simplest of assignments. Again.” He stopped, looking down at Sadi with cool contempt. “You disgust me.”

Sadi forced himself onto his hands and knees, coughing, each breath a sharp, ragged drag. “I—won’t—fail you—again—”

“Won’t you?” Akito knelt suddenly, one hand snapping out to grip the back of Sadi’s neck, forcing his face toward the floor.

Sadi went still, every line in his body going rigid.

I saw why when Akito’s other hand moved lower, shoving Sadi’s torn shirt up to expose the small of his back. Right at the base of his spine, burned into the skin like a brand, was a seal mark—small, intricate, dark against pale flesh. I could only see part of it from my angle, but the pattern made my stomach clench: interlocking circles, binding lines, the unmistakable flow of a compulsion array.

Akito didn’t look angry; he looked disappointed, which was far worse. He looked at Sadi the way a master swordsmith looks at a blade that has warped in the forge.

“A blade that cannot hold its edge under pressure is merely scrap metal, Sadi,” Akito said softly, his fingers hovering near the brand. “I am not being cruel; I am being thorough. If you cannot survive the fire I build for you here, you will never survive the world outside.”

He looked toward the door. “Even waste like Tadashi has a purpose. He is the whetstone. Use his failure to sharpen your resolve. If you cannot even be superior to a broken slave, then you are of no use to this clan.”

Akito’s fingers pressed, two of them settling precisely over the center sigil.

“Let me remind you,” he murmured, “what happens when you fail me.”

“No—” Sadi’s voice strangled into a single syllable. “Fath—”

Akito’s energy signature flared.

The seal ignited.

Sadi’s scream tore through the room, sharp and high and raw. His back arched violently, muscles seizing, fingers clawing uselessly at the tatami. His heels drummed against the floor as if trying to escape his own skin.

The noise seemed to vibrate through the collar around my neck. It buzzed abruptly, the sharp jolt making me clamp a hand over my own throat. My vision spotted black. Every instinct screamed at me to move, to do something.

But my legs would not obey.

Even if I stepped in—what then? Interpose myself between Akito and his son? Between a father and the discipline of his heir? Between a clan elder and his own blood?

The collar’s presence loomed large and invisible. There wasn’t a threat to the household, and Akito’s study was a restricted area for me. The longer I stood in that corridor, disobeying the order to wait in the kitchen, the more I expected its bite to turn from a nip to a full, skull-shaking shock.

Akito kept his fingers on Sadi’s seal for a breath longer, two, three—an eternity of shrieks.

Then, he released it.

Sadi collapsed like a puppet with its strings cut, limbs twitching weakly. Foam traced the corner of his mouth. His eyes rolled half-open, unfocused, then slid shut as his chest heaved.

“Pathetic,” Akito said, standing. He wiped his hand fastidiously on a hank of Sadi’s torn sleeve, as if discarding some filth. “Useless. Sometimes I wonder whether it is worth keeping you at all.”

He stepped over Sadi’s body without looking down, walked to the door on the far side of the study—his private room—and disappeared inside. The shōji slid closed behind him with careful, quiet precision.

Silence rushed back in, loud in my ears.

I waited, counting heartbeats. Ten. Twenty. Thirty. No sound came from behind the inner door. No flicker of movement.

The collar lay against my neck like a coiled snake, quiet. I gathered my thoughts. The son of my master was wounded. I didn’t mean to disobey. Hoping that it would be enough to stave off the collar’s strike, I moved.

Crossing the threshold felt like stepping into a forbidden shrine. The study smelled of ink, paper, and the harsh tang of burned power—the scent of active seals. Scrolls lined the walls in neat racks. The lamp on the desk still burned, its light pooling gold over an open report. I stepped around it, barefoot on tatami, and knelt beside Sadi.

He lay curled awkwardly on his side, one hand still half-clenched under his cheek. His breathing was shallow but steady, his face slack in unconsciousness. Sweat matted his hair to his temples.

“Forgive me,” I whispered as I lifted the torn edge of his shirt, the words barely air. The collar gave a sharp, warning zap—a stinging reminder of Akito’s first rule. I flinched, biting down on my tongue.

The seal at the base of his spine was clearer now, the skin around it reddened and slightly raised from recent activation. It wasn’t like the control array carved into my collar—those were metal-bound, adjustable. This was a direct brand upon flesh.

My training with seals, limited as it was, was enough to recognize the structure.

A slavery array.

Not the simple kind used for temporary restraints or short-term commands. This was deeper, multi-layered: obedience, pain induction, loyalty enforcement, likely even memory redaction if certain conditions were met. The lines of it spiraled inward, anchoring around a central character.

My fingers hovered a hair’s breadth above the mark, not quite touching as I sent the faintest thread of energy toward it.

The array quivered in response, humming like a plucked string. Information fluttered back along the line—a whisper of its bindings, the signature of the one who held the other end.

Master.

The sigil that formed at the core of the array was precise, unmistakable.

Akito’s personal mark.

My hand dropped to my thigh.

My own collar prickled in sympathy. My heart hammered against my ribs, the ache in my side forgotten for a moment beneath a different kind of shock.

All this time, Akito had not just been training his son harshly.

He had enslaved him.

Not metaphorically. Not in the way the elders liked to say we all belonged to the clan. Literally. A full obedience seal, keyed to his energy signature, burned into his own child’s flesh.

I stared at the mark at the base of Sadi’s spine, the delicate cruelty of its lines, and for the first time since this mission began, the pain in my body and the cold in my bones felt far away.

Akito Masamune was the master of his own son’s slavery.

S adi was heavier than he looked.

I hooked my hands under his arms and tried to leverage him up. My ribs screamed in protest; black dots crowded the edges of my vision. For a moment, I thought my arms would simply give out and drop him.

Forgive me, I apologized silently as I shifted my grip.

His head lolled against my shoulder, hot with fever. The copper tang of dried blood and sweat clung to him. One of his boots dragged, catching on the tatami.

Step by step, I half-walked, half-dragged him out of the study, down the corridor, and toward his room. It was the second door on the upper floor, facing the inner garden. The staircase was the worst part. Each step upward made my bruised ribs grind; my legs shook so badly that more than once I had to stop, chest heaving, and brace both of us against the wall.

The collar lay blessedly quiet around my neck. No reprimand for leaving the kitchen and moving Akito’s son. No random shock from the auxiliary seal yet.

Sadi’s room was warmer than the rest of the house. A brazier still glowed faintly in the corner, banked coals giving off a gentle heat. The sliding doors were closed, paper tight against the winter air.

I maneuvered him inside and eased him down onto the futon, trying not to jar his injuries. He groaned once, a low, incoherent sound. Fever flushed his cheeks, and sweat plastered dark hair to his forehead.

His clothes were a mess—mission blacks torn and stiff with half-dried blood. Not all of it was his, but enough was that I didn’t dare leave it.

Forgive me , I thought again, and began to undress him.

I worked quickly, my hands steady from long practice. I peeled the shirt away from a torn shoulder, using a knife from his belt to cut the fabric where it had stuck to a clotted wound. I eased his pants carefully over a swollen knee. Each movement risked waking him; each time he shifted or hissed under his breath, my heart stumbled.

Under the clothes, the damage was clearer. Bruises bloomed along his ribs and across his hip. A shallow but ugly cut scored his upper arm. Scrapes lined his hands and forearms—signs of a fight, a fall, or both.

And at the base of his spine, dark against his skin: the slavery seal. I forced myself not to look at it. Not now. That horror could wait. He needed care first.

I fetched water from the kitchen, my steps automatic despite the ache. I found clean cloths in a cedar chest and a small lacquered box of medical supplies—salve, bandages, and a pinch of fever powder—tucked neatly on a shelf.

I cleaned his wounds, wiping away blood and dirt with water that quickly turned pink, then brown. When I rinsed the cut on his arm with strong alcohol, he moaned, his brow furrowing, but he didn’t wake. I bound it with clean linen, checked his ribs with careful fingers—no obvious breaks—and smoothed salve over the worst of the bruises.

His skin burned under my touch. Fever.

I mixed the bitter-smelling powder with a little cooled tea I found in a covered pot, then cradled his head and coaxed the liquid between his lips. Some dribbled out the corner of his mouth, but he swallowed most of it on reflex.

When I was done, I pulled a fresh blanket over him and sat back on my heels, watching his face.

The room’s warmth seeped into my chilled bones, loosening muscles that had been clenched for hours. My own fever—one I’d been ignoring since the shocks at Master Kenta’s—floated to the surface now that the work was momentarily done. My head felt thick, stuffed with cotton. The collar was a dull weight around my throat, not yet buzzing.

Just for a little while, I told myself, I would sit here. Watch his breathing. Make sure the fever came down.

He shifted in his sleep, his features tightening and then smoothing again. I adjusted the blanket, tucking it a little higher.

The brazier’s steady glow blurred. The tatami under my knees softened into something distant. My eyelids drooped, despite the nagging thought that I should return to the kitchen, back to my designated place.

Warmth, after so long in the cold, was a dangerous luxury. I did not remember closing my eyes.

I jerked awake to the sound of a low groan.

For a disoriented second, I thought I was back in the dungeon and the sound my own. Then the weight of a blanket under my hands and the faint smell of cedar and ink—not mold and straw—pulled me back.

My hand flew to my neck. The collar was silent. No jolt for falling asleep. I’d been out long enough for the auxiliary seal—the one meant to keep me alert—to have run its course. Akito must have set it to expire at night, a small, cruel mercy to allow for basic rest.

Sadi shifted, his hand fumbling weakly at his side. His face screwed up in pain as he tried to push himself upright. I eased an arm behind his shoulders, helping him sit. He squinted at me, then around the room, as if confirming his surroundings.

“You—” His voice was rough, low. “Tadashi? What are you doing here? In my room?”

I swallowed. I couldn’t answer that without a permission to speak. He brushed off my arm, irritated. “Well? Answer me when I’m talking to you!”

I cleared my throat. The command was explicit enough.

“I…” I kept my tone as neutral as possible. “Head Akito requested my service in this house. I am assigned here until the New Year.”

Recognition flared in his eyes, followed quickly by something brighter: amusement.

“Oh.” His mouth curled. “So it’s true.”

I blinked.

“Father told me.” He leaned back against the wall, wincing, but the grin stayed. “The great clan heir on an atonement mission, running around as a slave. I thought he was exaggerating. But here you are.” His gaze swept over me, lingering on the collar. “Slumped on my floor like a servant.”

Heat flushed my face despite the chill in my bones. I straightened from my weary slouch but kept my head respectfully lowered. “I am fulfilling the elders’ judgment,” I said quietly. “As is my duty.”

He chuckled, then hissed as the movement tugged at his bruises. “Duty, huh.” His gaze sharpened with sudden mischief, or maybe cruelty. “Get up. Stand before me.”

I obeyed, pushing myself up from the tatami. My legs tingled, stiff from where I’d been sitting too long. I took two deliberate steps back, then turned to face him directly, putting a small but formal distance between the futon and myself.

He looked up at me from his pillows, eyes bright with fever and triumph. “Now. Bow.”

I hesitated for half a heartbeat, then folded at the waist, lowering my upper body in a deep, formal bow.

“Lower,” he said, his voice a silken command.

I sank first to my knees, then shuffled forward on them until I was beside his futon. I bent, pressing my forehead to the cool tatami near his feet, a position of absolute submission. The scent of cedar wood filled my senses, mixed now with the sharp, metallic taste of my own humiliation.

A soft, breathless laugh escaped him. “Call me ‘Master Sadi.’”

I said nothing, the words stuck in my throat like a fish bone.

“Master Sadi,” I finally forced out. The collar didn’t punish me for it.

“Again.”

“Master Sadi.”

He shifted, and I saw from the corner of my eye that he was leaning forward, watching me with open delight.

“Never thought…” he murmured. “I used to imagine this, you know. You, the perfect heir, always ahead of me. Father never letting me forget it. I thought it was just a stupid fantasy. Making you bow. Making you—”

The shōji slammed open.

Akito filled the doorway, his presence like a blade sliding into the room. I felt the weight of his gaze pin me to the tatami, cold and lethal, before it shifted toward the futon. Beside me, Sadi’s shallow, triumphant breathing hitched. The silence that followed was heavy with the sudden, sharp evaporation of his delight.

“I have been calling for you,” Akito said, his voice like ice. “Slave. You did not come.”

I lifted my head slightly. “Forgive—”

The collar detonated.

The world vanished in white.

Agony arced down my spine, up into my skull, and out into my limbs. My back snapped rigid; my hands clawed uselessly at empty air. I hit the floor, my cheek slamming into the tatami. Every muscle locked, then spasmed, then locked again. My vision stuttered: light, dark, light.

Somewhere through the roaring in my ears, I heard Sadi gasp. Akito’s sandals moved, measured, unhurried.

The shock cut off, leaving me panting, my chest heaving, my face pressed to the floor. My fingers twitched uncontrollably against the mat.

Akito loomed over us, his shadow long in the lamplight. He surveyed me once, then turned his gaze on Sadi.

“What,” he said softly, “are you doing?”

Color drained from Sadi’s face. “I—was just—”

“Using my property for your games.” Akito’s eyes narrowed. “You are grounded. Until I say otherwise, you will not leave this house. No missions. No training outside these walls.”

Sadi’s jaw tightened. “Father—”

“Do you intend to argue?” Akito asked.

Silence.

Akito turned back to me. “Get up.”

My arms shook as I pushed myself upright. The world tilted; I steadied myself with a hand on the wall.

“You will both work,” Akito said. “This house is still unacceptable. You”—a slight jerk of his chin toward Sadi—“will coordinate the interior. You”—his eyes pinned me—“will follow his directions and complete the tasks I assign, without error.”

He drew a folded slip of paper from his sleeve and let it fall at my feet.

“Additional duties,” he said. “Repair the kitchen roof. Clear snow from the upper eaves. The tiles are loose near the west end—fix them.”

I bowed, forcing the motion through the throbbing in my head.

He crouched slightly, fingers brushing the collar. His energy signature flared; the auxiliary seal he’d set yesterday stirred.

“I will renew the alert seal,” he said. “It seems I was too lenient.”

New threads of fire burrowed into the metal, then into the skin beneath. The collar buzzed once, a sharp, warning nip. Sadi flinched, his earlier delight extinguished.

“You will not sleep,” Akito said. “You will not hide. If you fail to complete this list by sunset, the consequences will fall on both of you equally.”

He straightened. “And, Sadi.” His voice cooled further, if that was possible. “You will not use my tools for your entertainment again. Unless I give you permission.”

He left without waiting for an answer, sliding the door shut with clean finality.

Silence settled again, thicker this time.

Sadi stared at the closed door, his hands balled into fists against the blanket. Fury and humiliation wavered on his face, chased quickly under by something else—wariness, maybe. Fear.

Then his gaze cut to me, sharp.

“How much did you see?” he demanded.

I lowered my head. “Pardon?”

“Last night.” His voice dropped. “In Father’s study. How much did you see?”

The slavery seal burned in my memory, its lines crisp and unforgiving.

“Enough to know,” I said quietly, “that you were in pain.”

His breath hissed between his teeth. For a moment, I thought he might call for Akito right then.

Instead, he leaned forward, his voice a harsh whisper. “You will not say a word about any of this. To anyone. Not Elder Low. Not Masada. Not your precious siblings. No one.”

“I am bound by house rules already,” I said. “Head Akito forbade me to speak of anything that happens here.”

“Good.” His tone took on a cruel edge. “Because if you do, I’ll make your life even more of a hell than it already is. I don’t care what Father’s stupid mission is. I’ll find ways.”

I bowed lower. “Understood.”

He glared at me a moment longer, then sagged back against the wall, his breath coming a little fast from the exertion.

“Bring me sake,” he said abruptly. “And food.”

“In the kitchen, there is only rice,” I said carefully.

“Then cook rice,” he snapped. “Make soup from what you can find. I don’t care. Just do it. And don’t touch so much as a grain for yourself unless I say you can.”

He waved a hand, already turning his face away. “And get out. I don’t want to look at you.”

I backed out of the room and slid the door closed.

The kitchen was as empty as it had been yesterday. I found a small sack of rice—barely enough for a single portion, a few dried fish heads in a jar, and some aging miso paste. That was all.

I rinsed the rice, set it to cook, and started a thin broth with the miso and fish heads, skimming off the scum as it rose. The smell made my stomach cramp painfully. I hadn’t eaten since… I couldn’t remember. Master Kenta’s house? A heel of stale bread from Oni’s guard?

My hands shook as I ladled the rice and soup into bowls and set them on a tray with a flask of warmed sake I’d found at the back of a cupboard. The collar nipped me once for moving too slowly.

Carrying the tray back to Sadi’s room took more concentration than it should have. Each step felt precarious, as if the floor might tilt away from under me at any moment.

He accepted the tray without looking at me. “Leave it. Go work.”

He didn’t speak to me again.

The rest of the morning blurred into a haze of motion and pain. Dusting beams, scrubbing floors, hauling buckets. The random shocks came more often now—small, sharp stings at unpredictable intervals that jerked my muscles and broke my rhythm. Once, as I balanced on a stool to clean the upper frames of a shōji, a zap hit the base of my skull. My hand slipped; I caught myself on the frame just in time.

Fever burned behind my eyes. My shirt clung to my back with sweat. Each breath rasped slightly in my chest. When I bent too fast, the room spun; when I straightened, blackness licked at the edges of my vision.

At some point, carrying a stack of folded cloths from one room to another, the collar nipped me again. My knee buckled. I lurched sideways, shoulder slamming into a low table. The impact rocked it; a vase leaped from its surface. Porcelain shattered on the floorboards.

The sound rang through the corridor like a bell.

I froze, my heart plummeting. White shards of a vase—simple but finely made—lay scattered at my feet, some stained with a drop of my own blood where a piece had nicked my ankle.

Footsteps thundered from down the hall.

Sadi appeared in the doorway, one hand braced on the frame, face pale but eyes sharp. “What was that?”

“I—” My tongue felt swollen. “Forgive me. I—tripped.”

He took in the broken vase and the way I was half-curled on the floor, one arm wrapped around my ribs.

“You’re unbelievable,” he said. He stepped forward and kicked me in the thigh, hard enough to jolt me but not to truly injure. “Lazy even as a slave.”

Pain flared; my leg jerked. I bit back a sound.

“I’m telling Father,” he added with petty satisfaction. “He’ll write it down. And then Masada will know you can’t even carry a vase without breaking it.”

I bowed from where I knelt, forehead almost to the floor despite the throbbing in my head. “I accept any punishment.”

He sneered. “My father will see to it. Clean the mess first. Get up.”

He grabbed my arm and hauled me to my feet. My vision swam; the room drooped sideways. He didn’t notice—or didn’t care. “Pick it up. All of it. And if I find so much as one shard left later, I’ll step on it and blame you for that, too.”

He watched long enough to see me kneel and start gathering the pieces, then turned away, muttering under his breath.

I knelt in the hall, scrubbing the area where the porcelain had shattered. My fingers were numb, and the vinegar-water stung the small cuts on my hands. By the time I swept up the last fragment and disposed of it, my hands were shaking so violently I could hardly hold the broom.

Some time later I felt a weight on the back of my neck that wasn’t the collar. When I looked up, Sadi was standing at the end of the corridor, partially hidden by the shadow of a pillar. He wasn’t yelling anymore. He was just… watching.

He looked at my hands—red and shaking—and then at the collar. His face was a closed book, but his lips were pressed thin, and his eyes held a strange, intense focus. Was that disgust? Curiosity? I couldn’t tell. He didn’t say anything when I gathered the bucket to move to the next room. He just stepped back into the shadows, his gaze following the trail of my wet footprints;

Afternoon light slanted thin and cold through the windows when I finally climbed onto the roof.

The tiles were icy under my bare feet, slick with a sheen of meltwater where the weak sun had touched them. The wind knifed through my damp clothes, stealing what little warmth I had left. From up here, the compound spread out in neat lines of courtyards and rooftops. A world of order I had failed to belong to.

The roof section Akito had mentioned was easy enough to find. Several tiles had shifted, leaving gaps where snow and rain could seep in. I checked my footing carefully, weight evenly distributed, and began the slow work of reseating them.

My fingers were numb within minutes. Each time I reached, my ribs spasmed. A cough clawed at my throat, tearing free in a raw, painful burst. The movement sent a bright bolt of agony through my side.

The collar chose that moment to bite.

A sharp jolt snapped into my neck and made my muscles jerk. My foot skidded on the slick tile; only a lucky grab at a roof beam kept me from sliding immediately off the edge.

My heart hammered. I pressed my forehead to the cold ceramic, forcing my breathing to slow.

I shouldn’t be up here.

I was too tired. Too sick. My thoughts moved sluggishly, like fish under ice.

But the list. The orders. The promise of another report with my failures itemized and carried to Father’s hands.

“You will not ask for lenience,” Father had said. The memory of his voice cut through the haze, colder than the wind. “Whatever hardship comes, you will endure it in silence. This is the weight of your failure.”

The weight was crushing me. It was in the collar, in my burning ribs, in the tremor of my hands that could barely hold a tile.

I shifted toward the last loose tile near the edge. Down below, the yard was a long way off. The packed earth looked hard. Final.

A thought, quiet and terrible, uncoiled in my mind: It would be so easy.

Not to jump. Just… not to catch myself. To let the skid become a fall. To let the weight drop.

The shame hit instantly, hot and nauseating. A dutiful son does not think such things. A protector does not lay down his arms.

The memory came as a sensory assault—the metallic scent of blood cutting through the phantom smell of cedar and snow. Mother’s face rose in my mind, the way she’d looked the last time I’d seen her, her hair lifted by the wind.

“Listen to me, Tadashi.” Her voice had been strained. I had understood every word of her dying commands. A final mission from a mother bleeding out on a forest floor.

Protect your siblings.

Take care of them, all right?

Now, run!

I had run. I had left her to death. And I had promised. So easily.

To look after Shiro and Raito. To make Father proud. Yet, here I knelt in other people’s houses with a collar around my neck.

I realized then that I had never actually stopped. I was still running. Running from my failure to save her. Running from my failure to be the strength she believed I was.

The cold thought returned, clearer now, seductive in its simplicity: If I fell, I would stop running. The mission would be over.

I wouldn’t have to fail them anymore. No more missed evaluations. No more reports. No more collars or whips or disappointed eyes. No more making promises I wasn’t strong enough to keep. The shame of leaving her, the shame of failing them all… it would end.

I could almost feel the relief. To finally, finally be allowed to stop fighting. To stop trying to be strong when every fiber of my being screamed to lie down.

I looked at the edge. The emptiness didn’t call with fear, but with a promise of peace . My grip on the tile loosened. Just a little. The wind bumped me, an accomplice.

But the memory of my mother’s voice tried to steady me.

“Whatever happens, don’t give up. Be strong.” Her arms were tight around me. She kissed my forehead gently. “Look after your siblings, Tadashi. I love you.”

I squeezed my eyes shut, my breath shuddering past my bruised ribs.

I’m sorry. I tried to be the strength you needed. I really did. But maybe I’m just not… that strong. Mother, I am so… tired. Please. I—

“Tadashi!”

Sadi’s voice cracked across the courtyard like a whip, shattering the memory.

I jerked violently, my heart slamming against my ribs.

“Are you done up there or are you sleeping?” he shouted, petty and present . “I’m not going to cover for you if Father comes home and sees the mess!”

The sharp annoyance in his voice cut through the fog.

Sleeping .

On duty.

I had been about to—

Horror flooded in, cold and clean.

What was I doing?

No. No, no, no. I had sworn. I had sworn .

GET UP. KEEP MOVING.

My fingers tightened convulsively on the tile. My breath came in ragged gasps. The wind gusted, tugging at my clothes. I swallowed hard, throat thick.

“I’m—almost finished!” I called back, my voice strangled. “Coming down now.”

I hurried, my fingers clumsy as I settled the last tile. The adrenaline spike made my hands shake worse. All I could think of was getting off the roof, getting my feet back on solid wood, away from the edge. I shuffled toward the ladder propped at the eaves, feet cautious on the slick tiles.

The collar chose that moment to snap again.

A sharp, unexpected sting at the base of my neck. My calf muscle spasmed. My foot skidded on a patch of meltwater—not toward the ladder, but sideways, toward the open edge.

For an instant, I was weightless over the void. The grey sky spun.

No.

Mother, I’m sorry—

And then I fell.
