Chapter 1

← Back to Masamune Clan Series [EN]

I woke with the taste of metal in my mouth and fire in my spine.

The shock slammed through every nerve: white, blinding, stripping the world down to pain and the ragged sound of my own choking. My body arched off the thin futon.

I couldn’t breathe. Couldn’t think. Every instinct screamed: submit, still, quiet —

Then, suddenly, nothing.

No burn. No current. Just the echo of it, like lightning etched into my muscles.

My hand flew to my throat before I was fully conscious.

Cold skin. Bare.

My fingers clawed desperately around my neck, searching for the weight that wasn’t there. The thick band of crude metal, the raw edges where it rubbed flesh to blood.

I found only sweat and the familiar ridges of old scars.

No collar.

I stayed there a moment, palm flattened against my throat, feeling my pulse thunder under my skin.

It felt wrong. Wrong to be able to swallow without obstruction. Wrong to draw a full breath without fear of sudden fire.

It felt like a dream.

I forced my eyes open. Darkness pressed in from every side.

The ceiling was too close; bare wooden planks, one of them warped and cracked. The air in the little storeroom was warm, stale, heavy with dust and the faint scent of old tatami.

My breath sounded too loud. The walls felt too close.

You’re home , I told myself. You’re in the Masamune house. Master Oni isn’t here .

The words didn’t sink in. My shoulders were still clenched, every muscle wired and waiting. It took effort to uncurl my fingers from my neck.

Control your breathing .

Master Oni’s voice rose unbidden in my head, smooth and cold, cutting clean through the fog of panic. I latched onto the command automatically.

In. Count to four.

My lungs fought me at first, still convinced they were being crushed. I forced the breath in anyway.

One. Two. Three. Four.

Hold.

The darkness pulsed against my vision. Sweat cooled on my skin.

Out. Slow.

I exhaled, dragging the air out of my lungs until my chest ached.

Again.

Again.

Thought followed the rhythm. The spiking panic smoothed into something thin and tight, like a string pulled too hard but not yet broken.

My back throbbed dully where Master Oni’s last whipping had reopened half-healed scars. The skin there felt too hot, too tight, like a badly fitted bandage. Every shift of my weight made something pull and sting. It didn’t help that the elders’ “physical conditioning” yesterday hadn’t been kind to fresh wounds.

They never are.

I stared up at the warped plank, letting my eyes adjust. A sliver of gray seeped in under the door; no windows here, only thin slits of the dark sky through the high vents.

My fingers brushed the inside of my right wrist. The skin there tingled faintly, a ghost sensation where chains used to bite.

You’ve returned , I thought again, more to the ceiling than to myself. They took the collar off. The chain too. You’re free .

The word sat strangely in my mind, as alien as the bare skin at my throat.

I shifted carefully onto my side, ignoring the protests from my back, and drew my knees toward my chest to make the small space feel less vast and less crushing at the same time. The futon rustled under me, thin stuffing already flattened from three nights of sleep.

Three days.

Three days since I stepped back through the main hall gates.

Three days since the punishment mission was concluded, my debt for abandoning the heir evaluation settled.

Last night they had “debriefed” me for five hours. Question after question, circling the same ground, peeling away every layer of action, intention, hesitation.

Not a single raised voice from them. No need. Polite words could flay as well as any whip. By the end, I had felt… exposed. As if they had peeled my skin away and were sifting through what was left with gloved fingers.

I’d bowed, I’d answered, I’d accepted every implied rebuke with the automatic grace drilled into me since childhood.

I had not dishonored the clan.

I had not complained.

They still looked at me like I was something sharp they couldn’t decide whether to sheath or discard.

You pledged your life, your strength, and your will , I reminded myself, the words of the oath looping through my mind with the steady, familiar cadence of prayer. You will endure all trials, bear all burdens, and sacrifice all that you are for the sake of the Masamune name .

This is what that means.

Something in my chest twisted, hot and raw. Not at the words themselves—they were bone-deep, part of me—but at the memory that rose with them.

Mother’s arms around me, the press of her cheek against my hair, the smell of soap and rice and the faint tang of blood from the wound she’d ignored so she could hold me a little longer.

“Be strong,” she’d whispered, voice shaking even though she was smiling. “Protect them for me, Tadashi. Promise?”

I had promised. I’d meant it with everything I was.

I still do.

I curled my fingers into the futon to stop them from shaking.

I couldn’t protect her.

My throat closed. I swallowed hard against it, against the familiar weight of that failure. There was no collar for the gesture to catch on, but the habit was too ingrained.

Across the hall, a floorboard creaked.

I froze, listening.

Soft footfalls. The quiet clink of a door sliding open, then shut.

Shiro.

Her energy signature was faint—she’d learned control in my absence—but still recognizable, a familiar hum in the house’s silence.

She always woke before dawn now, before anyone else, to run her own drills. She’d told me, the day I came back, that she wasn’t going to let anyone question her worth again.

I’d believed her. She’d improved more in a year and a half than I had in twice that.

Her rising meant it was nearly time for my own morning run and exercises. The elders had been very clear: I was to rebuild my physical capacity as quickly as possible.

Elder Low would evaluate me today. If he found me unprepared…

There had been no explicit threat. There didn’t need to be.

I exhaled, long and slow, until there was nothing left in my lungs.

Then I pushed myself upright.

Pain flared along my back, sharp as a knife. I bit it down before it could become sound.

Master Oni had taught me well; my body knew how to move around damage, how to fold into obedience even when it screamed.

Clothes. Stretch. Run.

I dressed in the dark, fingers finding familiar folds of cloth, the rough texture of the training uniform. The fabric rasped over the welts across my shoulders and spine. Each contact was a reminder, a tally of choices made.

Raito is still angry.

The thought came unbidden as I pulled my shirt over my head, the memory of yesterday’s brief exchange with him bright and fresh.

“Where were you?” he’d demanded, fists balled at his sides, eyes shining with something too tangled to name.

“Why didn’t you send word? Why didn’t you…” His voice had cracked.

I’d opened my mouth. The truth had pressed against my teeth.

I’m sorry. The mission was —

Pain had seared through my throat, sudden and sharp, the speech seal flaring like burning wire. The words had crumpled, scattered. I’d swallowed them back down, eyes watering, forced my expression to stay mild.

“I’m sorry,” I’d said instead, voice hoarse. “It was a mission, Raito. I—”

“That’s all you ever say,” he’d snapped. “Mission, clan, duty. Just say you didn’t care enough to come back. You’re not my brother anymore!”

He’d stormed off before I could stop him. Before I could endure the seal’s punishment long enough to force anything real out.

I tightened the belt at my waist now, pulling it snug enough to bite.

I do care. I always have.

It didn’t matter. The things I couldn’t say might as well not exist.

Outside, the first thin birdsong of morning threaded through the house’s silence. I slid the storeroom door open with care, waiting for the faint hitch of wood, guiding it past.

The corridor was a dim gray tunnel. Shiro’s door was closed now; she must already be in the courtyard, stretching.

I stepped out, closed the door behind me, and headed for the back exit.

The morning air hit me like cool water; clean and damp, smelling faintly of earth and distant rain. The sky above the compound walls was a deep, bruised indigo, with only a thin silver line beginning to bleed across the horizon.

My lungs expanded greedily, as if trying to make up for all the phantom strangling in my dreams.

I bowed briefly toward the main house, a habit I hadn’t quite shaken, then set off at a steady jog along the training route Father had drilled into me as a child.

Left at the well. Past the old maple. Around the outer wall twice.

Pain settled in quickly, a familiar companion. My back protested the jostling; the elders’ drills from yesterday had left my legs heavy, muscles torn and rebuilding. Every breath tugged at bruises along my ribs.

Good , I thought, leaning into it. Let it burn. Let it empty you.

By the third lap, movement was the only thing in my head.

When I finally stopped, sweat plastered my hair to my forehead and soaked the collar of my shirt. My pulse hammered in my ears. For a moment, standing in the yard with the sky lightening overhead, I almost felt… clear.

Then the scent of rice and grilled fish drifted from the kitchen window, and the knot in my stomach tightened. Not just from hunger.

I was late.

I wiped my face on my sleeve, rolled my shoulders to test how much the skin on my back would tolerate, then slid the rear door open and stepped inside.

Voices greeted me immediately: Raito’s higher, animated tones; Shiro’s quieter, more measured responses; the deeper rumble of Father’s when he did speak, though he was mostly silent these days at breakfast, reading reports even as he ate.

By the time I reached the dining room, their plates were almost empty.

I stopped at the threshold, straightened, and bowed low.

“Forgive me,” I said, keeping my gaze fixed on the tatami. My voice came out steady, if a little rough from the run. “I lost track of time during my exercises.”

The room went briefly quiet.

I could feel their attention like pressure on my skin. Shiro’s presence was warm and worried; Raito’s prickled, all sharp edges. Father’s was…contained. A wall more than a presence.

“Sit,” Shiro said softly after a beat. “I—”

“Tadashi.”

Father’s voice cut across hers.

I kept my head down a heartbeat longer, then straightened and dared to look.

He was looking at me.

For the first time since I’d returned—since that first night when he’d acknowledged my report and dismissed me in the same breath—his eyes were actually on my face, not somewhere over my shoulder or on the papers in his hand.

It was like being struck.

He looked older than I remembered. There were more lines at the corners of his eyes, the faintest silver at his temples. His expression, however, was as unreadable as ever.

Cool, composed, a little tired. In this light, he looked less like the formidable head of the clan and more like the father who had once knelt to straighten the knot of my obi before my first ceremony.

My chest clenched with a sudden, fierce rush of longing that I stamped down immediately.

Dangerous. Useless.

Weak.

I dropped my gaze to the table edge.

“Yes, Father,” I said.

“Has Elder Low seen you fit for duty?” He asked. His tone held nothing: no warmth, no anger, just a simple, flat question.

“Not yet,” I replied. “He will evaluate me this afternoon.”

A faint nod. He picked up his teacup, studying the surface of the liquid as if it held more interest than I did.

“Good. We have an interclan meeting tonight. You’ll attend.” He set the cup down with a soft click. “Be prepared. Ask Elder Mei for the agenda.”

A muscle under my eye twitched. “Yes, Father. Understood.”

He reached for a small envelope beside his plate and slid it across the table toward Shiro without looking at her. “Groceries,” he said. “Make sure the household is stocked for the week.”

Shiro accepted it with both hands. “Yes, Father.”

Father’s gaze flicked briefly to Raito, then away. “And you—mind your studies.” His eyes did not turn back to me, but his next words were directed in my direction. “Tadashi, see to your siblings.”

“I’ll escort Raito to the academy this morning, Father,” I said immediately.

Raito stiffened. Out of the corner of my eye, I saw his jaw tighten.

“I don’t need him to take me,” he burst out before Father could respond. “I’m almost eleven. I’m not a child.”

“Raito,” Shiro murmured, a warning in her tone.

He didn’t look at her. He was glaring at me, lower lip pushed forward a fraction. Anger and hurt both, thinly disguised as stubborn pride.

I met his eyes only for a second before lowering mine. The urge to apologize rose, thick and useless.

Father’s attention slid between us, assessing. For a heartbeat, the air in the room felt brittle.

“Very well,” he said at last. “Go by yourself.”

Relief flashed across Raito’s face, quickly masked.

Father’s gaze landed fully on me again. It was a brief, cool glance, but there was weight behind it, something unspoken.

The message was clear. I had been reading those silences all my life.

Shadow him. Make sure he’s safe. Do not be seen .

I nodded slightly, bowing my head in acquiescence.

Father’s attention was gone again as quickly as it had come. He rose smoothly to his feet.

“I’ll be at the compound until late,” he informed Shiro. “Send for me if anything urgent arises.”

“Yes, Father.”

He stepped past me without another word, without a touch or even the faintest brush of his sleeve. I kept my back straight, my hands at my sides, and didn’t let my eyes follow him as he left.

When the sound of his footsteps faded, the room seemed to breathe again.

“Tadashi,” Shiro said, her voice softer now. “I saved you a bowl of miso. Eat before you go.”

The concern in her tone warmed and cut at the same time.

I looked at the table. A small bowl sat near the far corner, steam no longer rising from it but the broth still faintly warm, a piece of grilled fish set neatly beside it.

My stomach cramped so hard it almost doubled me over. I hadn’t eaten since yesterday morning; the debriefing had run past dinner, and by the time they’d dismissed me, the kitchen had been empty, the pots already washed. I’d gone straight to my room, too drained to even search for leftovers.

I wanted that soup with a child’s intensity. The smell of fermented soy and green onion was dizzying.

But Raito already stood, grabbing his bag.

If I sat down now, if I took the time to eat, I’d lose the chance to shadow him discreetly. He walked fast when he was angry, and he was angry most of the time lately.

“It’s all right,” I said, putting a hand out to still Shiro when she half-rose. “I need to go. I’ll be late for the academy.”

“You’d finish in three minutes,” she argued quietly. “You’re shaking.”

I steadied my hands against my thighs. The tremor was small, but she always noticed more than she should.

“I’m fine,” I lied gently. “Thank you for saving it.”

Her mouth thinned, but she didn’t push further. She knew better than anyone how much I worried over Raito. How much I felt I owed him.

“At least drink some tea,” she tried.

I shook my head, already moving to stack the empty bowls and plates, muscle memory taking over. “Let me help you clean up instead. It’ll be quicker.”

Between the two of us, the table was cleared in under a minute. I carried dishes to the kitchen, rinsed my hands, and hurried back to my room to change.

My academy books were stacked neatly beside the futon; I scooped them up, slid the strap over my shoulder, and checked once—twice—that the mission scrolls and Elder Low’s summons were tucked safely into the inner pocket of my jacket.

Outside, the front door slid open and shut with a decisive thud. Raito didn’t like to linger.

I stepped into the hall, closed the storeroom door silently behind me, and moved toward the entrance at a pace just slower than a run.

“Be careful,” Shiro called softly from the dining room.

I nodded without turning. “You too.”

By the time I reached the street, Raito was already half a block ahead, his small figure stiff with determination, the morning light catching on his hair.

I fell into step on the opposite side of the road, far enough back that, to anyone watching, we were simply two separate students headed in the same direction.

The village was waking around us; shopkeepers lifting shutters, early vendors arranging their wares, the murmur of neighbors exchanging gossip over low fences.

I kept my eyes moving, cataloging faces, noting anything out of place.

An old habit. A necessary one.

Raito never looked back.

I’m sorry , I wanted to say, For everything I couldn’t do. For everything I still can’t say .

When we reached the academy gates, he passed through without slowing, merging into the stream of students his age.

I waited until I saw him greeted by friends I half-remembered, watched him laugh at something one of them said—too loud, too bright—and only then turned away.

The acceleration class building loomed on the far side of the courtyard, its shadow cutting a sharp line across the flagstones. I adjusted the weight of my books, straightened my shoulders despite the pull of the welts on my back, and walked toward it.

Elder Low’s evaluation waited this afternoon. The interclan meeting, tonight.

For now, there was only the classroom door ahead of me, and the familiar, suffocating expectation that I would not, could not , afford to fail.
