Tempered in Silence

Book 4 of Masamune Clan Specials

He survived the mission. Now he must survive coming home. Tadashi Masamune is everything a ninja heir should be: disciplined, obedient, lethal. No one sees the scars beneath the uniform. After eighteen months on a so-called punishment mission, Tadashi returns to the Masamune clan stripped of his collar but not his trauma. His father sees only a weapon reclaimed. His younger brother sees a stranger who abandoned him. And a cruel speech seal ensures that the truth of what Tadashi endured will never be spoken. Forced back into academy life, clan politics, and a brutal combat evaluation, Tadashi discovers that returning home does not mean returning to safety. Strength is expected. Silence is mandatory. And endurance is the only acceptable response to pain. In a world that forbids weakness, is it possible to heal without breaking everything that holds him together?***

I woke with the taste of metal in my mouth and fire in my spine.

The shock slammed through every nerve: white, blinding, stripping the world down to pain and the ragged sound of my own choking. My body arched off the thin futon.

I couldn’t breathe. Couldn’t think. Every instinct screamed: submit, still, quiet —

Then, suddenly, nothing.

No burn. No current. Just the echo of it, like lightning etched into my muscles.

My hand flew to my throat before I was fully conscious.

Cold skin. Bare.

My fingers clawed desperately around my neck, searching for the weight that wasn’t there. The thick band of crude metal, the raw edges where it rubbed flesh to blood.

I found only sweat and the familiar ridges of old scars.

No collar.

I stayed there a moment, palm flattened against my throat, feeling my pulse thunder under my skin.

It felt wrong. Wrong to be able to swallow without obstruction. Wrong to draw a full breath without fear of sudden fire.

It felt like a dream.

I forced my eyes open. Darkness pressed in from every side.

The ceiling was too close; bare wooden planks, one of them warped and cracked. The air in the little storeroom was warm, stale, heavy with dust and the faint scent of old tatami.

My breath sounded too loud. The walls felt too close.

You’re home , I told myself. You’re in the Masamune house. Master Oni isn’t here .

The words didn’t sink in. My shoulders were still clenched, every muscle wired and waiting. It took effort to uncurl my fingers from my neck.

Control your breathing .

Master Oni’s voice rose unbidden in my head, smooth and cold, cutting clean through the fog of panic. I latched onto the command automatically.

In. Count to four.

My lungs fought me at first, still convinced they were being crushed. I forced the breath in anyway.

One. Two. Three. Four.

Hold.

The darkness pulsed against my vision. Sweat cooled on my skin.

Out. Slow.

I exhaled, dragging the air out of my lungs until my chest ached.

Again.

Again.

Thought followed the rhythm. The spiking panic smoothed into something thin and tight, like a string pulled too hard but not yet broken.

My back throbbed dully where Master Oni’s last whipping had reopened half-healed scars. The skin there felt too hot, too tight, like a badly fitted bandage. Every shift of my weight made something pull and sting. It didn’t help that the elders’ “physical conditioning” yesterday hadn’t been kind to fresh wounds.

They never are.

I stared up at the warped plank, letting my eyes adjust. A sliver of gray seeped in under the door; no windows here, only thin slits of the dark sky through the high vents.

My fingers brushed the inside of my right wrist. The skin there tingled faintly, a ghost sensation where chains used to bite.

You’ve returned , I thought again, more to the ceiling than to myself. They took the collar off. The chain too. You’re free .

The word sat strangely in my mind, as alien as the bare skin at my throat.

I shifted carefully onto my side, ignoring the protests from my back, and drew my knees toward my chest to make the small space feel less vast and less crushing at the same time. The futon rustled under me, thin stuffing already flattened from three nights of sleep.

Three days.

Three days since I stepped back through the main hall gates.

Three days since the punishment mission was concluded, my debt for abandoning the heir evaluation settled.

Last night they had “debriefed” me for five hours. Question after question, circling the same ground, peeling away every layer of action, intention, hesitation.

Not a single raised voice from them. No need. Polite words could flay as well as any whip. By the end, I had felt… exposed. As if they had peeled my skin away and were sifting through what was left with gloved fingers.

I’d bowed, I’d answered, I’d accepted every implied rebuke with the automatic grace drilled into me since childhood.

I had not dishonored the clan.

I had not complained.

They still looked at me like I was something sharp they couldn’t decide whether to sheath or discard.

You pledged your life, your strength, and your will , I reminded myself, the words of the oath looping through my mind with the steady, familiar cadence of prayer. You will endure all trials, bear all burdens, and sacrifice all that you are for the sake of the Masamune name .

This is what that means.

Something in my chest twisted, hot and raw. Not at the words themselves—they were bone-deep, part of me—but at the memory that rose with them.

Mother’s arms around me, the press of her cheek against my hair, the smell of soap and rice and the faint tang of blood from the wound she’d ignored so she could hold me a little longer.

“Be strong,” she’d whispered, voice shaking even though she was smiling. “Protect them for me, Tadashi. Promise?”

I had promised. I’d meant it with everything I was.

I still do.

I curled my fingers into the futon to stop them from shaking.

I couldn’t protect her.

My throat closed. I swallowed hard against it, against the familiar weight of that failure. There was no collar for the gesture to catch on, but the habit was too ingrained.

Across the hall, a floorboard creaked.

I froze, listening.

Soft footfalls. The quiet clink of a door sliding open, then shut.

Shiro.

Her energy signature was faint—she’d learned control in my absence—but still recognizable, a familiar hum in the house’s silence.

She always woke before dawn now, before anyone else, to run her own drills. She’d told me, the day I came back, that she wasn’t going to let anyone question her worth again.

I’d believed her. She’d improved more in a year and a half than I had in twice that.

Her rising meant it was nearly time for my own morning run and exercises. The elders had been very clear: I was to rebuild my physical capacity as quickly as possible.

Elder Low would evaluate me today. If he found me unprepared…

There had been no explicit threat. There didn’t need to be.

I exhaled, long and slow, until there was nothing left in my lungs.

Then I pushed myself upright.

Pain flared along my back, sharp as a knife. I bit it down before it could become sound.

Master Oni had taught me well; my body knew how to move around damage, how to fold into obedience even when it screamed.

Clothes. Stretch. Run.

I dressed in the dark, fingers finding familiar folds of cloth, the rough texture of the training uniform. The fabric rasped over the welts across my shoulders and spine. Each contact was a reminder, a tally of choices made.

Raito is still angry.

The thought came unbidden as I pulled my shirt over my head, the memory of yesterday’s brief exchange with him bright and fresh.

“Where were you?” he’d demanded, fists balled at his sides, eyes shining with something too tangled to name.

“Why didn’t you send word? Why didn’t you…” His voice had cracked.

I’d opened my mouth. The truth had pressed against my teeth.

I’m sorry. The mission was —

Pain had seared through my throat, sudden and sharp, the speech seal flaring like burning wire. The words had crumpled, scattered. I’d swallowed them back down, eyes watering, forced my expression to stay mild.

“I’m sorry,” I’d said instead, voice hoarse. “It was a mission, Raito. I—”

“That’s all you ever say,” he’d snapped. “Mission, clan, duty. Just say you didn’t care enough to come back. You’re not my brother anymore!”

He’d stormed off before I could stop him. Before I could endure the seal’s punishment long enough to force anything real out.

I tightened the belt at my waist now, pulling it snug enough to bite.

I do care. I always have.

It didn’t matter. The things I couldn’t say might as well not exist.

Outside, the first thin birdsong of morning threaded through the house’s silence. I slid the storeroom door open with care, waiting for the faint hitch of wood, guiding it past.

The corridor was a dim gray tunnel. Shiro’s door was closed now; she must already be in the courtyard, stretching.

I stepped out, closed the door behind me, and headed for the back exit.

The morning air hit me like cool water; clean and damp, smelling faintly of earth and distant rain. The sky above the compound walls was a deep, bruised indigo, with only a thin silver line beginning to bleed across the horizon.

My lungs expanded greedily, as if trying to make up for all the phantom strangling in my dreams.

I bowed briefly toward the main house, a habit I hadn’t quite shaken, then set off at a steady jog along the training route Father had drilled into me as a child.

Left at the well. Past the old maple. Around the outer wall twice.

Pain settled in quickly, a familiar companion. My back protested the jostling; the elders’ drills from yesterday had left my legs heavy, muscles torn and rebuilding. Every breath tugged at bruises along my ribs.

Good , I thought, leaning into it. Let it burn. Let it empty you.

By the third lap, movement was the only thing in my head.

When I finally stopped, sweat plastered my hair to my forehead and soaked the collar of my shirt. My pulse hammered in my ears. For a moment, standing in the yard with the sky lightening overhead, I almost felt… clear.

Then the scent of rice and grilled fish drifted from the kitchen window, and the knot in my stomach tightened. Not just from hunger.

I was late.

I wiped my face on my sleeve, rolled my shoulders to test how much the skin on my back would tolerate, then slid the rear door open and stepped inside.

Voices greeted me immediately: Raito’s higher, animated tones; Shiro’s quieter, more measured responses; the deeper rumble of Father’s when he did speak, though he was mostly silent these days at breakfast, reading reports even as he ate.

By the time I reached the dining room, their plates were almost empty.

I stopped at the threshold, straightened, and bowed low.

“Forgive me,” I said, keeping my gaze fixed on the tatami. My voice came out steady, if a little rough from the run. “I lost track of time during my exercises.”

The room went briefly quiet.

I could feel their attention like pressure on my skin. Shiro’s presence was warm and worried; Raito’s prickled, all sharp edges. Father’s was…contained. A wall more than a presence.

“Sit,” Shiro said softly after a beat. “I—”

“Tadashi.”

Father’s voice cut across hers.

I kept my head down a heartbeat longer, then straightened and dared to look.

He was looking at me.

For the first time since I’d returned—since that first night when he’d acknowledged my report and dismissed me in the same breath—his eyes were actually on my face, not somewhere over my shoulder or on the papers in his hand.

It was like being struck.

He looked older than I remembered. There were more lines at the corners of his eyes, the faintest silver at his temples. His expression, however, was as unreadable as ever.

Cool, composed, a little tired. In this light, he looked less like the formidable head of the clan and more like the father who had once knelt to straighten the knot of my obi before my first ceremony.

My chest clenched with a sudden, fierce rush of longing that I stamped down immediately.

Dangerous. Useless.

Weak.

I dropped my gaze to the table edge.

“Yes, Father,” I said.

“Has Elder Low seen you fit for duty?” He asked. His tone held nothing: no warmth, no anger, just a simple, flat question.

“Not yet,” I replied. “He will evaluate me this afternoon.”

A faint nod. He picked up his teacup, studying the surface of the liquid as if it held more interest than I did.

“Good. We have an interclan meeting tonight. You’ll attend.” He set the cup down with a soft click. “Be prepared. Ask Elder Mei for the agenda.”

A muscle under my eye twitched. “Yes, Father. Understood.”

He reached for a small envelope beside his plate and slid it across the table toward Shiro without looking at her. “Groceries,” he said. “Make sure the household is stocked for the week.”

Shiro accepted it with both hands. “Yes, Father.”

Father’s gaze flicked briefly to Raito, then away. “And you—mind your studies.” His eyes did not turn back to me, but his next words were directed in my direction. “Tadashi, see to your siblings.”

“I’ll escort Raito to the academy this morning, Father,” I said immediately.

Raito stiffened. Out of the corner of my eye, I saw his jaw tighten.

“I don’t need him to take me,” he burst out before Father could respond. “I’m almost eleven. I’m not a child.”

“Raito,” Shiro murmured, a warning in her tone.

He didn’t look at her. He was glaring at me, lower lip pushed forward a fraction. Anger and hurt both, thinly disguised as stubborn pride.

I met his eyes only for a second before lowering mine. The urge to apologize rose, thick and useless.

Father’s attention slid between us, assessing. For a heartbeat, the air in the room felt brittle.

“Very well,” he said at last. “Go by yourself.”

Relief flashed across Raito’s face, quickly masked.

Father’s gaze landed fully on me again. It was a brief, cool glance, but there was weight behind it, something unspoken.

The message was clear. I had been reading those silences all my life.

Shadow him. Make sure he’s safe. Do not be seen .

I nodded slightly, bowing my head in acquiescence.

Father’s attention was gone again as quickly as it had come. He rose smoothly to his feet.

“I’ll be at the compound until late,” he informed Shiro. “Send for me if anything urgent arises.”

“Yes, Father.”

He stepped past me without another word, without a touch or even the faintest brush of his sleeve. I kept my back straight, my hands at my sides, and didn’t let my eyes follow him as he left.

When the sound of his footsteps faded, the room seemed to breathe again.

“Tadashi,” Shiro said, her voice softer now. “I saved you a bowl of miso. Eat before you go.”

The concern in her tone warmed and cut at the same time.

I looked at the table. A small bowl sat near the far corner, steam no longer rising from it but the broth still faintly warm, a piece of grilled fish set neatly beside it.

My stomach cramped so hard it almost doubled me over. I hadn’t eaten since yesterday morning; the debriefing had run past dinner, and by the time they’d dismissed me, the kitchen had been empty, the pots already washed. I’d gone straight to my room, too drained to even search for leftovers.

I wanted that soup with a child’s intensity. The smell of fermented soy and green onion was dizzying.

But Raito already stood, grabbing his bag.

If I sat down now, if I took the time to eat, I’d lose the chance to shadow him discreetly. He walked fast when he was angry, and he was angry most of the time lately.

“It’s all right,” I said, putting a hand out to still Shiro when she half-rose. “I need to go. I’ll be late for the academy.”

“You’d finish in three minutes,” she argued quietly. “You’re shaking.”

I steadied my hands against my thighs. The tremor was small, but she always noticed more than she should.

“I’m fine,” I lied gently. “Thank you for saving it.”

Her mouth thinned, but she didn’t push further. She knew better than anyone how much I worried over Raito. How much I felt I owed him.

“At least drink some tea,” she tried.

I shook my head, already moving to stack the empty bowls and plates, muscle memory taking over. “Let me help you clean up instead. It’ll be quicker.”

Between the two of us, the table was cleared in under a minute. I carried dishes to the kitchen, rinsed my hands, and hurried back to my room to change.

My academy books were stacked neatly beside the futon; I scooped them up, slid the strap over my shoulder, and checked once—twice—that the mission scrolls and Elder Low’s summons were tucked safely into the inner pocket of my jacket.

Outside, the front door slid open and shut with a decisive thud. Raito didn’t like to linger.

I stepped into the hall, closed the storeroom door silently behind me, and moved toward the entrance at a pace just slower than a run.

“Be careful,” Shiro called softly from the dining room.

I nodded without turning. “You too.”

By the time I reached the street, Raito was already half a block ahead, his small figure stiff with determination, the morning light catching on his hair.

I fell into step on the opposite side of the road, far enough back that, to anyone watching, we were simply two separate students headed in the same direction.

The village was waking around us; shopkeepers lifting shutters, early vendors arranging their wares, the murmur of neighbors exchanging gossip over low fences.

I kept my eyes moving, cataloging faces, noting anything out of place.

An old habit. A necessary one.

Raito never looked back.

I’m sorry , I wanted to say, For everything I couldn’t do. For everything I still can’t say .

When we reached the academy gates, he passed through without slowing, merging into the stream of students his age.

I waited until I saw him greeted by friends I half-remembered, watched him laugh at something one of them said—too loud, too bright—and only then turned away.

The acceleration class building loomed on the far side of the courtyard, its shadow cutting a sharp line across the flagstones. I adjusted the weight of my books, straightened my shoulders despite the pull of the welts on my back, and walked toward it.

Elder Low’s evaluation waited this afternoon. The interclan meeting, tonight.

For now, there was only the classroom door ahead of me, and the familiar, suffocating expectation that I would not, could not , afford to fail.

B y the time the break bell rang, my hand was cramping from writing.

The sharp clang cut through the low murmur of the classroom. Chairs scraped back, feet shuffled, the air filled with the rustle of books and paper. Students poured toward the door in loose clumps, already talking about bento boxes and the new dango stall by the east gate.

I didn’t move.

My pen hovered over the page a heartbeat longer as the room emptied around me, then dropped back into motion. The line of text I’d been writing blurred for a moment, then came back into focus.

Focus.

I wrote the next sentence of my geography summary in neat, careful strokes, ignoring the hollow ache gnawing at my stomach.

Father hadn’t given me my allowance this morning. He hadn’t said anything about it—just passed Shiro the grocery envelope and walked out without a glance in my direction.

It wasn’t the first time. It wouldn’t be the last.

I shoved back the hunger. My body was trained to operate on less. It always had.

I shifted slightly in my seat, careful of my back. The welts there had stiffened over the morning, each movement tugging something too tight. I rolled my shoulders once, then stilled as a twinge shot across my spine.

Endure.

Across the room, someone snickered. Another voice chimed in, low but not low enough.

“Look, he’s not moving. Guess the little lord isn’t allowed outside without his minder.”

“Maybe he’s too stupid to find the cafeteria,” another replied. “He had to redo the year, didn’t he?”

The words pricked, but only faintly. I’d heard worse, from mouths that came with whips and collars and contracts, not wooden desks and ink-stained fingers.

I kept my eyes on my paper and continued writing.

The acceleration class was half-empty now, sunlight slanting across the ordered rows of desks. It was a prestigious program; compressed curriculum, heavier assignments, higher expectations. Students came from all over the continent to enroll: heirs of minor clans, children of wealthy merchants, a few foreign scholarship kids with accents that still stood out.

Most of them were older than me. To them, I was new. A stranger. A name they knew from clan politics and rumor, attached to a face they’d never seen in their classroom.

“Did you hear?” a girl near the front whispered to her friend, not as quiet as she thought. “He disappeared for a year and a half because he failed his exams. His father bribed the principal to keep him in the acceleration track.”

“Seriously? That’s so unfair.”

“I mean, what else could it be? Nobody misses that much class and doesn’t get expelled. My cousin said the Masamune clan basically bribes half the board.”

I turned a page in my notebook, making no sign I’d heard.

The truth was stranger than the rumor, and less flattering.

I hadn’t failed exams. I’d just failed the clan.

The punishment mission hadn’t appeared on any academy record. As far as these students were concerned, I’d simply vanished one term and reappeared in theirs.

They were curious. I could feel their glances, quick and sideways, whenever I raised my hand to answer a question. But curiosity only went so far. No one had chosen the empty seat beside me in the back row.

Too much trouble, I supposed. Too many eyes on a Masamune heir.

Better to watch from a distance than risk getting entangled.

A shadow fell across my desk.

I finished the sentence I was writing—“The western trade routes were formalized under the third daimyo”—and only then looked up.

Ginzo stood there, one hand braced on the next desk over, the other shoved into his pocket with calculated casualness. Two of his usual satellites flanked him. A third hung back, lounging against the windowsill with arms folded.

Ginzo was the oldest in the class, seventeen and failing to hide it. He wore the standard academy uniform jacket, but the buttons were undone, showing a patterned shirt beneath that probably cost more than most students’ entire wardrobes. A gold ring winked on his thumb as he drummed it against the desk.

Son of a Kawa Kingdom merchant, I’d heard. Rich. Indulged. Too used to getting his way to accept that strength was something you had to earn instead of buy.

“Masamune,” he drawled, stretching my surname into something lazy and faintly mocking. “You planning to marry that textbook, or did your daddy forget to pack your lunch with your honor today?”

A couple of the lingering students snickered. Others glanced over and then quickly away.

I capped my pen and laid it neatly beside my notebook.

“I’m reviewing for the history exam,” I said, keeping my tone mild. “The last unit was extensive.”

It wasn’t an answer to his real question, and we both knew it.

He leaned in a little, eyes searching my face for a flinch, a crack.

“You deaf as well as dumb?” he asked. “I asked if you were too scared to go outside. Or is the big bad cafeteria too much for the clan heir?”

“I don’t think the cafeteria poses a threat to anyone,” I replied. “Unless the curry is particularly bad today.”

One of his lackeys snorted before catching himself.

Ginzo’s mouth tightened for a fraction of a second. Then he flashed a smile that didn’t touch his eyes.

“Funny,” he said. “So you can talk. I was starting to think maybe your father bought you a place here and forgot to include a brain in the package.”

My jaw wanted to clench. I kept it relaxed.

“If my presence here bothers you,” I said, “you’re welcome to take it up with the administration. I’m sure they’d be interested in hearing your concerns about their entrance criteria.”

His smile thinned.

“Administration doesn’t scare me,” he said. “Rich people talk to rich people. They understand each other.”

His gaze flicked to the crest embroidered on my jacket, then back to my face.

“But you… you just stand there and everyone bends over. Must be nice.”

“I assure you, very few people bend over for me,” I said before I could stop myself. “Most of them would prefer I disappear.”

A couple of the nearby students shifted, startled by the honesty.

Ginzo’s eyes narrowed. He straightened slightly, choosing a new angle.

“So which is it, then?” he asked, voice carrying now. “Are you redoing the year because you’re stupid, or because you were too busy polishing your family’s reputation to bother showing up?”

“I’m catching up on missed material,” I said. “I’m not redoing the year.”

“Right,” he said, drawing the word out. “Because you’re special. All of us have to pass our exams like peasants, but you—” he raised his voice theatrically— “you get to vanish for a year and a half and waltz right back into the acceleration class. Must be all that…Masamune money.”

Soft laughter rippled around the room. No one joined in with much enthusiasm, but no one objected either.

My fingers curled slightly against the underside of the desk, out of sight.

He wasn’t entirely wrong. Clan influence had smoothed paths for me that others had to claw their way along. They’d never see the cost of that smoothing.

“I’m sure if you asked the principal,” I said quietly, “he’d let you take eighteen months off and see how it affects your grades.”

Ginzo’s eyes sharpened. He’d heard the edge under the politeness.

Before he could answer, the boy by the window pushed off the sill and sauntered forward.

“Oi, Ginzo,” he said, voice higher, more nasal. “Why waste your time? He’s not worth it.”

He came into clearer view as he stepped around Ginzo’s shoulder.

My breath stopped.

Erachi.

Not the name I’d called him by then—I’d never been allowed to use the master’s son’s name—but the face was the same. Slightly rounder now, a little less baby fat around the cheeks, but the sharp little eyes, the crooked front tooth, the way his upper lip curled when he sneered—

His father’s son.

For a heartbeat, the classroom dissolved.

The clean wooden desks vanished, replaced by carpet rolls stacked to the ceiling, the air heavy with dust and dye. Master Kenta’s shop. The slap of his sandals on the stone floor. The electric crackle of the collar’s seal being activated.

“Erachi,” Kenta’s voice, oiled and amused. “Our pet thinks he can fold faster if we let him rest his hands. Why don’t you remind him what happens to lazy animals?”

A jolt through the collar, my body locking and going limp at the same time, the world reduced to white-hot static—

A foot slamming into my ribs. A boy’s laugh, too high and thin.

“Get up, dog,” Erachi had jeered, his face flushed with excitement. “Or I’ll tell Father you bit me again.”

I dragged myself back to the present with an effort that felt like lifting a boulder.

Erachi was in front of me now, in an academy uniform, not rich merchant silks. No collar on my neck.

My fingers rose, unthinking, to the base of my throat, grazing the fabric of my turtleneck. The faint, raised ring of scar beneath the cloth seemed to burn under my touch.

He doesn’t know, I told myself. The collar had a disguise seal. He never saw my real face. To him, I was a nameless slave with different eyes, different hair.

He can’t recognize you.

“Look at him,” Erachi said now, smirking. “Shaking. Maybe he really is scared to go outside.”

He stepped in close, crowding my space, the same way he’d done in the back room of the carpet shop when his father wasn’t looking. His hand shot out suddenly and shoved me in the shoulder.

I let the force carry me rather than resist it. It still sent me stumbling back against the wall.

Pain flared along my spine as abused skin hit solid wood. For a second, my vision tunneled.

Don’t move. Don’t react. Stay still and it’ll be over faster —

The slave training overlapped with long-suppressed instincts that screamed the opposite: break his wrist, drive your elbow into his throat, end the threat before it multiplies .

I dug my fingernails into my palms, the small pain a spur to clarity.

You can’t. If you hurt him, the elders will hear. Father will —

“Careful,” I said, my voice even despite the tightness in my chest. “If you push too hard, you might hurt yourself.”

Erachi blinked, thrown for a moment by the composure.

Ginzo laughed. “Oh? The little heir’s got a tongue after all.”

He reached casually across my desk and plucked up the top sheet of paper.

My essay.

My heart lurched.

I’d stayed up past midnight writing that, cramming paragraphs between evening drills and dawn exercises. Anko had assigned it as a make-up for the units I’d missed during my absence. “No extensions,” she’d said. “Being behind is your problem, not mine.”

If I didn’t hand it in today, my marks would suffer. The elders monitored my academy performance almost as closely as my mission reports. A failing grade here was another lash waiting at home.

“What’s this?” Ginzo asked, waving the paper lightly. “Love letter to the principal? Or an apology to the rest of us for wasting our time?”

“I’d appreciate it if you didn’t touch that,” I said. Somehow my voice stayed quiet. “It’s due this period.”

He ignored me, eyes scanning the neat lines.

“‘Analyze the political impact of the second trade war on interclan relations,’” he read aloud in a mock-ponderous tone. “Wow. Look at all these big words. Did your cousin write this for you?”

A flicker of movement at the corners of the room—students inching closer for a better view. The circle around us tightened, leaving me pinned between the wall and a half-ring of bodies.

“I’m fairly certain Anko-sensei would recognize her own writing,” I said.

His grin widened. “Nah. Everyone knows she does everything for you. She probably wipes your ass too, Heir.”

The laughter this time had more bite.

Erachi shifted his weight, shoulder pressing harder into my chest. The contact sent a spike of pain through my bruised ribs.

“Give it back,” I said.

Ginzo’s eyebrows went up. “And if I don’t?”

My stomach knotted. I could see how this would play out. He’d rip the essay in half, maybe into quarters, enjoying the spectacle. I’d stand there and endure, because raising a hand would make it worse.

Then I’d stand in front of Anko with nothing to turn in. Her disappointment—sharp, clinical—would cut deeper than any strap. The elders would hear. There would be questions about my “discipline,” about whether the punishment mission had taught me anything at all.

Either way, I bled.

“If you destroy it,” I said carefully, “then I’ll still have to explain to Anko-sensei why I don’t have my assignment. She will ask who last saw it.” I met his eyes. “Are you sure you want that attention?”

For a moment, something wavered. He hesitated, weighing the satisfaction of tearing it up against the nuisance of staff involvement.

Then his jaw set.

“Please,” he said. “Sensei loves me. All the teachers do. I’m their favorite merchant’s little boy, remember?”

His fingers tightened on the paper.

“Besides,” he added, voice dropping, “you really ought to learn what happens when you don’t keep your things close, Masamune.”

He raised his other hand to grip the top of the sheet.

The sound of paper about to be torn is subtle—a faint stretch, a soft protest of fibers under tension. It hit me like a blade across the throat.

Something inside me went very, very still.

If you stand here and let him, you accept the beating from the elders as deserved.

If you stop him, you risk another kind of punishment.

There is no path without pain.

So choose the one where something is saved.

My body moved before the thought fully finished.

I shifted my weight off the wall, slipping sideways under Erachi’s arm instead of pushing back against it. He stumbled half a step as his support vanished, bumping into one of the other boys. Their elbows tangled.

“Hey—!”

I ducked low, letting their confusion hide my motion. My hand shot out, not for Ginzo’s wrist—that would be too obvious—but for the edge of the desk between us.

I hooked my fingers under it and gave the slightest jerk.

The desk’s front leg lifted just enough. Ginzo, caught mid-tear, had his weight on his toes. The shift unbalanced him. His arm swung out to compensate—

—and the paper slipped from his grasp, fluttering up and away in a brief arc.

I straightened into that arc. My fingers closed on the sheet just as it began to fall.

“What the—”

Ginzo swore, lunging for me, his face flushing dark with embarrassment and anger.

“I warned you,” I said softly.

I stepped back as he came forward. Not far. Just enough that when his foot, already off-balance from the tipping desk, landed, it came down on the strap of an abandoned satchel on the floor.

His heel slid.

Instinct screamed at me to steady him. Training whispered that accidents were sometimes permitted.

I pivoted aside.

His body twisted as he tried to catch himself. He went down hard on one knee, his hand slamming into the floor to break his fall. I heard a sharp intake of breath—pain, not serious, but not pleasant either.

Silence slammed over the room for a heartbeat.

Then the bell rang, shattering it. Break was over.

Chairs squeaked, voices rose in a sudden flurry as students scrambled for their seats. No one wanted to be caught standing when a teacher walked in.

They moved around us like water around stones, carefully not looking too closely. I saw the quick, sidelong glances anyway, the way they edged their desks a little farther away from mine.

Ginzo hissed through his teeth, cradling his wrist as he pushed himself up. A flush burned high on his cheekbones.

“You little—” he began.

The classroom door slid open with a brisk rattle.

Anko stepped through, scrolls tucked under one arm, a stack of graded papers in the other. Her gaze swept the room in a practiced scan, taking in the disarray—the half-standing students, Ginzo straightening with a wince, me a pace away with my books scattered at my feet.

Her eyes paused on us.

“Is there a problem?” she asked.

Her tone was even, but I knew her too well not to hear the steel under it.

I bent immediately, gathering my fallen notebooks into a tidy stack.

“No, Anko-sensei,” I said, straightening. “Ginzo stumbled over a bag. I was just about to help him up.”

Her gaze flicked to Ginzo. “Is that so?”

Color stained his ears. For all his bluster, admitting that he’d been nearly taken out by a skinny student with a misplaced satchel—and in front of a teacher, no less—would be worse than keeping quiet.

He clicked his tongue, looking away. “Tch. Floor’s uneven,” he muttered. “I’m fine.”

Anko held his eye a breath longer, then nodded once. “Find your seat before I decide to test your balance with a pop quiz.”

A ripple of relieved laughter moved through the room.

She turned to the front, setting the scrolls on her desk. “Assignments on my table, please. If you forgot yours, congratulations—you’ve just volunteered to write two extra essays by tomorrow.”

I stepped forward, smoothed my retrieved paper one last time to remove the worst of the creases, and placed it on the growing pile.

Her hand brushed mine briefly as she took it. A tiny shock went through me; for a moment, I thought she might say something, ask for an explanation.

She didn’t. Her face was as impassive as stone.

“Back to your seat, Tadashi,” she said.

“Yes, Sensei.”

As I walked down the aisle, I heard the whisper start up at the back.

“No wonder his grades are good,” someone murmured. “Anko-sensei is his cousin. Of course she goes easy on him.”

“Teacher’s pet,” another snickered. “Must be nice.”

I kept my expression neutral, my steps measured. Inside, something between a laugh and a sigh twisted in my chest.

If only they knew.

Anko did not go easy on me. Never had. If anything, she was harsher—a relentless, unyielding force who refused to accept anything less than perfection because “you’re Masamune; the world will always expect more.”

Late essays meant double work. Wrong answers in class meant extra drills after school. When I stumbled, she didn’t offer a hand; she handed me another weight and told me to keep climbing.

It wasn’t cruelty. It was… faith, in her own hard way.

I slid into my seat at the back. The chair creaked softly. The students on either side shifted their desks an inch farther away, as if my presence might be contagious.

Out of the corner of my eye, I saw Ginzo flex his wrist and glare at me, humiliation simmering in his eyes.

“This isn’t over,” he muttered under his breath as Anko began her lecture.

I didn’t look at him.

“Open your textbooks to page one hundred and twelve,” Anko said, chalk tapping briskly against the board. “We’ll be discussing the socioeconomic aftermath of the second trade war. Masamune, you’ll summarize last week’s reading.”

“Yes, Sensei.”

I stood, the familiar weight of expectation settling onto my shoulders like a cloak.

My back ached. My stomach was empty. My hands still remembered the shape of a collar long since removed.

It didn’t matter.

A Masamune endcures all trials, bears all burdens.

I drew a breath and began to speak.

B y the time I cleared the academy doors, the sun was already dropping toward late afternoon.

Too late.

Anko had kept me after class to quiz me on treaties I hadn’t been present to study when they were signed. I’d answered every question I could, apologized when I couldn’t, bowed, and then run.

Now my lungs burned and my legs felt hollow as I cut across the courtyard toward the main gate.

“Masamune?”

I skidded to a halt.

Saki Fuma stood just outside the academy gate. Her long hair was tied up in a high tail, clan crest clear on her uniform. A small leather satchel was slung across her chest.

She took one look at me and frowned.

“You look like you’re about to fall over,” she said. “You’re pale.”

“I’m fine,” I lied, trying to catch my breath without gasping. “I’m just—late.”

“For what?”

“Clan matters. I’m supposed to be at the clan hall in—” I checked the angle of the sun, swallowed. “Seven minutes.”

She winced. “Ouch.”

I inclined my head. “If you’ll excuse me—”

I stepped to go around her. She moved neatly into my path again, eyes narrowing.

“Have you eaten?” she asked.

I hesitated a fraction too long.

Her expression sharpened. “You haven’t. Of course you haven’t. Honestly, Masamune men…” She shook her head, already pulling her satchel around. “Here. One second.”

“Fuma, truly, I don’t—”

“Stop.” She fished out a small glass vial, its contents a dark, murky green. She uncorked it; a sharp, herbal smell hit my nose—bitter, with something warm underneath.

She held it out.

“Drink.”

I looked from the bottle to her face. “What is it?”

“Herbal tonic,” she said. “For stamina. I make it myself. It tastes terrible, but it works. Ask any of my cousins.”

I believed the first part immediately.

“I don’t wish to take your supplies,” I said.

Her eyes flashed. “Don’t make me order you as Fuma heir.”

There was humor in her tone, but also steel. And the clock was still ticking.

I bowed slightly and took the vial.

The liquid was worse than it smelled—bitter, astringent, with a strange, earthy aftertaste—but it was warm going down, heat unfurling in my stomach and then spreading outward in a slow wave. My fingers, chilled from adrenaline and fatigue, tingled.

I handed the vial back, trying not to cough. “Thank you, Fuma.”

Her expression softened. “You’re welcome. Try not to die in there, all right? I’d like you conscious at the interclan meeting so I’m not the only young heir being bored to death.”

The thought of tonight made my insides twist, but I managed a faint smile. “I’ll do my best.”

“See you later then,” she said brightly, tucking the vial away. “Go.”

I went.

The road to the Masamune compound blurred under my feet. The tonic’s warmth buoyed me just enough to keep my stride from breaking, the world narrowing to the rhythm of my breath and the slap of my sandals against stone.

Elder Low does not tolerate excuses.

Punishment mission or not, heir or not, I doubted “school ran late” would qualify as a viable excuse.

By the time the clan training hall came into view, my shirt clung damp to my back and my pulse thundered in my ears. The tall wooden doors of the arena annex were open, new recruits in grey training uniforms filing through another entrance under the guidance of senior operatives.

Did he intend to make my evaluation public? My insides twisted. I wouldn’t put it past him.

A large wall clock hung above the main doors.

One minute to the hour.

I forced myself not to sprint the last few steps. Running into Elder Low’s presence like a panicked child would make a worse impression than being fractionally winded.

A lone figure leaned against the wall beside the door, arms folded, one ankle hooked over the other.

Sadi.

His posture was relaxed on the surface, but his eyes when they flicked to me were anything but. He took in my disheveled state—the sweat, the slightly uneven breathing—and one corner of his mouth curled.

“Cutting it close, aren’t you, Heir,” he drawled.

I stopped a respectful distance away and bowed. “Sadi.”

Up close, the differences five years made were stark. He’d grown broader across the shoulders since I last saw him outside a servant’s uniform. The easy strength in the way he held himself wasn’t just youth; it was hard-earned muscle, the kind that came from constant, brutal fieldwork.

There was a faint tightness around his eyes I hadn’t seen before. I knew, too well, where some of that came from.

A burn, intricate and dark, at the base of his spine. A brand of obedience older than our clans.

He straightened off the wall with a push of his shoulder. “What, did they keep you late at the academy to polish your halo?”

“I had additional questions to answer,” I said. There was no point denying it. “Anko is thorough.”

He snorted. “Of course she is.”

For a heartbeat, flickers of another time overlaid the present. Sadi slumped against the edge of a low futon, shirt rucked up, the skin over his lower back raw and inflamed where the slave brand had flared under his father’s control.

Later that same day, his fingers closing around my wrist as I slipped on a wet roof tile, yanking me back from a three-story fall. Shoving food into my hand afterward, grumbling about “useless slaves dying on my watch.”

He’d pretended not to remember any of it when my punishment service at Akito’s house ended. It was easier that way. For both of us.

Now, whatever softness there had been was buried under the same armor he always wore in public: sarcasm, sharp edges, competitiveness honed to a spiteful gleam.

The clock ticked over to the hour.

The inner door slid open.

Elder Low stood there, slight and straight-backed in his simple dark robes, white hair pulled into a severe knot at the back of his head. His eyes moved from Sadi to me, weighing, measuring.

“You’re on time,” he said. “Good. Come.”

We bowed as one. “Yes, Elder Low.”

His private quarters behind the arena were austere. Tatami mats, a low table, shelves lined with scrolls and a few carefully placed weapons. No clutter. No softness.

We knelt opposite him as he sat.

I bowed, forehead to the mat. “Tadashi Masamune, heir to the Masamune clan, reporting for evaluation.”

“Mm.” His fingers tapped once, twice, against his knee. “You have been away for eighteen months. Your mental conditioning has been tested adequately.” A faint, bitter humor in the word “adequately.” “Today I will see what remains of your physical capacity.”

“Yes, Elder.”

He turned his head to Sadi. “Sadi.”

Sadi straightened. “Elder Low.”

“You will help me test him.”

A brief spark lit in Sadi’s eyes. “With pleasure, Elder.”

Low ignored the tone. “The new recruits are assembled in the arena. They need to see what is expected of them if they are to rise in this clan. You will spar with Tadashi, in stages.”

He held up a thin, veined hand, counting off with his fingers.

“First, hand to hand. Then bo staff. Then weighted chain. Finally, wooden sword. You—” his gaze pinned Sadi “—will go hard. No holding back. Use every advantage. Do you understand?”

“Yes, Elder.” Sadi’s smile was sharp. “I’ll make it… educational.”

Low’s eyes slid back to me. “You will fight back, defend yourself, and remain standing until I call an end. There will be no intervals for rest.” His gaze did not waver. “If you fall, you will rise. If you are disarmed, you will improvise. If you disgrace yourself, you will not do it in front of the recruits. Is that clear?”

My heartbeat thudded once, heavy.

“Yes, Elder,” I said. “Understood.”

He watched me for a moment longer, as if testing the words for weakness. Then he rose in one smooth motion.

“Very well. To the arena.”

The roar of the recruits hit us as soon as we stepped through the side entrance.

The Masamune training arena was a vast rectangle of packed earth and smoothed stone, ringed by tiered wooden platforms. The lowest tier, nearest the practice floor, was crowded with the new intake—young faces, some eager, some nervous, all turning as we entered. Above them, scattered among the higher tiers, stood older operatives and clan observers, arms folded, eyes intent.

In the shadowed corner of the uppermost tier, two figures sat side by side.

Father.

And Akito.

For a moment, my feet almost stopped moving.

Father’s profile was stark against the dimness—strong jaw, straight nose, eyes half-lidded as if merely observing, but I knew that look. He missed nothing. Every misstep, every lag in response time, would be noted and weighed.

Beside him, Akito lounged with deceptive ease, one leg crossed over the other, his hands resting lightly on his knee. His gaze, when it met mine, was flat and cold. I remembered that gaze from another angle—yanked up by the hair from the floor with a collar biting into my throat.

I tore my eyes away and followed Elder Low to the center of the arena.

He raised one hand; the noise subsided into a bright, humming silence.

“Recruits,” he said, voice carrying without effort, “today you will see what your years of work may one day allow you to attempt. Tadashi Masamune, clan heir. Sadi Masamune, deputy clan heir. Both have bled for this clan. Both continue to do so.”

A flicker of something passed over Sadi’s face at that—pride, resentment, both—but he rolled his shoulders, loosening his joints.

“This is not a performance,” Low went on. “This is instruction. Watch. Learn.”

He stepped back, leaving the space between us clear.

Sadi’s eyes found mine, dark and bright all at once. “Try not to embarrass yourself, Heir,” he murmured, just loud enough for me to hear.

I bowed. “I’ll do my best not to slow you down.”

His grin flashed, wolfish. “We’ll see.”

Elder Low’s hand chopped the air.

“Begin.”

Sadi exploded forward.

It was like being hit by a storm.

He closed the distance in two strides, fist arcing toward my temple in a strike that would have rattled my brain if it landed clean. I got my forearm up in time; the impact sent a jolt down to my shoulder.

No time to absorb. I pivoted, letting the force go past, driving a palm strike toward his ribs, but he turned with me, forearm smashing down on mine. His knee came up, hunting my midsection.

I dropped my elbow, catching him on the thigh instead of the gut. Still enough power to make the muscle scream.

He was stronger than I remembered. Broad field work had added weight to his blows, each hit a promise of bruises I’d be feeling for days.

I flowed back, two steps, three, letting his combinations skim air or graze instead of land full force. His style was aggressive, heavy—a bulldozer compared to my scalpel. That had always been true. Today, the difference felt starker.

I slipped inside a wide hook and drove my knuckles into his ribs. He grunted, short and sharp, and answered with a headbutt that came too fast to fully avoid.

Our foreheads collided with a crack. White flashed at the edge of my vision.

Dirty, I thought, not without a flicker of admiration. Elder Low had told him to use every advantage. He was obeying.

I staggered half a step. His fist was already on its way toward my jaw.

I snapped my head back just enough that his knuckles scraped skin instead of shattering bone. Pain bloomed, hot and immediate, but teeth stayed intact. I caught his wrist with my other hand, twisted, using his forward momentum to pull him off balance.

We traded in tight quarters—elbows, knees, short hooks to the body. My back screamed with every twist, the welts threatening to tear. One of his punches slid past my guard and buried itself in my stomach; air punched out of me in a hiss.

I dropped, letting my legs go loose under me.

He followed, overextending just enough that my sweep took his feet from under him. He hit the packed earth shoulder-first, rolled, came up already laughing.

“Not bad,” he said under his breath.

I didn’t answer. Couldn’t. My lungs were busy clawing air back inside.

Elder Low’s voice cut across the arena. “Staff.”

A pair of senior operatives stepped forward from the sidelines, each tossing a polished wooden staff into the ring with practiced arcs.

Sadi snatched his out of the air one-handed. I caught mine with both hands, the familiarity of the smooth wood under my palms a small anchor.

We circled for a breath, staffs held low.

Then we went in.

The first clash of wood on wood rang like a bell. Sadi struck high, then low, then high again, using his reach to rain blows from different angles. I parried, the impacts stinging my hands. Sliding step, inside his guard, snap the butt end toward his knee—

He jerked the leg back, my strike grazing rather than crippling. His return swing cracked across my knuckles. Pain shot up my fingers; I almost lost my grip.

Don’t. If you drop it now, he’ll break you.

I adjusted, shifting to a looser, more flexible hold, letting the staff roll against my skin instead of fighting every impact head-on. Strike, block, redirect. Feet moving constantly, never straight back—diagonal, always looking for an angle.

We danced across the arena, the rhythm of the fight building: strike-block-strike, the sharp whip of wood through air. A blow slipped past my defenses and smacked into my ribs—right over the fading bruise Erachi’s shove had aggravated earlier. Stars burst behind my eyes.

The crack of wood on flesh was too close, too familiar. It echoed the sound of the cane my masters used on me. Pain, fire, the electric burn of the collar—

“Count your breaths,” his voice murmured unbidden in my head. “If you scream, I will increase the voltage. If you stay quiet, it ends sooner.”

The world tilted for a heartbeat.

My guard dipped. Sadi’s staff slammed into my thigh. My leg buckled.

The ground surged up. I twisted as I fell, taking the impact on my side instead of my already-abused back. The shock still sent a lance of agony through me.

Up. Get up .

I rolled, staff an extension of my arm, blocking the follow-up strike by instinct more than sight. Sadi’s face loomed above me for a moment, sweat beading his brow, eyes bright with adrenaline.

And something else. A flicker of—question? Understanding that my mind wasn’t in the fight? It was gone before I could be sure, swallowed under a renewed snarl of effort as he bore down.

From the corner of my eye, I saw Father’s silhouette shift slightly on the upper tier.

Disappointment? Calculation? I couldn’t tell at this distance. My chest tightened anyway.

You’re being watched. Stand.

I pushed off the ground, forcing my trembling muscles to obey. The recruits’ murmurs swelled—dismay, excitement, I couldn’t separate them.

Elder Low’s voice sliced through again. “Chain.”

Relief and dread tangled in my throat.

The staff was taken from my hands as I straightened; a weighted chain was thrust into its place. The metal lay heavy and cold across my palms, dark links coiled around a dense iron weight at one end.

My stomach knotted.

Chains.

Images surged up, unbidden. Shackles clinking in a darkened dungeon cell. The slap of a length of chain across bare shoulders. The rattle as I’d dragged a too-heavy bucket across stone, collar biting, Master Oni’s fingers forming the activation seal whenever I slowed.

A voice in my head: “ You are property. Tools do not flinch from their own handles. ”

“Heir.”

Sadi’s voice, low, close.

I realized I’d frozen, chain hanging limp at my side. His eyes, locked on mine, were suddenly very sharp, very present.

“You with me?” he muttered, too quiet for the recruits to hear.

“Yes,” I said. The word scraped. “Apologies.”

I wrapped the chain around my forearm, the way I’d been taught. The links were smooth, oiled, indifferent to what they’d been used for before.

This is not that. This is now. Move .

We moved.

Sadi swung first, the weighted end whistling toward my shoulder. I ducked; the iron grazed my hair as it passed, the wind of it cold against my scalp.

I snapped my wrist, sending my own chain out low. He jumped; the weight bit into the back of his calf instead of his ankle. He hissed, momentum carrying him forward anyway, his chain looping back in a vicious arc.

Metal on metal rang as our weapons collided. Sparks spat where the weights kissed mid-air.

Chains crack like whips when they hit flesh. Every impact, every near miss, ricocheted against old bruises in my memory as much as my skin. I forced my world to shrink: trajectory, distance, Sadi’s shoulders, his hips, the minute shifts that telegraphed direction.

A loop of his chain snagged my wrist, the sudden drag threatening to yank me off my feet. I stepped into it instead of back, twisting, wrapping the chain around my forearm to shorten the lever. His eyes widened as I pulled, hard.

He stumbled in, off-balance. My knee came up, driving toward his thigh. He turned enough that it hit muscle, not joint. Still enough to make his leg falter.

For a moment, we were chest to chest, chains binding us both, breathing hard, sweat dripping into our eyes.

“Don’t make me look bad in front of my father,” he panted, barely audible.

The words hit deeper than any strike.

I opened my mouth, closed it again. There was nothing safe to say.

Elder Low clapped once, sharp.

“Enough. Swords.”

The chains were taken from us, coiled away. Wooden bokken were thrust into our hands—the familiar weight of them settling into my grip like an old friend.

I exhaled.

This, at least, was home.

We faced each other, brought the blades up in mirrored guards.

“Begin,” Low said again.

Our first exchange was like a conversation spoken in an old, half-forgotten language. Cut, parry, countercut. Sadi favored power—overhand blows meant to overwhelm. I answered with economy, turning his strength aside with minimal contact, letting his momentum carry him past.

Wood rang against wood in rapid succession. Sparks of pain bloomed where stray strikes slipped through—his tip snapping into my upper arm, my blade rapping his fingers hard enough to make him swear under his breath.

As the tempo rose, the clean lines of formal kata began to fray. Footing slipped on churned earth. Breath grew ragged. Fatigue made fine control harder with each passing exchange.

Sadi adapted first, or perhaps he simply reverted. He stepped in close on a bind, shoulder slamming into my chest, his bokken hooking behind my ankle even as he drove his knee toward my ribs.

I twisted, taking some of the force on my hip instead. Pain flared; my stance cracked open for a heartbeat.

He shoved, hard.

I went down.

The fall was ugly and it hurt, my already-bruised back slamming into the ground. For a fraction of a second, the sky spun overhead. Somewhere above, a figure on the upper tier shifted and stood.

Father?

By the time I blinked grit from my eyes and pushed up on one elbow, the seat where he’d been was empty.

A hollow space opened under my breastbone.

Of course he left.

I had fallen. I had faltered.

Weak. Inadequate.

Another shadow loomed. Sadi, bokken raised for a finishing strike.

I could have rolled away.

Instead, I rolled toward.

The downward cut, meant for my chest, bit into the earth just past my ribs as I twisted. Pain screamed up my spine as I deliberately let myself complete the motion into another bad fall, shoulder slamming again into hard ground—but my legs scissored around Sadi’s planted foot.

His balance, already committed forward, shattered.

He crashed down beside me with a curse, his sword arm momentarily tangled under his own weight. My hand snapped out, fingers closing around his wrist, pinning it.

We moved in the same instant—him trying to yank free, me rolling over him, using the torque of my already-rolling body to pry the bokken from his grip.

The wooden blade flew loose, skittering across the dirt.

I came up on one knee, Sadi half turned under me, his free hand scrambling for leverage.

My bokken hammered into the ground just beside his head, close enough that the wind of it stirred his hair.

He froze.

For a hanging second, we stared at each other. His chest heaved, sweat streaking his face, eyes blazing.

Then he moved—fast, going for my ankle with a grab that would topple me if it landed.

I shifted, letting his fingers close on air, and brought the hilt of my sword down in a short, sharp arc.

It connected with the side of his head, just above the ear. Not full force—enough to rattle, not to break.

His eyes rolled for a heartbeat. Then his body went limp.

Silence flooded the arena, deep and stunned.

Elder Low raised his hand.

“Enough.”

The word was both judgment and reprieve.

I pushed to my feet, every muscle screaming. My bokken felt ten times heavier than it had when we started. Sweat stung my eyes, soaked my clothes. My breath tore in and out in ragged pulls that I forced into something like control.

Do not curl. Do not fold. You are being watched .

I turned to the recruits and bowed, as low and as steady as I could make it.

I straightened. The faces lining the arena blurred at the edges—wide-eyed, awed, envious, afraid. I couldn’t tell.

I looked up, toward the upper tier.

Father’s seat was still empty.

Only Akito remained, a dark slash against the wood. His gaze was fixed on me with an intensity that made my skin crawl—cold, appraising, something like hatred simmering just below the surface.

The same look he’d given me as I knelt on his floor in chains, back bloody from his anger taken out on someone more convenient than his son. The same faint curl of the lip that had accompanied the snap of his fingers and the flash of pain.

I dropped my eyes, my wooden sword suddenly heavy enough to drag my arm toward the ground.

My lungs burned. My pulse roared. Every part of me ached.

I had stayed standing. I had not broken.

But as Akito’s gaze bored into me, and the empty space where Father had sat yawned above, it felt less like victory than another test I’d only barely survived.
