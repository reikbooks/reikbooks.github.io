The Storeroom

Book 12 of Masamune Clan Series

In a house full of people, he was completely alone. As the quiet heir to his clan, Tadashi lives in the shadow of his brilliant sister, constantly vying for a single word of praise from his father. But when tragedy strikes, the fragile family rituals shatter, and Tadashi is left to navigate a sea of silence. Forced to become the invisible caretaker for his grieving siblings, can he hold his broken family together without losing himself in the process?

“ H ow was your day?”

It was the bell that ended the night for us. It came after the dishes were stacked, after Mother wiped my younger brother Raito’s mouth and set the steaming teapot down. It was a ritual from a time before clan meetings and the heavy robes, a time when I was just Tadashi. Father would ask it the same way every evening, his voice a low rumble across the table.

As a boy, I was starved for his attention. That question was a sliver of light under a locked door, a chance to make him look at me—truly look at me. So that night, I told him about the shed. I’d been bursting with it all through dinner, my legs jiggling under the low table.

I had found it by accident, playing hide-and-seek with Shiro out past the camellias, where the hedge grew thick and the ground sloped down. I saw the roof first, a dark hump half-swallowed by cypress trees. It looked like a slumbering monster. Inside, it smelled of wet wood and old metal, crammed with forgotten tools that lay about like sleeping animals with teeth. It felt like the sort of place ghosts might like. That made it perfect.

I had no idea it was her hiding spot; Shiro never told me secrets when there was a game on. I must have counted to one hundred three times, my voice growing thin in the twilight.

When I finally gave up, she popped out from behind a line of overgrown hedges, her laughter bright and triumphant. The sting of losing vanished, replaced by the secret thrill of our discovery. We chased each other home, our sleeves snagging on leaves, the whole afternoon a bright, skipping thing in my chest.

Hearing the story, a frown drew Mother’s brows together. “Tadashi, what were you doing in there? It’s a dangerous place. Full of broken glass and sharp, rusty nails.”

“I was careful, Mother,” I promised, my voice earnest.

“But he was in such a hurry he tripped,” Shiro added, breezy as ever, pouring her tea with two hands the way she’d seen Mother do. She didn’t look at me.

Mother’s worried gaze snapped back. “Are you hurt, Tadashi?”

I shook my head, though I could feel the faint, dull throb in my knee from where I had stumbled. A tiny price for an afternoon of adventure.

“Don’t play there again,” Mother said, her tone leaving no room for argument. “It’s too far for my voice to carry. Why were you out so long today, anyway?”

I hesitated. Shiro’s foot touched mine under the table, a quick, sharp warning. A part of the story I’d been told to leave out. But Mother could always tell when something was missing. Her eyes narrowed as if she could see the secret words I was holding on my tongue.

“Tadashi?” The warning was in her voice now, too.

Across the table, Father lowered his newspaper. The sound of the paper creasing was as loud as a slamming door. His eyes, dark and serious, fixed on me. The warmth of the room seemed to vanish. A place in my chest went cold and small. I confessed in a mumble, my eyes glued to the tatami mat. “Shiro… she got locked in the shed for a while.”

“Oh, my—”

“I’m fine, Mother!” Shiro cut in, shooting a furious glare at me. “It was nothing. I just called for Tadashi and he helped me climb out the window.”

That was not how it had felt. The truth was, after I’d run out of the spooky shed, the game ended and she still hadn’t appeared. The sky was turning purple. My gut went heavy with a real fear. I circled the building, my mouth dry, and there, through the grimy glass, I saw a shape moving. A dark silhouette. Small hands hit the pane with a rattling thud. For a breath, I almost ran, certain it was a ghost.

But then I heard my name, muffled and annoyed and scared all at once. “Tadashi!”

The door she’d slipped through had jammed shut. It took me what felt like hours to find a thick, sturdy branch to pry the window shutter open. The rusted hinge screamed as I put my whole body into it, the bark biting into my palms. My arms shook. When the wood finally gave, the edge scraped my skin raw. I lifted the window just enough for her to crawl out. Her cheeks were flushed, her hair stuck to her forehead. And then she stood up and laughed as if she’d planned it all. I laughed too, because she was out, and safe. Only then did the sting in my hands and knee rush back all at once.

It shouldn’t have been a big deal. But now my parents were watching me. Father wore the same expression he’d had when I was four and let go of Raito’s hand near the koi pond. The look he reserved for when my guard slipped, when I failed.

My stomach twisted into a tight, sick knot. I shrank into myself, sliding my hands from the table to clench the fabric of my hakama, waiting for the words I knew were coming like waiting for the rain you can already feel.

“You should have been watching her more carefully,” Father said, his voice low and heavy, each word a stone dropped into the quiet room. “What if something worse had happened? You are the eldest, Tadashi. It is your duty to keep them safe. Do you understand?”

I flinched as if he had struck me. “Yes, Father. I’m sorry.”

“You’re grounded for a week, and you’ll have extra chores. This weekend, you will help me clean out that shed.”

I bit my lip, imagining the spiders and the rust, and nodded quickly. “Yes, Father.”

“Oh, by the way, Father,” Shiro said, her smile quick as a sparrow changing direction. She took the air in the room and steered it. “I was learning striking theory with Elder Low today. He said I was the first to master the new form!”

Just like that, the storm passed over me and the sun came out for her. The tension broke. Father smiled at Shiro, a real smile that reached his eyes. Their conversation about stances and katas carried the rest of the meal.

I swallowed against the knot of jealousy in my throat. It tasted like ash. I wanted them to look at me like that. But she always outshone me. In the dojo, she was a rising star while I was just… there. I had no victories to share, only failures. I lowered my head and practiced not needing anything.

“How was your day?”

Father asked again a few weeks later. The question landed in the middle of the table, a prize to be won. I saw my chance and lunged for it. Maybe this time.

“Shiro and I were training with Elder Low today, Father. He said—”

“I’ve already spoken with Elder Low,” Father said, cutting me off without looking up.

The words died in my throat. Before I could recover, Shiro seized the opening. “Tadashi was sluggish, Father!” she announced, and launched into a breathless report of the day’s lesson. Her eyes shone. She wanted to win even this.

Lately, everything felt like a small contest we did not name. Shiro couldn’t stand to be second to me in anything. She didn’t need to worry. She was better in every way that mattered to Father. Couldn’t you leave this one thing for me? I shot her a wounded look, but it bounced off her. She was unstoppable.

I knew excuses did not live long in Father’s world. You simply accepted your failings and their consequences. Still, my knee throbbed under the table from a fall during horse-riding practice that morning. The swelling made my hakama tight around the joint. A hot, pulsing knot of pain. I thought, If I say it, will he hear me? Or will he just say, ‘Use your other leg’ ? The words stuck in my mouth and became nothing.

After Shiro finished, Father turned to me, his brow creased with that familiar look of disapproval. “I’ve seen your training report. Elder Low is right. Starting tomorrow, you will begin your morning exercises an hour earlier.”

The sentence landed hard. An extra hour? My knee throbbed in protest. The thought of more running, more drills in the pre-dawn cold, made my stomach churn. I wanted to object, to explain, but then I saw Mother look up from feeding Raito, her eyes flicking between Father and me, her face pale with a silent plea.

“Tadashi?” Father prompted, his gaze unwavering. “The heir selection is fast approaching. You cannot afford these weak reports. To be chosen, you must surpass Shiro. Do you understand?”

Ah.

A few days before, hidden in the hallway, I had heard their voices through the paper wall. Father’s, sharp and insistent. Mother’s, tight and low.

“He is not ready,” Father had said.

“He is our eldest,” Mother replied. “He carries more than she does. He breaks himself trying to please you.”

“The clan needs strength. Shiro is the best candidate.”

“And I say he should be the only candidate. He will be crushed if you set them against each other.”

“Both names are already registered,” Father said, and the paper had rustled like dry leaves.

I had stood there, breath held, wanting to be good enough to end their argument, or to be nothing at all so they would not have to fight. I could not be both. So I chose the only thing I could do. I chose to be quiet.

Clutching my aching knee under the table, I swallowed the pain and the protest. “Yes, Father,” I said, my voice steadier than I felt. “I’ll start earlier tomorrow.”

“How were things at home today?” Father asked, but this time, the question was for Mother.

The heir selection was over. Shiro earned the highest score, a result that surprised no one. But then, in front of all the elders, she had withdrawn. Father called a vote, and they accepted me as the clan heir in her place. A fragile, uncertain peace had settled over our home, like dust that stays until someone moves too fast.

Mother smiled, a genuine, relaxed smile. She recounted Raito’s mischief—a chaotic attempt to “help” in the kitchen that ended with a fine dusting of flour over every surface, including the cat. I remembered it well: Raito’s giggling as he’d tossed a soft puff of flour at my face. I’d caught him, tickling him until he shrieked, and turned his energy into a game of cleaning up before Mother saw the worst of it.

“Well, who was watching him?” Father asked, his brow furrowing as his gaze, by habit, fell on me.

“I was, Father,” I said quickly, my heart giving a little jump. “It’s all cleaned up.”

It was no great feat, not like mastering a kata. But I held a tiny, foolish flicker of hope. I did not dream big. A small smile can fill an entire evening when you are hungry enough.

He only gave a slight nod. Then his attention turned to Shiro, asking about a new mochi recipe. As she launched into an enthusiastic story, I quietly folded my little hope into a thin, tight thing, the way you fold paper until it is hard and cannot unfold without tearing.

Don’t make a scene. Don’t ruin the peace, I told myself. I pulled Raito onto my lap. He was warm and heavy, and smelled of flour and soy. He leaned back against my chest, relaxing the way children do when they feel safe without thinking about it.

Soon, Father excused himself to rest. But as he stood, he did something I never expected. He walked around the table and stopped behind me. He placed a hand on my shoulder. The weight of it was warm and solid.

“Look after your siblings and help your mother, Tadashi.”

I looked up. He was smiling at me. It wasn’t the brilliant, proud smile he gave Shiro for her victories. It was something else—quieter, softer. A look of acknowledgement. I stared, my throat tight.

Father… he had been paying attention after all. He’d seen the flour, the game, the cleanup. He’d seen it all.

Something opened in my chest, like a window you didn’t know was stuck until it finally moved. A warmth I hadn’t realized I was so starved for spread through me, like the sun breaking through a week of cold rain. I could only nod, my lips parting in a real smile of my own, one that wasn’t shadowed by hope or fear.

Maybe my aptitude for training would never match Shiro’s brilliance. Maybe I would never win his praise for my accomplishments with a sword. But perhaps there was another kind of strength, one that didn’t make a loud noise or win any contests. A quiet strength that he saw all the same.

I will protect this family, Father, I vowed in the small, private place where my thoughts were always louder than my voice. I will give it my all.

I was stuffing the last of the damp leaves from the back garden into a bin when Mother called from the kitchen window. “Is the front yard swept, Tadashi? Your father will be home soon.”

“Yes, Mother, just finished the back!” I called back, my own voice thin with exhaustion.

The rake lines were clean in the soil. The afternoon light had gone soft. I could hear Raito on the porch, humming to himself, knocking his little wooden horse against the step. I had fed him lunch, wiped the egg yolk from his cheek like a badge, and left him on the porch with my schoolbag nearby while I finished the yard. I told myself I would pack it up the moment I was done.

I rounded the corner of the house, and my stomach dropped into a cold, hollow space.

My books lay open and wrong. Pages were bent, fluttering in the breeze. Raito sat in the middle of them, one of my textbooks in both hands, tearing at the corner with the serious concentration of a child peeling fruit.

“Raito!” The word came out too sharp. I lunged. “No! Those are my books!”

Startled, he flinched and yanked back with all the strength his small arms had. I grabbed harder. The paper ripped. The sound was crisp and awful, louder than a thunderclap. His face crumpled. Then came the wail—a shattering sound of pure, wounded betrayal.

“Tadashi, what’s wrong with Raito?” Mother’s voice floated out, quick now with concern.

I crouched, my heart in my throat, hands shaking as I scraped the ruined pages into a pile. My homework, the neat columns of numbers I’d spent an hour on, was in shredded curls that made no sense. I could already hear my teacher’s voice asking for it, and I could already see Father’s face.

Just then, the front gate groaned open. That awful, heavy sound.

Father .

And I hadn’t swept the front yard.

The broom lay against the wall, the front path still scattered with leaves. My heart hammered against my ribs. I snatched the cloth baby carrier from the porch, somehow managing to swing the still-sobbing Raito onto my back, his wet face pressing into my neck. I grabbed the broom. If I moved fast, maybe—

Father stepped through the gate. He stopped. His eyes went from me, to the wailing child on my back, to the unswept path. I watched his expression settle into that familiar flatness. Not anger that burned. Cooler than that. It was the look that said I had failed at something basic.

“What is all this? Why is he crying?” His voice was clipped as he came forward and lifted Raito from my back with ease.

I blinked against the sting in my own eyes. “Sorry, Father. He was… playing with my books,” I mumbled, the excuse sounding pathetic even to me.

Father let out a sigh, a low sound heavy with a disappointment that was worse than any shout. He shifted Raito on his hip and turned toward the house. “Finish your chore.”

When I finally walked into the house, dinner was already over. That left me alone with Father. He spoke standing, like a judge before a case, and lectured me on diligence, on patience, on foresight.

“You finish what you start,” he said. “If you know your father will return soon, you make the front yard ready before the back. Think ahead, Tadashi. Anticipate. No excuses.”

I bowed my head, shame burning my cheeks and the tips of my ears, nodding at each point as if collecting stones. “Yes, Father.”

Apparently, keeping my promise to protect the family was harder than I ever imagined.

Since being named heir, the duties that used to be chores had become tests. School, clan training, homework, housework—I was caught in a vice, squeezed from all sides. The elders monitored my grades. Instructors punished any slip in my stances. At home, Father’s nightly question hung over me like a blade waiting to fall. If Raito cried for too long, it was a mark against me. If Shiro and I snapped at each other, it was a mark. If I slipped, I was corrected. The corrections were meant to build me, but sometimes they felt like cuts.

When he finished, his retreating back was a wall I couldn’t seem to scale. A thousand questions pressed against my teeth and died there.

Why is it so hard to please you, Father?

Is there truly nothing I can do that will ever be right in your eyes?

I remember the last dinner before Mother passed away. I remember it with a clarity that still aches. The room had that easy warmth it only got when she was there, telling us about a family recipe for sweet rice balls, her voice a soft, warm melody in the lamp-lit room.

Shiro, ever the perfectionist, peppered her with questions. Father listened with a rare, relaxed smile that made him look younger. Raito giggled on Mother’s lap. The whole room felt golden.

In the middle of her story, Mother’s eyes met mine. Her smile softened just for me. “Would you like some more tofu soup, Tadashi? You finished your bowl so quickly.”

Before I could answer, Shiro chimed in, bright and unthinking. “He’s probably starving, Mother. He was the last one to finish training with Elder Low today. Again.”

Why does she have to say that? The words stung. A flash of heat went through me. I shot her a glare, but she just rolled her eyes, as if she were only reminding me to try harder, as if it was kindness.

“Are you still hungry, Tadashi?” Mother asked, already rising to fetch the heavy iron pot from the stove.

“You’re making Mother work more? Get it yourself!” Shiro protested.

I flinched, my face hot. “No, it’s okay, Mother, I’m full—”

Too late. She returned and ladled a generous serving into my bowl, her expression firm but gentle. “Eat,” she said softly. As she set the bowl down, her hand came to rest on my hair for just a moment before she resumed her story. I lowered my head over the rising steam, my cheeks burning. “Thank you, Mother,” I mumbled.

I don’t know when it started, this feeling of being an observer in my own family. A silent moon orbiting a warm, bright planet, close enough to feel its heat but never truly a part of its world. I yearned to be like Shiro, to effortlessly command their attention, to be a character in the story instead of just a spectator. Most days, the distance was a suffocating loneliness.

But something was different that night. As I watched the easy flow of laughter between them, a quiet, unfamiliar warmth bloomed in my chest that wasn’t about them looking at me. It was about me looking at them and feeling the string that tied me to them pull tight. Their joy was mine even if they did not say my name. It felt like belonging that did not require applause.

And I thought, if this is all it ever is—if I am just here and useful and part of the background—maybe it is still enough. As long as we are together, maybe I will be all right.

The night Mother died, our dinner was a black hole. It swallowed sound. It bent the room around it.

Nausea churned in my gut. Heat crawled under my skin and made my bones ache. My head swam. The bruises from the fight throbbed in a dull circle around my ribs when I breathed too deep. There hadn’t been time to go to Anko for medicine. Raito had clung to me all day, his small hands digging into my clothes, crying for Mother until his voice went hoarse. He had finally fallen asleep in my lap, spent, but his body still jerked now and then with leftover sobs.

I clutched my teacup in both hands because they shook. I breathed in the scent of tofu soup and felt a needle of memory pierce through me.

Would you like some more tofu soup, Tadashi?

The line ran on a loop in my head, each repetition sharper. It would not stop. I shut my eyes for a second and saw her hand with the ladle again.

Why did it have to be Mother? Why couldn’t it have been me? I clenched my jaw hard enough to hurt, fighting to swallow the sob rising in my throat. My eyes burned. Everything felt swollen and hot, like my skin no longer fit. The air was too thick. The room was wrong.

“Is Raito asleep?” Father’s voice sliced through the silence, making me jump.

I blinked, clearing the blur of tears. He wasn’t looking at me. He was staring at Mother’s empty place at the table, his face a mask of stone. The space wasn’t just empty; it was a wound in the fabric of the room.

“Yes, Father,” I whispered, my voice a scratchy thing.

“Stay with him tonight. Keep him calm,” he ordered, his voice thick and hoarse. He still wouldn’t meet my gaze. “Your grandmother arrives tomorrow. Prepare the guest room.” He rose stiffly, his back a rigid line of grief, and left the room without another word.

I carried Raito to his futon. He made a small sound and then settled again with his fist by his mouth. When I returned to the kitchen, Shiro was already stacking dishes. I moved to help her. The scrape of bowls on wood was too loud in the quiet.

She did not look at me. I could feel her anger like heat from a fire you can’t see but know is burning nearby. Her words from earlier in the day kept replaying. They squeezed the air out of my lungs.

This is all your fault! It should have been you who died!

My chest constricted until it hurt. She was right. If only I had been stronger. If only I hadn’t left her alone.

Oh, God, take me instead. Please.

Shiro… I’m so sorry. I am so, so sorry.

I wanted to say it, to beg for her forgiveness, but the blaze in her eyes told me it was useless. The look in her eyes killed the sound. There was nothing I could say that would reach her.

We didn’t speak again that night.

“How are things at home?” Father asked Grandmother a the next day.

I sat between them, a piece of furniture on the tatami, with the living weight of my brother asleep on my back. Raito had finally cried himself out, his small whimpers quieting into soft breaths against my neck. My shoulders ached from carrying him, but I did not dare untie him. When I tried earlier, he woke and screamed for Mother until his voice cracked and my hands shook.

Grandmother sighed, her eyes etched with worry. “Raito has not stopped crying for his mother. Shiro has locked herself in her room, but she’s agreed to come back with me to the countryside.”

I stared at the empty bowls on the table. It hit me then—soon the whole house would be like this. Quiet. Spaces where voices used to live, now hollow. No one to laugh in the kitchen. No one to argue over the last fish. It wasn’t just Mother I was losing. It felt like I was losing everyone.

I swallowed my protest. It wasn’t my place to ask for anything. I told myself it was better for Shiro to go, better for her to be somewhere Mother had once been happy. Father said they would be safe there. So I told myself what older brothers tell themselves.

Want what is best for them, not for you.

Father cut Grandmother off. “Thank you for your help, Mother-in-law. Tadashi will see to anything you need.” With that final, dismissive command, he was gone.

“Grandmother, you should rest. I’ll clean up,” I said quickly when she reached for the dishes. She looked at me for a second, then nodded and patted my arm.

It was awkward work with Raito on my back. I dragged a stool to the sink with my foot and climbed up a step so I could reach without leaning forward too much. My hands were clumsy in the water.

The hand towel Mother had used just yesterday morning was still hanging by the sink, folded in her careful way. I reached for it, my own hand trembling, and without thinking, I pressed the soft cotton to my face.

Her scent clung to the fabric. Not perfume, just soap and sunshine and something that belonged only to her. For a split second, it was as if she were standing right beside me, her hand on my shoulder. I could almost hear her.

You’re such a good helper. Thank you, Tadashi.

A ragged breath tore from my chest. I told myself the sting in my eyes was from the steam. The lie lasted a heartbeat. The tears came hot and silent and would not listen when I told them to stop. I bit my lip hard to keep my shoulders from shaking and waking Raito. My vision blurred.

The sink filled with bowls that would not wash themselves. The weight on my back, the ache in my chest, the long list of things to do—they all piled up until I could barely stand under them. But Mother had taught me what to do when a task felt too big.

One dish at a time.

So I forced my hands back into the water. I forced myself to finish. I washed a bowl. I rinsed it. I set it down. I reached for another. I kept going until the water had gone cool and my eyes had dried on their own.

I stood there with the towel in my hands and told myself again what I had promised.

I will look after them. I will not fail. Even if no one sees it, I will keep going.

One step at a time.

F ather never asked me how I was doing anymore. After Grandmother took Shiro and Raito away, the silence at the dinner table became a solid thing, too heavy to bear. We simply stopped eating together.

The nightly question vanished, and with it, the last frayed thread of our family ritual. The house became a tomb where every spot remembered who was missing.

On our first morning alone, he still came down for breakfast. I had woken before dawn, my body a map of aches from my morning drills, and made porridge. It was the only thing I knew how to make without Mother’s hands to guide me. I set a bowl before him on the low table, my heart a timid drum against my ribs.

Father took a single, slow spoonful, and then his movements stilled. He stared down into the white porcelain as if looking into a deep, dark well. It was so unlike him that a cold prickle of concern ran down my arms.

“Father?” I whispered into the crushing quiet.

He turned. For a breath, he looked straight at me with his real eyes, the ones he always kept locked away behind a mask of duty. In that flash, the mask slipped. I saw grief so raw it felt like a cut, a fury he was holding down by force, a longing that made his face look older, and a cold desire for vengeance that made my skin crawl. And beneath it all, the same blame I had seen in Shiro’s eyes when she screamed at me.

It should have been you.

My breath caught. I flinched back, bracing for a blow that didn’t come.

The moment shattered as quickly as it had formed. He looked away, his jaw clenched so tight the muscle jumped, as if the very sight of me was a fresh wound. Without a word, he rose from the tatami and left for work.

The silence he left behind felt like a judgment. I stared at the untouched bowl of porridge, a sharp knot twisting in my gut. My eyes stung with hot, unshed tears. Was my cooking that bad? Or was it me? Was my presence so unbearable he couldn’t even eat?

That afternoon, I borrowed a cookbook from the clan library. I practiced small things. The tofu soup broke apart. The eggs stuck and burned. For dinner, I put stir-fried carrots and fried fish on the table. The carrots were bland. The fish was hard.

I did my homework at the low table and waited. The food cooled. Sleep dragged at me like a heavy blanket. Father never came home.

From then on, he was a ghost in the house we shared. When he did return, it was long after I had fallen asleep. The pantry slowly emptied, one item at a time. He never left money for groceries, never brought home ingredients. The eggs and oil vanished first. Then the salt and the last of the rice were gone. Soon, only a little canister of sugar and a half-empty tin of tea leaves remained.

After three days of a gnawing, aching hunger, I finally swallowed my pride and went to the clan’s communal kitchen. The doorway smelled of miso and steam. The woman in charge stared at me, her expression puzzled. This kitchen, the food it served, was for families with hardship coupons. The clan heir was never supposed to fall into that category.

I bowed my head low, shame burning my ears. My voice came out small. “I can help wash dishes,” I said. “I’ll come early tomorrow. Please.”

Her expression softened. She slid a bowl of rice, egg, and soup across the counter. My hands shook as I took it. Relief and shame and fear tangled inside me until I could hardly breathe. I chose a table that faced the meeting hall door.

He’s just busy, I told myself, clutching the warm bowl. He has to manage everything now. Father said I’m old enough to look after myself.

But a desperate, foolish hope took root in my chest. Maybe, if he came out of his meeting… maybe, if he saw me waiting here… he would join me. We wouldn’t have to talk. He wouldn’t have to smile. I wouldn’t cry. I wouldn’t ask for anything.

Father… I just want to sit with you again.

But he never came.

The house was too quiet after that. Silence filled places I didn’t know sound lived. It pressed on my ears. I threw myself into my duties until I couldn’t think.

The elders were pleased. My days became a frantic blur of school, martial arts training, and clan administration. If I stopped, the grief caught up, so I didn’t stop. Exhaustion hummed under my skin. My grades slipped. My training faltered. The elders’ solution was to pile on more work.

I was running on empty, and I knew Father saw the reports.

He tried to talk to me once. I was coming out of the training yard, shirt stuck to my back, hands trembling from holding stances too long. It had been one of those days where nothing fit. He found me by the gate.

“What is the problem?” he asked.

They were careful words. But they slid through the ache in my body and landed wrong. In my head they echoed as, Why are you so incompetent? What is wrong with you?

I wanted him to see the things I did right without me holding them up like trophies. I didn’t want another list of what I had done wrong. I couldn’t bear to disappoint him again. So I kept my eyes on the ground and said, “There’s no problem, Father. Everything is fine.”

He watched me for a long second. Then he nodded once, sharp. That was the end. We never spoke of it again.

The loneliness after that was a cold that ate slowly. Sometimes I wondered if he remembered I was still in the house. He left on a business trip without telling me. I found out from Elder Mei when she told me to deliver documents to the station.

He was gone five days. It felt like five years. When he returned, I ran to him at the door and hugged him. He patted my head once, distracted, and said he needed to rest. No apology. No question about how I had managed.

A short time later, I was hospitalized for a week with food poisoning. I stared at the door every time a shadow passed, but he never visited.

It was then that a cold understanding finally settled in me. My existence, my needs, my presence… it was all an inconvenience he had to work around.

I told myself to stop hoping.

So when I came home from school to find movers carrying boxes from our nearly empty house, I shouldn’t have been surprised. But a dread so cold it felt like ice water seized me.

He’s moving? Without telling me?

My blood ran cold when I looked inside and realized only my things remained, a small, sad pile in my room. Panic snapped tight in my chest.

He’s leaving me behind. He’s finally getting rid of me.

I ran after the foreman, my heart hammering. “Sir, excuse me! Where are you taking everything?”

“Oh, you’re the owner’s son?” he said, wiping sweat from his brow. “Your father left a message. Said you need to pack your things quickly if you want them moved with the rest.” He had his men hand me a few empty boxes. “Hurry up, kid. We need to be at the new place in half an hour.”

Relief hit so hard my knees almost buckled.

No. He wasn’t throwing me away. He still remembered I existed.

I packed like the house was on fire, throwing everything into boxes—no sorting, no order, just a desperate, frantic rush not to be left behind. I didn’t stop to think. I just moved.

The new house was our current main residence. The movers placed the furniture and left me alone in the vast, echoing silence. I walked through the empty rooms, my footsteps loud on the wooden floors. One bedroom for Father. One large room filled with Shiro’s boxed-up belongings. One smaller room with Raito’s toys and furniture.

There was no room for me.

My gaze fell on a small, dark, windowless storeroom by the back door. It smelled of dust and old paper. No one had left a note for it. It was a place for what didn’t belong anywhere else. For things that were forgotten, for things that didn’t quite belong. I stood in the doorway a long time. Then I carried my boxes in and called it mine.

I decided to make the new house spotless before he arrived. It felt like a way to make up for everything I couldn’t fix. I pushed heavy boxes against the walls, wiped windows, swept and mopped until the floors shone. I even unpacked my siblings’ things, arranging their belongings where they would look right, hoping it would make the house feel less empty. I made Father’s bed. I folded towels. Finally, I unpacked my few possessions in the stuffy storeroom and spread my futon on the floor.

It was close to midnight when Father came home. He took in the rooms with a glance. He didn’t mention the move. He didn’t comment on my hours of work. He told me our new location was a secret, and that it was my duty to set up a detector shield around the property that night.

It was a complex task. My hands shook with exhaustion as I followed the clan’s book of seals. I went out into the dark yard and tied paper slips to the corners where the fence met the posts. I traced the characters again and again until they felt right. Each time I tested the shield, it wavered and fell. I started over. I kept my breathing slow the way Elder Low taught us. Finally, on the fifth try, the air around the house gave a small shiver, and the paper fluttered once and stilled. The circle held.

When I reported back, Father came out. He inspected my work and gave a sharp nod. As he turned to leave, he placed a hand on my shoulder.

“Good work,” he said. And for the first time in what felt like a lifetime, he smiled at me.

My exhaustion, my hunger, the ache in my heart—it all evaporated in the warmth of that single touch. I smiled back without thinking. It hurt in a good way.

I collapsed onto my futon in the storeroom, my energy gone. The night air crept under the door and made me shiver. I reached for a blanket from the pile of clothes in the corner. My fingers brushed something soft and familiar. I froze.

It was Raito’s blanket. Light blue, with a faded red ball pattern. It must have gotten mixed in with my things. I clutched it, bringing the soft fabric to my face. It smelled faintly of the gentle fabric softener Shiro always insisted on using for him.

The smell turned a key in my chest. The lock I had been holding closed slid open. Their faces swam before my eyes.

Raito. Shiro. I miss you.

They had to be happy at Grandmother’s. Safe and cared for. It was my duty as their brother to be happy for them. I forced myself to remember that. I told myself not to be selfish, not to ask for things that would make their lives harder. I couldn’t ask them to come back to this empty, silent life.

That night, I curled around the blanket on my futon and let my tears fall where no one could see. I kept them quiet, the way I had learned to do everything.

I wiped my face on the edge of the blanket and made another small promise in the dark, the kind you make to yourself because you have to keep going.

I will be useful. I will not ask. I will keep watch over our home.

With my heart laid bare, I finally slept, clutching the blanket to my chest.
