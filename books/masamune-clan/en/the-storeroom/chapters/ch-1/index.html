Chapter 1

← Back to Masamune Clan Series [EN]

“ H ow was your day?”

It was the bell that ended the night for us. It came after the dishes were stacked, after Mother wiped my younger brother Raito’s mouth and set the steaming teapot down. It was a ritual from a time before clan meetings and the heavy robes, a time when I was just Tadashi. Father would ask it the same way every evening, his voice a low rumble across the table.

As a boy, I was starved for his attention. That question was a sliver of light under a locked door, a chance to make him look at me—truly look at me. So that night, I told him about the shed. I’d been bursting with it all through dinner, my legs jiggling under the low table.

I had found it by accident, playing hide-and-seek with Shiro out past the camellias, where the hedge grew thick and the ground sloped down. I saw the roof first, a dark hump half-swallowed by cypress trees. It looked like a slumbering monster. Inside, it smelled of wet wood and old metal, crammed with forgotten tools that lay about like sleeping animals with teeth. It felt like the sort of place ghosts might like. That made it perfect.

I had no idea it was her hiding spot; Shiro never told me secrets when there was a game on. I must have counted to one hundred three times, my voice growing thin in the twilight.

When I finally gave up, she popped out from behind a line of overgrown hedges, her laughter bright and triumphant. The sting of losing vanished, replaced by the secret thrill of our discovery. We chased each other home, our sleeves snagging on leaves, the whole afternoon a bright, skipping thing in my chest.

Hearing the story, a frown drew Mother’s brows together. “Tadashi, what were you doing in there? It’s a dangerous place. Full of broken glass and sharp, rusty nails.”

“I was careful, Mother,” I promised, my voice earnest.

“But he was in such a hurry he tripped,” Shiro added, breezy as ever, pouring her tea with two hands the way she’d seen Mother do. She didn’t look at me.

Mother’s worried gaze snapped back. “Are you hurt, Tadashi?”

I shook my head, though I could feel the faint, dull throb in my knee from where I had stumbled. A tiny price for an afternoon of adventure.

“Don’t play there again,” Mother said, her tone leaving no room for argument. “It’s too far for my voice to carry. Why were you out so long today, anyway?”

I hesitated. Shiro’s foot touched mine under the table, a quick, sharp warning. A part of the story I’d been told to leave out. But Mother could always tell when something was missing. Her eyes narrowed as if she could see the secret words I was holding on my tongue.

“Tadashi?” The warning was in her voice now, too.

Across the table, Father lowered his newspaper. The sound of the paper creasing was as loud as a slamming door. His eyes, dark and serious, fixed on me. The warmth of the room seemed to vanish. A place in my chest went cold and small. I confessed in a mumble, my eyes glued to the tatami mat. “Shiro… she got locked in the shed for a while.”

“Oh, my—”

“I’m fine, Mother!” Shiro cut in, shooting a furious glare at me. “It was nothing. I just called for Tadashi and he helped me climb out the window.”

That was not how it had felt. The truth was, after I’d run out of the spooky shed, the game ended and she still hadn’t appeared. The sky was turning purple. My gut went heavy with a real fear. I circled the building, my mouth dry, and there, through the grimy glass, I saw a shape moving. A dark silhouette. Small hands hit the pane with a rattling thud. For a breath, I almost ran, certain it was a ghost.

But then I heard my name, muffled and annoyed and scared all at once. “Tadashi!”

The door she’d slipped through had jammed shut. It took me what felt like hours to find a thick, sturdy branch to pry the window shutter open. The rusted hinge screamed as I put my whole body into it, the bark biting into my palms. My arms shook. When the wood finally gave, the edge scraped my skin raw. I lifted the window just enough for her to crawl out. Her cheeks were flushed, her hair stuck to her forehead. And then she stood up and laughed as if she’d planned it all. I laughed too, because she was out, and safe. Only then did the sting in my hands and knee rush back all at once.

It shouldn’t have been a big deal. But now my parents were watching me. Father wore the same expression he’d had when I was four and let go of Raito’s hand near the koi pond. The look he reserved for when my guard slipped, when I failed.

My stomach twisted into a tight, sick knot. I shrank into myself, sliding my hands from the table to clench the fabric of my hakama, waiting for the words I knew were coming like waiting for the rain you can already feel.

“You should have been watching her more carefully,” Father said, his voice low and heavy, each word a stone dropped into the quiet room. “What if something worse had happened? You are the eldest, Tadashi. It is your duty to keep them safe. Do you understand?”

I flinched as if he had struck me. “Yes, Father. I’m sorry.”

“You’re grounded for a week, and you’ll have extra chores. This weekend, you will help me clean out that shed.”

I bit my lip, imagining the spiders and the rust, and nodded quickly. “Yes, Father.”

“Oh, by the way, Father,” Shiro said, her smile quick as a sparrow changing direction. She took the air in the room and steered it. “I was learning striking theory with Elder Low today. He said I was the first to master the new form!”

Just like that, the storm passed over me and the sun came out for her. The tension broke. Father smiled at Shiro, a real smile that reached his eyes. Their conversation about stances and katas carried the rest of the meal.

I swallowed against the knot of jealousy in my throat. It tasted like ash. I wanted them to look at me like that. But she always outshone me. In the dojo, she was a rising star while I was just… there. I had no victories to share, only failures. I lowered my head and practiced not needing anything.

“How was your day?”

Father asked again a few weeks later. The question landed in the middle of the table, a prize to be won. I saw my chance and lunged for it. Maybe this time.

“Shiro and I were training with Elder Low today, Father. He said—”

“I’ve already spoken with Elder Low,” Father said, cutting me off without looking up.

The words died in my throat. Before I could recover, Shiro seized the opening. “Tadashi was sluggish, Father!” she announced, and launched into a breathless report of the day’s lesson. Her eyes shone. She wanted to win even this.

Lately, everything felt like a small contest we did not name. Shiro couldn’t stand to be second to me in anything. She didn’t need to worry. She was better in every way that mattered to Father. Couldn’t you leave this one thing for me? I shot her a wounded look, but it bounced off her. She was unstoppable.

I knew excuses did not live long in Father’s world. You simply accepted your failings and their consequences. Still, my knee throbbed under the table from a fall during horse-riding practice that morning. The swelling made my hakama tight around the joint. A hot, pulsing knot of pain. I thought, If I say it, will he hear me? Or will he just say, ‘Use your other leg’ ? The words stuck in my mouth and became nothing.

After Shiro finished, Father turned to me, his brow creased with that familiar look of disapproval. “I’ve seen your training report. Elder Low is right. Starting tomorrow, you will begin your morning exercises an hour earlier.”

The sentence landed hard. An extra hour? My knee throbbed in protest. The thought of more running, more drills in the pre-dawn cold, made my stomach churn. I wanted to object, to explain, but then I saw Mother look up from feeding Raito, her eyes flicking between Father and me, her face pale with a silent plea.

“Tadashi?” Father prompted, his gaze unwavering. “The heir selection is fast approaching. You cannot afford these weak reports. To be chosen, you must surpass Shiro. Do you understand?”

Ah.

A few days before, hidden in the hallway, I had heard their voices through the paper wall. Father’s, sharp and insistent. Mother’s, tight and low.

“He is not ready,” Father had said.

“He is our eldest,” Mother replied. “He carries more than she does. He breaks himself trying to please you.”

“The clan needs strength. Shiro is the best candidate.”

“And I say he should be the only candidate. He will be crushed if you set them against each other.”

“Both names are already registered,” Father said, and the paper had rustled like dry leaves.

I had stood there, breath held, wanting to be good enough to end their argument, or to be nothing at all so they would not have to fight. I could not be both. So I chose the only thing I could do. I chose to be quiet.

Clutching my aching knee under the table, I swallowed the pain and the protest. “Yes, Father,” I said, my voice steadier than I felt. “I’ll start earlier tomorrow.”

“How were things at home today?” Father asked, but this time, the question was for Mother.

The heir selection was over. Shiro earned the highest score, a result that surprised no one. But then, in front of all the elders, she had withdrawn. Father called a vote, and they accepted me as the clan heir in her place. A fragile, uncertain peace had settled over our home, like dust that stays until someone moves too fast.

Mother smiled, a genuine, relaxed smile. She recounted Raito’s mischief—a chaotic attempt to “help” in the kitchen that ended with a fine dusting of flour over every surface, including the cat. I remembered it well: Raito’s giggling as he’d tossed a soft puff of flour at my face. I’d caught him, tickling him until he shrieked, and turned his energy into a game of cleaning up before Mother saw the worst of it.

“Well, who was watching him?” Father asked, his brow furrowing as his gaze, by habit, fell on me.

“I was, Father,” I said quickly, my heart giving a little jump. “It’s all cleaned up.”

It was no great feat, not like mastering a kata. But I held a tiny, foolish flicker of hope. I did not dream big. A small smile can fill an entire evening when you are hungry enough.

He only gave a slight nod. Then his attention turned to Shiro, asking about a new mochi recipe. As she launched into an enthusiastic story, I quietly folded my little hope into a thin, tight thing, the way you fold paper until it is hard and cannot unfold without tearing.

Don’t make a scene. Don’t ruin the peace, I told myself. I pulled Raito onto my lap. He was warm and heavy, and smelled of flour and soy. He leaned back against my chest, relaxing the way children do when they feel safe without thinking about it.

Soon, Father excused himself to rest. But as he stood, he did something I never expected. He walked around the table and stopped behind me. He placed a hand on my shoulder. The weight of it was warm and solid.

“Look after your siblings and help your mother, Tadashi.”

I looked up. He was smiling at me. It wasn’t the brilliant, proud smile he gave Shiro for her victories. It was something else—quieter, softer. A look of acknowledgement. I stared, my throat tight.

Father… he had been paying attention after all. He’d seen the flour, the game, the cleanup. He’d seen it all.

Something opened in my chest, like a window you didn’t know was stuck until it finally moved. A warmth I hadn’t realized I was so starved for spread through me, like the sun breaking through a week of cold rain. I could only nod, my lips parting in a real smile of my own, one that wasn’t shadowed by hope or fear.

Maybe my aptitude for training would never match Shiro’s brilliance. Maybe I would never win his praise for my accomplishments with a sword. But perhaps there was another kind of strength, one that didn’t make a loud noise or win any contests. A quiet strength that he saw all the same.

I will protect this family, Father, I vowed in the small, private place where my thoughts were always louder than my voice. I will give it my all.
