Chapter 4

← Back to Masamune Clan Series [EN]

I didn’t know it at the time, but it was Anko who spoke to Father about me. I suppose it was her quiet words that prompted him to find a more secure house for us, then bring Shiro and Raito home.

When my siblings returned from Grandmother’s, it felt like breathing after holding my breath too long. I didn’t know if Shiro had forgiven me for what happened to Mother. The white-hot anger that had burned in her eyes was gone, replaced by a quiet, weary sadness. She just stood there, thin and tired. I pulled them both into an embrace so tight my own bones ached, my body trembling with the fear of ever being separated again. I buried my face in Raito’s hair and never wanted to let go. A silent, desperate prayer formed in the depths of my heart, words I could never say aloud.

Forgive me. Please, forgive me. I’ll be a better brother. I’ll be more patient, more attentive. I know I can’t replace Mother, but I will give you everything I have. I will do whatever it takes to make you feel safe and loved. Please let me try. Shiro, Raito… I love you so much.

After they came home, Father handed the grocery money to Shiro. She had Mother’s touch in the kitchen, a natural grace I could never master, and we began eating dinner together again. Father started checking our food supply, and he always came home for dinner, no matter how bad the weather.

We were all present, but the table was cold and quiet. The easy chatter was gone. Shiro, who used to be so talkative, said nothing. Raito was even quieter, clinging to my lap like a small, frightened koala, his fists perpetually knotted in the fabric of my shirt. He was a warm, heavy anchor, and he wouldn’t fuss as long as I held him.

Father never asked how we were doing. He looked exhausted, the lines around his eyes deeper than I had ever seen them. I assumed the clan was in trouble. During meals, he would glance briefly at Shiro and then at Raito, a quick, almost clinical check to see that they were breathing, that they were fed, that they were alright. Then he would eat in silence, thank Shiro for the meal, and retreat to his study. The door slid shut like a period at the end of a short sentence.

It took a week for the fog in my own head to clear enough for me to see it: Shiro was adrift. She used to love talking with Father about everything, matching his wit, sharing her triumphs. Now she had fallen silent, her vibrant, fiery energy muted to a dull ember. I realized with a jolt of guilt that she hadn’t just lost Mother. She was losing Father, too. I had been so focused on keeping the house from falling apart that I missed her slipping.

That night, after Father left the table, I stayed. Raito sat in my lap, listlessly pushing wooden blocks around. Shiro was clearing the dishes when she paused, sensing me watching her.

“Is something wrong, Tadashi?”

I offered her a small, hesitant smile and reached across the table, my hand gently covering hers where it rested on a stack of bowls. It was a clumsy gesture, but it was all I had.

“It’s nothing,” I said softly. “I just… I wanted to talk with you. How were you and Raito today?”

She froze, her eyes widening slightly in surprise. Then, slowly, a real smile spread across her face—a small, fragile sunbeam in the quiet, shadowed room.

I forget the exact night Father started asking again. Maybe the trouble at the clan had finally passed.

“How are you?” he began one evening. The old shape of the question startled me.

I waited for Shiro to jump at the chance, to talk about recipes or drills, but she only glanced at me. Her expression was unreadable.

My mouth went dry. I had wanted this for so long and now my thoughts scattered. It was my turn to fill the silence, but I couldn’t find anything interesting to say.

“Everything’s… fine, Father,” I stammered. “I had an exam this morning—”

“How is Raito?” Father cut in, his voice quiet but firm.

Ah.

A hot, familiar flush of shame crept up my neck. Of course. It was so obvious. He didn’t want a report on me. He wanted a report on my duties.

I looked down at Raito, who had drifted off to sleep on my lap, his mouth open a little, breath warm through my shirt. He was full and warm from dinner. I cleared my throat and forced myself to meet Father’s gaze.

“He’s doing well, Father. He doesn’t cry when I leave for school anymore.”

Father nodded, then followed with more questions—about Raito’s appetite, about Shiro’s studies, about the state of the house. I answered with the small pieces I had collected from the day. What Shiro had said about prices. How many apples were left. Whether Raito ate the egg or the rice first.

“You need to pay closer attention to them, Tadashi,” he said finally.

The words stunned me. I looked at my hands on the table. They had little cuts from cleaning the stovetop. Raito’s weight was a deadening pressure on my legs, which were numb with pins and needles. He had clung to me since he came home, afraid if I stood up I would disappear. I carried him on my back while sweeping. I chopped vegetables with him on my hip. I did the chores Shiro couldn’t reach because of the pot on the stove. I was bone-tired, perpetually exhausted, but I kept my face steady so they wouldn’t worry. I never complained, never let them see how thin I was being stretched.

And all of that, it seemed, was still not enough.

“Tadashi?” Father’s voice was impatient now. “Do you understand?”

I flinched and bowed my head. “Yes, Father. I understand,” I whispered, my own voice hoarse with a defeat I couldn’t name. “I’m sorry. I’ll do better next time.”

Time passed. The house found a rhythm that didn’t break if you breathed on it.

Raito stopped checking the door every five minutes. He began to play at Father’s feet sometimes, then wander back to me like a little bird that needed to touch home. Shiro stayed quieter than before, but the hollow in her eyes filled in. She read everything she could get her hands on. She asked me to show her my forms and practiced them in the hall with careful feet.

Father started calling me to his study. He spoke to me about clan things—supply lists, borders, names. He asked me to copy letters. I sat on the edge of the tatami and kept my back straight. Every evening, he asked his question.

“How are things at home today?”

My job was to give the right answer. When I did, he nodded, satisfied, and the talk ended. It felt like passing an exam. I learned to bring him what he wanted and leave the rest in the storeroom with the boxes.

One evening, the world was slow and thick around me. Heat rolled under my skin. My joints ached with a dull, persistent throb. I sat at the table anyway and held my cup so he wouldn’t see my hands shake.

I didn’t answer the nightly question right away. The fever wrecked me, and my mind scrambled to find an answer.

I wanted to say, I’m sick, Father. I have an exam tomorrow, but my head hurts too much to read. I’m trying, but if my grade is bad tomorrow, please don’t be angry. I promise I’ll make it up after I’m well.

But that wasn’t the report he wanted to hear.

“We’re fine, Father,” I said softly, my voice weak. I told him that Raito had eaten, that Shiro wanted to try a new recipe, that the roof in the storeroom was leaking and I had put a bowl under it.

“Can’t you fix it yourself?” he asked, his tone sharp with criticism. “Call a handyman if you must, but watch how he does it, so you can learn to do it on your own next time.”

My cheeks burned hot. Of course. How could I have been so stupid? A simple repair. He must think I’m useless.

“Yes, Father,” I mumbled. “I’ll… I’ll figure it out tomorrow.”

Later, the fever pulled me under. I didn’t hear Shiro come in until the futon dipped beside me. I was shivering under the blankets and didn’t hear her enter.

“We’re out of fever medicine,” she stated. Her voice was matter-of-fact. She set a cup of hot tea by my hand and pressed her cool palm to my forehead. “You’re burning up. Why didn’t you ask Father for more money?”

“Sorry… I forgot,” I whispered. It was a lie. I hadn’t forgotten. I was just too afraid to ask for something for myself. I couldn’t bring myself to risk the annoyed or disappointed look in Father’s eyes again.

“Fine. I’ll just tell Father the price of onions went up,” she said, placing a cool, damp cloth on my forehead. The relief was immediate. “I’ll get you some proper herbal medicine tomorrow.”

I swallowed against the sudden, hard lump in my throat. “T-thank you, Shiro. Sorry I always cause you trouble.”

She scoffed and took my hand, her fingers firm around mine, her grip surprisingly strong. “What are you talking about? You’re the least troublesome person in the world. You’ve been doing too much. You probably collapsed from exhaustion.”

The words sank in like medicine that finally reached the right place. After I had stopped expecting anything from Father, I taught myself not to expect it from anyone. I hadn’t noticed Shiro watching me so closely. I hadn’t realized she saw how much I was carrying.

“Get some rest,” she urged, her voice gentle. “You have to take care of yourself. Raito and I… we need you.”

Ah. Shiro…

I bit my lip as a warmth spread through my chest and made my eyes sting. I squeezed her hand, my fingers tightening around hers, and let the quiet, aching gratitude wash over me.

The question came again one night, as it always did. Father was asking me, but after so many years, so many wrong answers, I finally understood.

He wasn’t asking about me. He wasn’t interested in my life, my struggles, my small victories. Maybe he already knew from the reports on my grades from the elders, or maybe, my well-being just wasn’t a priority for him.

It hurt, but that was alright. A small, quiet part of me had finally made its peace with that.

As a son, I still wanted his hand on my shoulder, his eyes light with pride. I still yearned for his affection with a silent, hopeless ache. That want didn’t go away. But as a brother, I knew what I had to do.

When I stopped hoping to be seen by him, I finally saw what was in front of me. A little boy who slept better if I hummed. A sister who would set her own needs aside unless I asked her to sit and talk. A roof that leaked unless I learned how to seal it. A house that held together if I kept moving.

“How are things at home today, Tadashi?” my father asked, his gaze expectant.

I took a slow breath, summoned a faint, steady smile, and began to tell him the story of how my siblings were doing.

The rain began again, bringing back the insistent drip from the storeroom ceiling. I took the roll of tape and the sealant the handyman had used. Standing on the stool in the dark, I traced the crack before sealing it, each press of my finger a quiet attempt to keep something from breaking.

Drop by drop, the leak stilled.

I pushed my futon back beneath the patched spot and lay down, hoping it wouldn’t break again. The room smelled faintly of glue and clean cotton, an imitation of comfort that never reached my bones.

In the dark, I held the edges of Raito’s blue blanket and listened to the house breathe. Shiro coughed once down the hall. Father’s study door slid shut.

His question came back to me from far away, stripped to its bones. In my mind, I saw the worry etched in Mother’s eyes.

How are things at home, Tadashi?

The promises I’d made to her pressed heavy in my chest: take care of my siblings. Obey my father, make him proud. Protect what little we had left.

Could I even do it? The question hung in the darkness, unanswered. The house breathed around me, hollow.

We’re still here, Mother.

It wasn’t enough, but for now, it was all I had to offer.

I pressed my face into the worn fabric of the blanket, clinging to the faint trace of warmth it held, and holding on until my fingers ached.
