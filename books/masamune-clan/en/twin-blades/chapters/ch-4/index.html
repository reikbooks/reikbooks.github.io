O ne of Roroa’s men, a hulking brute in a black fedora, hauled Tadashi to his feet and forced him to kneel. From my vantage point, I could see Tadashi’s face clearly in the flickering lamplight.

His hair was plastered to his forehead with sweat, his eyes bloodshot, his lips dry and cracked. His shoulders shook with the effort of suppressing a tremor, but he met Roroa’s gaze with defiance.

Roroa backhanded him across the face. The blow sent Tadashi reeling, but the brute in the fedora yanked him back up.

“My man in Keniiwari tells me you’re the one responsible for slaughtering all those wolves guarding our crops,” Roroa said, his voice dangerously soft.

I frowned. There was a hillside near the farm where Tadashi had been sent. Rows of neatly planted trees. I’d assumed it was a tea plantation. It wasn’t.

Tadashi remained silent. The speech seal! He couldn’t answer. He just glared at Roroa, his eyes cold and hard.

Roroa’s eyes narrowed, and he gestured to his men. They descended on Tadashi, their fists and boots a blur of violence. It was brief, brutal, and effective. When they hauled him back to his feet, blood trickled from his nose, staining his already bloodied shirt.

“You think I’m an idiot?” Roroa snarled. “You had the nerve to try to use my sister to get to me? I knew that Sando character was bad news, but you, you’re even dumber than he is. You actually trusted Misao to bring you here? My sweet little sister? Tell me, who sent you?”

They slammed Tadashi back to the floor, their boots connecting with his ribs. Shit, they were going to kill him! I had to do something, but I was trapped. No way out. And I had no idea how many men Roroa had. I had to be smart about this.

“Last chance,” Roroa growled. “Who sent you?”

My heart stuttered to a stop as Tadashi spoke.

“Sando’s father,” he rasped, his voice rough with pain.

Silence. Roroa stared at him, his face a mask of disbelief. I wasn’t faring much better.

Then it hit me. The old man hadn’t put a speech seal on Tadashi for this mission. He couldn’t talk about what happened in Keniiwari, but he could talk about me. About my mission.

“What the hell are you talking about?” Roroa snapped. “Who is this Sando, exactly?”

A harsh laugh erupted from Tadashi’s throat, a sound that was more like a strangled gasp.

“He’s next in line to be the Masamune clan leader,” Tadashi wheezed, a manic grin splitting his bruised lips. I could practically feel another headache coming on.

Surprise flickered across Roroa’s face.

“So Sando’s father is the clan leader?”

Tadashi shrugged, still grinning like a madman. “You kill me, you kill Sando, his father will hunt you down. He’ll tear your whole operation apart, piece by piece, until there’s nothing left.”

He was bluffing, stalling for time. But would it work?

“I don’t give a damn about your clan!” Roroa roared. “They’re the ones who started this, sending you two here to poke around in my business.”

Tadashi shook his head. “You started it, Roroa. You got one of our own arrested. Akane Masamune. Ring any bells?”

“Tch. Never heard of her,” Roroa scoffed. “Not my fault if she’s got a taste for the good stuff.”

“Don’t play stupid,” Tadashi snarled, the words coming out with surprising venom. His usual goody-two-shoes persona often made me forget he’d been trained to lie, to manipulate people during missions. “Two months ago, your boy Mio set Akane up. He gave her a bag of weed, and she got busted for it.”

Roroa’s eyes narrowed to slits. “Who is this Akane? Your little girlfriend?”

“She’s my best friend, you moron!” Tadashi shot back, the lie rolling off his tongue with practiced ease. “If you hadn’t used Mio, she wouldn’t get into this mess—”

Roroa’s fist slammed into Tadashi’s jaw, cutting him off mid-sentence.

“You think you’re so high and mighty, huh?” Roroa roared, his voice shaking with rage.

“Get Mio in here!” he barked at his men.

Roroa grabbed Tadashi’s chin, forcing him to look up. “You were willing to poison yourself to find the man who got your precious best friend arrested? That’s dedication. Or maybe just plain stupidity. So, tell me, how does it feel? The drug. Pretty good, huh?”

Tadashi’s jaw clenched, but he didn’t answer. Roroa studied him for a moment, a frown creasing his forehead.

“You’re in withdrawal. You’re hurting, aren’t you?” Roroa said, his voice deceptively gentle. “Why did you stop taking it?”

Tadashi mumbled something incoherent. Roroa released his chin and grabbed a fistful of his hair, yanking his head back. “Speak up, you spineless coward!”

Tadashi closed his eyes, drawing a shaky breath.

“Sando… he made me stop.”

“And you listened to him?” Roroa asked, a cruel smile twisting his lips. “You clan people are so strange. He’s your master, is he?”

“He locked me in his room,” Tadashi said, his voice barely a whisper. “Said he wouldn’t let me out until I stopped.”

“Just like a master locking his dog in a cage,” Roroa chuckled. “You’re a pathetic joke.”

Shouts erupted from outside, the sound of splintering wood and clashing steel. The library doors burst open, and several of Roroa’s men stumbled inside, dragging a young man with them. Mio. Round-faced, sweating, his hands bound behind his back.

“Well, well, well. Look who we have here. Mio. Come closer. Let’s have a little chat.”

Mio went still, his eyes widening in fear as he recognized Roroa.

“Remember our last conversation?” Roroa asked, his voice a soft purr. “Should I take another finger? Just to refresh your memory?”

Mio shook his head frantically. “N-no! I’ve been careful! I swear, it won’t happen again, Boss!”

Roroa tutted, shaking his head. “You see, Mio, your little mistake has created quite a mess. Seems you’ve attracted the attention of the Masamune clan. Even the clan leader’s son is involved. You understand what that means, don’t you?”

Mio’s eyes widened in terror. He shook his head, a whimper escaping his lips. Roroa grabbed a fistful of his hair, yanking his head back, forcing him to meet his gaze.

“I built this empire with my own two hands. I’ve kept it hidden, safe from prying eyes. But now the Masamune clan thinks they can waltz in and destroy everything I’ve worked for. They’re making us vulnerable. The cops will be crawling all over this place before long.” Roroa released Mio’s hair and nodded curtly at Misao.

“Initiate emergency protocol. We’re moving. Tonight.”

Misao nodded and disappeared through the shoji screen. Roroa beckoned one of his men closer, murmuring instructions.

Roroa turned back to Mio, his gaze cold and calculating. “You heard me, Mio. You’ve cost me a lot tonight. There are consequences for failure.”

The guard returned, carrying a long, flat case. He opened it, revealing rows of gleaming syringes.

“Boss, please! I’m sorry! I won’t—” A hand clamped over Mio’s mouth, stifling his pleas. He thrashed against the guards who held him down, but it was no use. Roroa selected a syringe, inspecting it carefully before plunging it into Mio’s arm.

The effect was almost instantaneous.

Mio’s body went rigid, then convulsed violently, froth bubbling at his lips. The guards released him, stepping back as his lifeless form crumpled to the floor. Roroa watched, a cruel smile playing on his lips. Then he turned to Tadashi.

“What do you think, Masamune?” Roroa asked, his voice laced with amusement. “Which is better, death by overdose, or a slow, agonizing death in a prison cell? No need for me to get my hands dirty. I’ll just give you a little boost. The cops will find you here, next to your friend Mio, and haul you away. Who’s going to believe a junkie? Especially one with no evidence.”

He chuckled softly. “I can even make it look like you’re the one who got her hooked. Your clan will disown you, leave you to rot in prison. You and your precious best friend, dying together. Poetic, isn’t it?”

Roroa reached for another syringe case. But before he could open it, the shoji screen flew open, and Misao stumbled inside, her face pale with panic.

“We’re under attack!” she gasped.

The cavalry had arrived. I smiled grimly. It had to be my father’s men.

“This way! The back’s still clear!” Misao shouted, leading the way.

Roroa didn’t hesitate. He tossed the syringe case to one of his men and bolted after Misao, his guards right behind him. The remaining guard kicked Tadashi, sending him sprawling face-first onto the tatami mat.

I didn’t wait for him to draw another syringe.

I moved like a shadow, silent and swift, snapping his neck before he even knew I was there. The guard crumpled to the floor, landing with a thud beside Tadashi.

Tadashi groaned, rolling onto his back. “What took you so long?” he grumbled.

I nudged the unconscious guard with my foot and quickly untied Tadashi’s hands.

“You know how it is,” I said, my voice light. “Had to stop for a coffee and donuts. Traffic was a nightmare.”

I helped him up. He looked like hell—pale, sweaty, his body trembling. The withdrawal was kicking in again. He was in no shape to fight, but I handed him his katana and tanto anyway. He gripped them tightly, his hands shaking.

“Sadi, I…” His voice cracked.

I didn’t give him a chance to finish. I pulled him along, hurrying down the hallway. It was deserted.

Roroa must have sent everyone to fight off the attackers. The wall opposite the bathroom swung inward, revealing a dark opening. A secret passage.

I ducked into the passage, but Tadashi doubled over, retching violently into the toilet.

“Damn it, Tadashi!” I swore under my breath. “I’m going after them. Meet me at the other end of this tunnel, alright?”

Tadashi nodded weakly, still clinging to the toilet bowl for support.

The passage was narrow, the air stale and damp. The floor was slick with moisture, and I lost my footing for a moment, my hand scraping against the rough stone. I scrambled back up. The tunnel opened into the pine forest behind the factory. The trees weren’t too thick here, and the ground sloped upwards, giving me a clear view of the factory below. I spotted them in the distance—Misao, Roroa, and the brute in the fedora. Running.

I palmed a handful of throwing knives as I closed the distance between us. Misao was in the lead, making her an easy target. I let a knife fly.

The knife spun through the air, finding its mark with a sickening thud.

“AAGH!” Misao crumpled to the ground, clutching her leg. Roroa yelled something, helping her up, but the brute in the fedora spun around, his hand a blur as he hurled a volley of knives in my direction. I dove for cover behind a thick pine.

The knives thudded into the tree trunk inches from my head. I risked a glance. Roroa was gone, the bastard, leaving Misao to fend for herself.

The brute saw me. He grinned, a cruel, predatory smile, and charged. I raised my katana just in time, barely parrying his blow.

He was wielding some kind of oversized, double-edged broadsword. He wasn’t particularly strong, but the sheer weight of the weapon was enough to make my arms ache with the effort of blocking his blows.

The weapon’s size was its own weakness, though. It was slow, clumsy. I waited for my moment, then struck.

My katana slipped past his defenses, finding its mark.

“AAGH!” He roared in pain, clutching his side. I kicked him away, not wasting time to see if he’d stay down, and sprinted after Roroa.

Stupid. I should have finished him off. I’d only gone a few steps when he was suddenly there, a mountain of muscle and fury, his broadsword whistling through the air, aimed for my head.

Steel met steel, the clash of blades echoing through the forest.

Tadashi stood in front of the brute, his katana held in a defensive stance, his face pale with exertion. I didn’t hesitate. I lunged, my blade slicing through the brute’s armor, ripping open his stomach. This time, I made sure he wouldn’t get up again.

“Take care of Misao. I’m going after Roroa.”

Tadashi nodded, and I was off, sprinting through the trees. I followed Roroa’s trail through the undergrowth, bursting out of the trees to find myself at the edge of a small clearing. A pond shimmered in the moonlight. On the far side, Roroa was arguing with someone. A man in a long, gray coat, his hair and eyes as white as snow.

Mitsurugi.

“—been doing your dirty work for years—” Roroa’s voice was a desperate whine.

“That was then, this is now,” the man in the gray coat said coldly. “I’ll inform Shirasu—”

The man in the gray coat stopped mid-sentence, his gaze fixing on me. He ignored Roroa completely, turning towards me, his hand disappearing beneath his coat.

He didn’t attack, not yet. His eyes—those unsettling yellow eyes—locked onto mine, and a wave of dizziness washed over me. I tried to break eye contact, but I couldn’t.

“Who the hell are you?” I growled, forcing the words out. The sound of my own voice seemed to break the spell.

The man in the gray coat smiled, a slow, predatory smile that sent a shiver down my spine.

“Well, well,” he drawled, his voice smooth as silk. “You must be Sando. Roroa told me about you. And your little friend, Taka. The Masamune clan’s twin blades. You’ve been busy, haven’t you? Killing my pets.”

“So you’re the one who sicced those things on us?” I snarled, launching myself at him, my katana a blur of silver in the moonlight.

The man laughed, a chilling sound that echoed through the trees. He flicked his wrist, and something small and metallic whizzed past my head. I ducked, barely avoiding it, then leaped back as a set of iron claws shot out, missing me by inches.

His defenses were impeccable. My blades met only steel, my attacks deflected with effortless ease. He pressed his attack, relentless, a whirlwind of flashing steel and deadly precision. I backflipped, narrowly avoiding a blow that would have cleaved me in two.

“Down!” Tadashi roared.

I dropped to the ground, trusting his instincts. Something sharp whistled past, missing me by a hair’s breadth, then returned to the man in the gray coat’s outstretched hand.

A boomerang! Of course.

“Well, hello there, Taka,” the man said, a cruel smile twisting his lips. “Long time no see. How’s that lovely girlfriend of yours?”

I stared at Tadashi, my heart pounding. He knew this guy? Tadashi’s face was ashen, but his expression remained impassive.

“Seishiro,” he said, his voice flat, devoid of emotion.

Seishiro let out a shrill whistle. Shadows shifted in the trees, and five wolves emerged from the darkness, circling us, their yellow eyes gleaming with predatory hunger. I backed away, pressing my back against Tadashi’s, our blades raised. From the corner of my eye, I saw Roroa. He was staring at the wolves, his face pale with terror, and then he turned and ran.

I cursed under my breath. He was getting away! I needed Roroa alive. He was the only lead I had now that Mio was dead.

Damn it! These damn wolves were just a distraction!

“Hold them off,” I hissed. “I’m going after Roroa.”

Tadashi stiffened. If he’d been at full strength, I wouldn’t have hesitated to use him as bait. But like this… I doubted he could even stand, let alone fight.

No choice.

“Five minutes,” Tadashi rasped, and then he was moving, his katana flashing in the moonlight. I was right beside him, our blades working in tandem, taking down three of the beasts before they could react.

Tadashi spun, his blade a whirlwind of steel, slashing at the remaining wolves, driving them back. Then he turned and ran, the wolves hot on his heels.

“Sending your friend to die for you?” Seishiro clucked his tongue disapprovingly. “What a shame. His father will be devastated.”

I ignored him, pressing my attack, forcing him back, my blades a relentless storm of steel.

I didn’t care about technique or strategy anymore. I just wanted to hit him. To make him pay. Seishiro was good, though. He moved like a phantom, dodging, weaving, always just out of reach. The boomerang was a constant threat, a blur of metal that kept me off balance. It came whistling back, closer this time, too fast to dodge.

“Down!”

I hit the ground, rolling to the side. The claws raked across my arm, tearing through my sleeve, but Seishiro was off balance, overextended.

A heavy thud. A grunt of pain.

I looked up. A spear protruded from Seishiro’s shoulder, the shaft quivering.

Father’s spear.

Seishiro roared in pain, yanking the spear free, then disappeared into the trees. I scrambled to my feet, ignoring the old man as I sprinted towards Roroa. The drug lord was about to wade across a shallow stream when I caught up to him, grabbing him by the collar.

One punch. That’s all it took to put him down. I bound his hands and feet with rope, working quickly. Father materialized beside me, his expression grim. I handed him the ledger I’d taken from Roroa’s office.

“They killed Mio,” I said, my voice tight. “This is Roroa, the one who’s been running the operation. And these are his records. Enough to clear Akane’s name.”

Father tucked the ledger under his arm. “Good work.”

“Misao’s over there,” I said, nodding towards the trees. “Roroa’s sister. She’s in on it, too.”

“Where do you think you’re going?” Father snapped, his eyes narrowing.

“Tadashi needs help,” I said, my voice clipped. “Those wolves are still out there.”

“Let them finish him!” the old man snarled.

I stared at him, my blood running cold.

He couldn’t be serious.

I shook my head. Father could punish me later. Right now, Tadashi needed me.

“I’m going,” I said, and then I was gone, racing back towards the sound of the fight. Come on, Tadashi. Please don’t be dead .

I found him on the ground, his back pressed against a tree, a wolf clinging to his shoulder, its fangs sunk deep into his flesh. Tadashi screamed, but I didn’t hesitate. I used the wolf’s distraction to my advantage.

Two quick strokes. A slash, a stab. The wolf released Tadashi, collapsing beside him.

I dragged him away from the dead wolf. He was covered in blood, his own and the wolf’s. His face was ashen, his hair matted with sweat, his eyes vacant.

“Tadashi, talk to me. Where are you hurt?” I asked, my eyes scanning his body, taking in the damage.

Tadashi shook his head, wincing as pain shot through him. “Shoulder,” he gasped.

I hauled him to his feet. Eight hours. That’s how long it would take us to get back to the city. And once we got there, we’d have to report to the clan immediately.

No way he could make it. And if we did make it back, and he didn’t report in right away… they’d know. They’d see the withdrawal. I didn’t know what they’d do to him, but whatever it was, it wouldn’t be pretty.

I glanced around, my mind racing, the map of the area flashing through my head. The beach house. The one I’d rented for Misao. An hour. Maybe less. I didn’t explain. Just pulled Tadashi along, guiding him through the trees. We made it to the villa just as he collapsed, unconscious.

For the next three days, he was in and out of it, fighting the fever, the pain from the wolf venom. I stayed with him, tending to his wounds, keeping him hydrated. The villa had some food, enough to get by. And luckily, Tadashi had brought some clothes and extra cash. I used it to extend our stay.

Tadashi kept asking about the clan, about going back, but I wouldn’t let him leave. I kept him locked in one of the bedrooms, for his own good.

It took a week, a full seven days of hell, but the withdrawal symptoms finally subsided. I made him promise that if he felt the urge to use again, if the cravings came back, he’d come to me. Only then did I let him out of that room.

After that, together we returned home.

We arrived back at the compound in the late afternoon, heading straight to the main hall for our debriefing. Shiro was inside, speaking with Elder Yun. She turned as we entered, her eyes widening in surprise, and then she glared at me, her expression hardening.

“You!” she spat, storming towards me. Before I could react, she had me by the collar, her hand connecting with my cheek in a resounding slap.

“Shiro, stop!” Tadashi shouted, stepping between us. Shiro released me, her chest heaving.

“Shut it!” Shiro rounded on Tadashi, her voice laced with venom. “You’ve been suspended ever since you abandoned your mission. Get in there and kneel in front of the council!”

Tadashi flinched as if she’d struck him, too, then bowed his head respectfully and disappeared into the courtroom.

It was obvious that Shiro had been filling in as acting clan leader while Tadashi and I were gone. The girl was sharp, a natural leader. No surprise she’d aced the preliminary test. The elders and clan officials seemed pleased with her performance.

I kept glancing over at Tadashi. He was kneeling, his gaze fixed on the tatami mat in front of him. I was worried he might crack, have another seizure or something, but he held it together. When the questioning began, he answered calmly, his voice steady. Then it was my turn.

I didn’t mention the drugs. I didn’t want the clan to punish him for it. I knew Father would grill me about it later, but I could always say I’d given him the stuff but he’d recovered quickly.

They asked about our delayed return, and I hesitated. There was no winning here. Tell the truth, I’d be punished. Lie, I’d be punished.

“It’s my fault,” Tadashi said abruptly. He swallowed hard, his gaze fixed on the floor. “I was injured during the wolf attack. I told Sadi to stay with me, to make sure I recovered fully before we returned.”

I stared at him, stunned. He wouldn’t meet my eyes. He avoided the gazes of his father and sister, too, both of whom were watching him with barely concealed disapproval. He just knelt there, head bowed, as the elders delivered their verdict.

I clenched my fists, my nails digging into my palms.

Why? I’d gone out of my way to protect him, and he’d just thrown it all away.

My eyes flicked towards my father. He was smiling. A cold, satisfied smile that sent a shiver down my spine. Whenever he looked that pleased, it usually meant I was about to get off easy. So this was it? Tadashi was taking the fall for me?

Something twisted in my chest, a mix of gratitude and anger. Tadashi’d shielded me from my father’s wrath, even though I hadn’t asked him to. My problems with the old man were far from over. He still held the leash. But I wasn’t alone anymore.

We’ll figure this out , Tadashi’s silence seemed to say.

Standing there in that stifling courtroom, surrounded by the judging eyes of the clan, with Tadashi by my side, I finally understood.

Tadashi deserved to be the clan leader.

As the elders dismissed us, I grabbed Tadashi’s arm before the guards could lead him away to solitary confinement. I knew what I had to do. I knew what side I was on.

Tadashi looked at me, his brow furrowed with concern. I gave him a small, reassuring smile.

I placed my clenched fist over my heart, then brought it down, placing it firmly in his open palm.

Yoroshiku.

You have my loyalty.

I will follow and protect you.

A promise. A new beginning.

A look of understanding, of gratitude, of something akin to hope, dawned on his face, and for the first time in a long time, I felt a glimmer of it, too.

Tadashi nodded, a faint smile touching his lips, and then the guards led him away.

Epilogue - Sadi Masamune

Epilogue - Sadi Masamune

I was waiting for Tadashi outside the library when he emerged, blinking, into the sunlight. Two weeks of solitary confinement had taken their toll. He was thinner, his face drawn and pale, his eyes shadowed. He walked with a slight limp, his shoulders slumped, his gaze fixed on the ground.

Fourteen days in a dark, silent cell. No human contact, except for the whipping sessions.

Brutal.

I’d done seven days once. It was enough to make me want to claw my way out of my own skin. I shook my head, approaching him cautiously.

He looked up, startled, then gave a small, hesitant nod. A ghost of a smile touched his lips, then faded just as quickly when I told him the elders had summoned him for a meeting.

He listened quietly, then nodded slowly. Just then, his stomach growled, a loud, rumbling protest that echoed through the hallway.

I sighed, rolling my eyes. “Come on, let’s get you something to eat.”

He started to protest, but I cut him off, steering him down the hallway towards the clan kitchen. I ordered him a bowl of rice porridge and a cup of warm milk. Simple, bland, easy on the stomach.

Tadashi frowned, his gaze flicking nervously towards the main meeting room, its doors visible from our table.

“Sadi, what if they’re waiting—”

“Just eat. And then go splash some water on your face. You look like you just crawled out of a grave. You can’t go in there looking like that.”

That shut him up. He ate quickly, and some color returned to his face. He still looked a little shaken, though.

“Thank you for the meal,” he said softly, giving me a small, grateful smile. As if I had just thrown him a feast. “The food was delicious.”

I shrugged, embarrassed by his gratitude. It was just some porridge. I didn’t even have to pay for it.

He stood, and I fell into step beside him, feeling like a guard escorting a condemned man to his execution.

The meeting room was packed, the elders and clan officials seated in a semicircle, their faces grim, their eyes sharp and unforgiving. If it were me, I’d be sweating bullets. But Tadashi, he just knelt in the center of the room, bowed deeply, and spoke.

“Thank you for allowing me to appear before you,” he said, his voice steady.

I closed the door quietly behind me and took a seat in the back row, my eyes scanning the room. The air was thick with tension.

It was a full house. All the major players were here. Father was seated among the clan officials, a smug smirk playing on his lips. Anko sat beside him, her arms crossed, her gaze fixed on Tadashi. And Shiro sat beside the Clan Leader, her posture perfect, her face betraying nothing.

I clenched my fists, my anxiety spiking with each passing moment. I knew what solitary did to a person. Tadashi might be putting on a brave face, but he was hurting. Inside and out.

The Clan Leader began by summarizing the recent mission to eliminate the drug operation. There had been much debate among the elders and clan officials about our actions. Abandoning a mission was a serious offense, and even though Tadashi had taken the blame, and they had punished him, it was clear they weren’t satisfied. Many felt his actions were unbecoming of a future leader. Some even brought up past mistakes, whispers of doubt and disappointment circulating through the room.

While we were gone, they’d appointed Shiro as acting clan leader. And they liked what they saw. She was decisive, smart, and ambitious. Everything they wanted in a leader. Everything Tadashi wasn’t.

“Therefore,” the Clan Leader said, his voice heavy with finality, “we have decided to permanently relieve you of your duties as successor. Effective immediately, Shiro will assume the role of heir apparent. Sadi will remain as second in command.”

Silence descended upon the room.

Father’s jaw clenched. He wasn’t happy. He’d been so sure this time…

Anko shot the Clan Leader a look that could curdle milk, her fingers twitching as if she longed to reach for a weapon. But this wasn’t the time or the place for a fight. She remained silent.

Shiro’s face was a mask, her expression unreadable. She stared at Tadashi, her gaze intense, almost predatory. There was something in her eyes, something cold and calculating, that I’d never seen before.

“Shiro’s appointment will be formally announced at the five-year evaluation,” the Clan Leader continued. “Until then, you will assist her, guide her, prepare her for her new role. Is that understood?”

Tadashi’s gaze remained locked on his sister’s, a silent conversation passing between them. His expression shifted rapidly—a storm of guilt, fear, and resignation raged across his face.

I couldn’t tear my eyes away. It was like watching a train wreck in slow motion. He was trapped, caught between a rock and a hard place. And there was nothing I could do to help him.

“Tadashi,” the Clan Leader’s voice boomed, cutting through the silence.

Tadashi’s eyes finally broke away from Shiro’s. He looked at the Clan Leader, his face flushed, his jaw clenched, then bowed his head.

“Yes, Clan Leader,” he said, his voice tight. “I understand.”

The meeting adjourned. Shiro didn’t stick around to celebrate her promotion. She practically ran out of the room, Tadashi trailing behind her. I followed, keeping my distance.

“Shiro, please, wait—” Tadashi called.

Shiro ignored him, hurrying down the hallway towards the north wing, her footsteps echoing in the silence. She turned a corner, disappearing into a small, empty room, and pulled Tadashi inside after her, slamming the door shut. I pressed my ear against the door, listening.

“Shiro, I just wanted—”

The sharp crack of a slap echoed through the room, cutting him off mid-sentence. I winced. I knew how strong she was. That had to hurt.

“Do you have any idea what you’ve done?” Shiro hissed, her voice trembling with suppressed fury. “I was sick with worry. Raito kept asking where you were. Father’s been running himself ragged trying to smooth things over with the elders. They thought you were dead. Or worse, that you’d betrayed the clan and gone over to the Mitsurugi.”

“Akito’s been losing his mind, accusing you of getting Sadi killed, demanding Father send out search parties. They searched for days, found nothing, and it just made things worse. Everyone’s on edge.”

“Some of the officials are already jockeying for your position, trying to push their own candidates. It’s chaos, Tadashi. That’s why Father asked me to come back.”

“He wants me to drop the ‘Akarui’ act and come back as Shiro Masamune —the prodigy, the one who scored highest, the heir apparent. They’re all watching me, expecting me to outperform you when you were already damn good at this. Some of them just can’t wait to see me fail, to prove I can’t match up. Do you have any idea what that’s like, Tadashi?”

“Shiro, I—”

“Don’t touch me!” she snapped. There was a moment of silence. When she continued, her voice low and dangerous. “You told me to live my own life, to find something I was passionate about, to make friends. And now you’re taking it all away. You’re killing ‘Akarui’, just like you killed ‘Raito’. Aren’t you satisfied with killing Mother?”

Tadashi gasped, inhaling sharply.

“Shiro, please,” he pleaded, his voice raw with pain. “Don’t say that. There has to be a way—”

A bitter laugh escaped Shiro’s lips.

“It’s too late, Tadashi. There’s no going back. You think you can just waltz back in here and reclaim your position? After all the mistakes you’ve made? You’re weak, Tadashi. Selfish. You only care about yourself, about your own feelings. You’ve never understood what it means to put the clan first.”

“What really happened out there? What are you hiding? Did Sadi do something stupid? And you’re covering for him? You chose him over the clan?”

“I wasn’t protecting him, Shiro. We were both injured. That wolf venom, it hit us hard. Sadi was in bad shape, and I didn’t know if we were still being followed. I told him to wait until I was stronger, so we could face whatever was coming… together.”

I closed my eyes. He made it sound so believable. If I didn’t know better, I might have bought it myself. But Shiro, she wasn’t falling for it.

“You’re forgetting something, Tadashi,” she said, her voice cold as ice. “I’m an empath. I can feel your emotions. And solitary confinement has left you raw, vulnerable. You can’t hide from me. You’re lying.” She let out a harsh laugh. “I don’t care who’s to blame anymore. You’re both cowards. Running away, hiding, making promises you can’t keep. You said you’d protect me from this, from the clan, from everything. And you broke your word.”

Silence stretched between them, heavy and suffocating. Tadashi had no answer.

Maybe she was right. Tadashi and I … maybe we were both cowards, too afraid to face the consequences. But we’d done what we thought was best. We’d tried to protect the clan, even if it meant keeping secrets that could hurt everyone.

“Shiro, I… I’m sorry.”

“Sorry doesn’t fix anything,” Shiro said coldly. “I’m stuck here now. I can’t just walk away. So I’m going to do what I have to do. I’m going to make Father proud. I’m going to be the leader this clan needs. Something you could never be.”

The door flew open, and Shiro stormed out. She whirled around, grabbing my arm, her grip like a vise.

“You breathe a word of this to anyone,” she hissed, her face inches from mine, “and I’ll kill you. Understand?”

I nodded, forcing a smile, my cheeks burning with shame. When I turned back to the room, Tadashi was leaning against the wall, his eyes closed, his body shaking uncontrollably.

I gave him some space, waiting outside the room, my heart pounding.

“Tadashi,” I said softly as he came out, closing the door behind him. He didn’t answer, wouldn’t look at me. His face was pale, his lower lip split and bleeding, a bright red mark blooming on his cheek.

I cleared my throat. “You alright?” I asked, my voice rough.

He shrugged, turning away, his voice a broken whisper.

“I… I need it, Sadi. The drug … I need it now.”

I spun him around, grabbing his shoulders, my fingers digging into his flesh. “Don’t you dare,” I growled, my voice low and dangerous. “Don’t you ever touch that shit again.”

Tadashi’s gaze dropped to the floor, his shoulders slumping. His hands trembled as he gripped his arms. “I know. But you said… you said I have to tell you.” He swallowed hard, his voice barely above a whisper. “Please… stop me. Any way you can.”

I stared at him, my heart pounding, understanding what he was really asking.

Something shifted inside me. A warmth spread through my chest, chasing away the coldness that had lingered for so long. No one had ever protected me from the old man like he did. No one had ever trusted me this way before. This whole messed-up situation had forged a bond between us—something stronger than rivalry, stronger than blood.

We’d both paid a heavy price, but I wouldn’t let it be in vain. I’d just come to realize that he was a true friend, and I didn’t want to let him down.

“You’re a real pain in the ass, you know that?” I said gruffly, falling into step beside him. “But you did good by telling me. Don’t worry—I’ve got you, and you’re gonna be okay.”

After all, I’d made him a promise, and I intended to keep it.

To accompany him.

To protect him.

No matter what.
