Chapter 4

I worked the ropes binding my hands, twisting and pulling until they loosened enough for me to slip free. Sliding my arm into my sleeve, I summoned the storage seal hidden there and pulled out a shuriken. My hands trembled, a mix of adrenaline and exhaustion flooding my limbs, but I worked as carefully and quickly as I could. The blade bit silently into the tough fibers, slicing through the ropes binding Shiro’s hands.

Just as I made progress, the train jolted to a sudden stop, almost throwing me forward. My stomach dropped. I immediately passed the shuriken into Shiro’s hands and scanned the fields beyond the train. We were in the middle of wide, open grasslands, rice paddies stretching into the horizon, dotted with farmhouses and distant outlines of trees.

A lone figure on horseback approached, galloping steadily towards us. He dismounted, tying his horse to one of the trees before climbing aboard our carriage.

Minho straightened instantly, his posture snapping to attention. Around him, the other criminals quickly tilted their heads in a gesture of respect.

“Master Seishiro,” Minho greeted with uncharacteristic politeness. “How are you today?”

The sharp blast of the train’s whistle signaled its movement again as Seishiro boarded. The man in his thirties looked entirely out of place amidst the grime and chaos of this wretched operation. He was dressed in a clean white shirt, a long beige coat draped over his shoulders. His sharp greyish-white hair and unnervingly bright silver spectacles gave him the air of a calculating scholar.

So this is the mastermind of the traffickers.

“I’m fine,” Seishiro said shortly, his voice smooth yet cold. His sharp gaze swept dismissively over the gaggle of thugs before settling on me and Shiro. “What do we have here?”

Minho cleared his throat, almost eager to explain. “We found them near the edge of the Heinna Forest. They look like spoiled brats who ran away from some rich family. I figured we could hit them up for a ransom before anything else.”

Seishiro circled us leisurely, his scrutiny heavy. I glared up at him, forcing myself to maintain eye contact even as I shifted slightly to keep Shiro close behind me.

His steps slowed as he stopped in front of Shiro, crouching beside her. His pale gaze lingered on her for an uncomfortable moment before his expression flickered, lightning-quick—his irises flashing a glaring yellow for but an instant. He raised an eyebrow, a small, humorless smile playing at his lips as if he’d just confirmed something.

Seishiro stood and beckoned to Junji, who handed him a long strip of black cloth without delay.

My heart jumped. “What are you doi—”

The words died in my mouth as Junji’s stick struck me with brutal force, again and again. Pain splintered through my entire being, the world vibrating. Warm blood trickled down the side of my face, blurring my vision.

It doesn’t hurt. It doesn’t hurt. Come on, Tadashi. It doesn’t hurt…

Seishiro ignored my struggle and protest as he carefully tied the cloth around Shiro’s eyes, cutting off her sight.

Then, his attention shifted to me. His brow furrowed as he studied my clothes. His expression turned calculating as his gaze lingered on the crest embroidered faintly on my kimono.

“Oh… hmm… this is quite unexpected,” he murmured, his smirk sharpening. “What’s his name?”

“Taka,” Minho replied, his tone tinged with pride. “He claims to be the son of a clan official. Ran away with his girlfriend, Akarui.”

“Taka from the Masamune clan… how fascinating,” Seishiro mused, almost to himself. “Good work, Minho. I’ll inform headquarters. We’ll interrogate them properly once we arrive.”

Suddenly Seishiro’s hand shot out, dragging me roughly by the arm. I stumbled as he leaned close, his breath icy against my ear.

“Running away like a naive fool during an official clan ceremony. Bringing an Empath into the wild, completely unprepared and half-armed. You’ve got guts, Taka Masamune .” His voice carried a faint undertone of amusement, his words barbed. “My master will enjoy toying with you both. I promise you’ll look back on today with regret for the rest of your life.”

With that, he yanked me down, shoving me forward until my forehead pressed against my knees. Before I could react, he grabbed my right hand and wrenched my thumb backward in one swift, merciless motion.

“AAH!” The scream tore from my lips.

Everything went white—blinding, consuming, drowning. Air caught in my throat, and my world narrowed to the searing agony radiating up my arm. I couldn’t think, couldn’t speak; I could only tremble, my breaths shallow.

Seishiro’s voice broke through faintly, as though from a great distance.

“Have your teacher told you this? The hallmark of disabling a swordsman isn’t to take their weapon—it’s to take their hands.” He said with mocking casualness. “Lucky for you, I’m in a good mood. At least your father won’t have to open a package containing one of your severed hands.”

He paused, then grabbed my left hand and dislocated the thumb with the same methodical cruelty.

It felt no less excruciating. My throat locked around a scream that didn’t come out, choking on ragged gasps of air. The raw, relentless anguish shattered the last remnants of my composure, tears streaming down my cheeks.

With a dismissive push, Seishiro shoved me backward, sending me sprawling behind Shiro once more. Every jolt sent fresh waves of agony up my arms, but I desperately twisted my body away from her, trying to avoid dragging her into my suffering any further. I couldn’t lift my head, couldn’t move except to curl inward, my knees drawn to my chest.

Shiro could feel my pain, but I wouldn’t let her. I squeezed my eyes shut and forced myself to regulate my breathing.

It’s okay.

It doesn’t hurt.

It doesn’t hurt.

Shiro will be okay.

Keep breathing.

Stay conscious.

“That’ll do for now,” Seishiro remarked carelessly. “Keep an eye on them. I’m checking the other carriages.”

“Yes, Sir,” Junji responded sharply.

The moment he and Minho disappeared, silence fell, broken only by the distant sound of rushing water.

The Doori River.

I bit back every ounce of pain and forced my trembling hand to reach for Shiro’s. Tracing faint letters on her palm, I managed to write: ‘Please untie my hands.’

Seconds later, I felt her fingers cutting at the bindings around my wrists, the shuriken she still held slicing through the ropes quickly. She had already managed to free herself.

Once I was unbound, I breathed deeply, adjusting my position to steady myself despite the throbbing pain in my arms.

“Oi!” I called sharply, injecting a note of irritation into my voice. “I need to pee!”

Junji barked a laugh. “Pee your pants, kid!”

The other guards chuckled, but one of them, clearly the least amused, approached me anyway.

The moment he was close enough, I struck.

What followed unraveled in a frantic blur: punches, shouts, bodies piling onto me. My feet were still tied, my dislocated hands useless. Within moments, I could do little but curl up and shield my head from the storm of fists and kicks.

“Hey!” Shiro’s shout tore through the chaos. The weight began lifting, one guard at a time. She had already incapacitated them somehow.

One guard reached for me, but I caught his wrist and smashed my fist into his nose. The sharp crunch of breaking cartilage echoed before he fell unconscious.

Shiro was by my side in an instant, slicing the binds around my feet with a kodachi. She helped me stand, steadying me. She then handed me the blade and recovered her wallet from the nearby table.

Before another wave of guards could rush in, I grabbed her arm and ran toward the open carriage door. The train stretched across the bridge spanning the wide, clear expanse of the Doori River.

I met Shiro’s gaze, nodded, and pulled her into a tight hug.

“Now!”

Together, we jumped.

The icy impact of the water hit like a slap, numbing and bruising. Agony rippled through my battered body as I swam desperately alongside Shiro toward the riverbank.

We clambered ashore, soaked and gasping. There was no time to rest. Without a word, we ran, following the tracks back toward the grasslands where Seishiro had left his horse.

When we were finally far enough to feel safe, we collapsed onto the grass. My hands throbbed mercilessly. Without a word, Shiro knelt beside me.

Her hands were steady as she worked. One by one, she expertly reset the dislocated joints in my thumbs, drawing gritted hisses of pain from me. She applied makeshift splints and wrapped both my hands in torn strips of cloth, her expression unreadable.

Once finished, Shiro looked at me seriously, her voice breaking through the heavy silence.

“Tadashi, we need to get back home,” she urged, her tone firm.

I stared at her, perplexed by the sudden change. “What about meeting the empath teacher? Isn’t that why you left in the first place?”

Shiro looked away. “It’s okay. I just… I want to go home.” When I didn’t respond, she added, “I’ll correspond with the teacher through letters. I’ll practice on my own. There’s no need to run anymore.”

Her words left me stunned. After everything, after all her pain and frustration, she was giving up on her escape?

I frowned, choosing my next words carefully. “But Shiro, you hate it at home… You shouldn’t have to hide behind the name Akarui. Let me talk to Father—”

Shiro cut me off with a bitter laugh. “He won’t listen to you. Do you have any idea what today’s going to cost you? You humiliated him in front of the elders. They all saw what you did. Father won’t forgive this—not easily. He already thinks you’re disappointing the clan.”

Her words stung. She was right, of course. My absences from the evaluation would be interpreted as a blatant failure. Father wasn’t just going to overlook that.

I took a shuddering breath.

I’m so sorry, Father.

Shiro lowered her gaze, her own expression pained yet determined. “Don’t worry about my problems,” she muttered. “I’ll figure out what to do with my life as Akarui after mastering my empath abilities. I don’t need you dragging yourself down for my sake.”

I sat there in silence, watching her face. It hurt to see her like this—lost in the identity Father had created to protect her. But it’d been years since Mother died. Why did Shiro still have to be someone else? Why did she have to be trapped like that?

I gazed toward the open field, the tall grasses swaying gently in the breeze, and for a flicker of time, I wondered… What if things didn’t have to be this way for her? What if we could change everything?

She didn’t need to be Akarui anymore. She didn’t need to struggle alone. Shiro, my real Shiro, could be the shining star of this clan. She always had that potential. Maybe it was time for her to reclaim her rightful place—the place that had always belonged to her.

I lowered my head, absently plucking a clump of grass.

“Shiro,” I began softly, my voice barely above a whisper, “if you want, you could just return as yourself … and take my place. You could replace me. Father and the elders—everyone would welcome you back with open arms. They’d accept you as the rightful heir.”

She didn’t answer immediately. When she finally spoke, her voice carried an edge.

“You… you want me to become the next clan heir?”

I froze, remembering Mother’s wish and my own promise. Guilt and shame tightening my throat.

“No, I didn’t mean it like that,” I breathed out quickly. “You don’t have to. I don’t want you to become anything you don’t want to be, Shiro. I just…” My voice faltered. “I just don’t want you to suffer. That’s all.”

An ache grew in my chest.

For her, I’d take on the burden of the clan heir, no matter how heavy it was. I’d also give up my status and step aside, if that was what she wanted.

I’d do anything for my sister.

For a long moment, Shiro said nothing. Then, to my shock, she lunged forward and wrapped me in a tight embrace. Her body trembled against mine, her hands clenched tightly into the fabric of my clothing.

“I don’t want the title, Tadashi,” she murmured, broken and raw. “I don’t want to belong to the clan. I just want to be free. I’m sorry….”

Her words cut through me, but I returned her embrace, holding her close. With a shaky breath, I lowered my head, pressing a gentle kiss to her forehead.

“It’s okay, Shiro. I understand. I’ll protect you from the clan,” I promised. It would be a tough promise to keep, but as long as my sister was safe, then nothing else mattered. “I love you. I’m always here for you.”

We mounted Seishiro’s horse and went back home.

The journey back was a blur—a mangrove forest, a dimly lit tunnel carved into a mountain, a border checkpoint, and finally, the towering gates of our clan compound under the veil of midnight.

As we passed beneath those gates, a tight ache gripped my chest. My thoughts snagged on the clan’s antique sword, the one I ‘d left behind.

Calm down. There’s a tracking seal on it, I reassured myself. Elder Mei could send Sadi to retrieve it later.

We stopped beneath the sprawling branches of an ancient tree near the park, its shadow swallowing us whole. I turned to my sister, my voice firmer than I felt.

“You should go home. You don’t need to come with me to the main hall.”

She hesitated. “What are you going to tell them?”

I shrugged. “The truth—well, most of it. I’ll say I returned to retrieve something I left behind and stumbled upon traffickers trying to drug and abduct Akarui. You didn’t know I was following you. We only met on the train and managed to escape together. It’ll align with what actually happened. Don’t worry, Shiro. Just corroborate the story when they question you.”

Her lips pressed into a tight line. But after a moment of deliberation, she nodded. Without another word, she turned and headed home.

I stood motionless under the tree, watching her retreat until she reached the intersection and disappeared.

With her gone, I finally allowed myself to take a breath.

The world seemed to tilt. My legs buckled as the full weight of exhaustion, hunger, and pain slammed into me. Leaning heavily against the tree trunk, I covered my burning eyes with my hands, struggling to steady myself.

Everything came crashing down.

Father’s anger. The scorn of the elders. The punishments waiting for me.

My head spun.

“Mother…” I whispered.

I’m scared.

I squeezed my eyes shut.

Come on, Tadashi. Don’t crumble now.

I don’t know if I can bear their wrath.

Your task isn’t over yet.

What if I’m not strong enough?

Get up. Don’t let them wait any longer.

Help me… please.

Don’t make them angrier. They’ve been waiting for you since morning.

Please, lend me some strength….

Stand up. Now!

I shuddered as I pushed myself upright, my breath shallow. I swallowed hard and fixed my gaze on the imposing silhouette of the main hall.

It’s okay.

I’m still alive.

Shiro is safe. That’s what matters.

I can do this…

I repeated the mantra as I forced my battered body forward. With unsteady steps, I made my way toward the main hall, where the storm was waiting.

I never imagined that when I’d next see my home, it would be after such a long time.

The elders didn’t strip me of my title as clan heir outright. No, that would’ve been too simple, too merciful. Instead, they devised something crueler. They made it clear where my loyalties should lie: the clan first, always . My family second—if at all.

After the main hall hearing that stretched until dawn, the verdict was cold and final: physical punishment, confinement in the isolation room, exile from home, and an atonement mission.

The punishment began immediately. I was thrown into the confinement room—a cold, dark cube that seemed to swallow all sound, all light, all time. They wanted me to think and rethink my betrayal .

Once released, the exile began. I would leave my family—not for a time, but indefinitely. I was forbidden from entering the house, speaking to my siblings, or even catching a fleeting glimpse of their faces. I wasn’t allowed near them, even in their most vulnerable moments.

And my atonement… was a mission unlike any I had ever been assigned.

Stripped of my name, rank, and identity, I was sent to serve every household in the clan. Disguised and under a vow of silence, I was to wander from door to door, carrying a letter bearing the clan leader’s seal—Father’s seal.

My orders were crystal clear: obey without hesitation . Clean their floors. Tend their fields. Watch their children. Stock their shops. Whatever anyone asked of me, I was to do it without a word of protest or refusal.

It was hell.

Every day brought a new task: humbling labor disguised as duty. I served in silence, bending to the whims of families who rarely extended even the basic kindness of food or shelter. Some nights I slept outside, lucky if I was thrown a blanket or some scraps.

Some families treated me indifferently, barely acknowledging my presence. Others took advantage of my punishment and silence, giving me grueling, meaningless jobs—or worse, for their amusement. Sadi and his father were particularly unrelenting. They summoned me time and again, assigning menial or grueling tasks just to see me bend to their will. Something about the smug way Sadi loomed over me in those moments felt more punishing than the actual work.

But, surprisingly, none of that was the hardest part.

The harsh and degrading treatment, the aching muscles, the constant hunger, the endless work—the servitude … they didn’t break me.

The silence did.

All this time, my siblings had been the brightest part of my day. Knowing they were safe gave me strength. But the clan severed that. I had no one to turn to.

I lost hours of sleep worrying about Shiro. Was she all right? Was Father angry with her? Was she punished, too? Could she continue her Empath training? I told myself that maybe with me gone, she wouldn’t be burdened with my emotions. That she might feel a little more at ease. Something about that thought made my chest hurt a little less.

And Raito—gods, Raito. I’d promised him a camping trip. But instead, I vanished. No explanation, no goodbyes. What did he think of me now? The memory of his accident haunted me. I had done nothing when he was hospitalized—not because I didn’t care, but because I wasn’t allowed to. I couldn’t hold his hand and comfort him.

I wasn’t there when he needed me most. Would he ever forgive me?

But none of it compared to Father.

Always, it came back to Father.

The memories of him from that night clawed at my mind.

During the hearing, Father had been calm. Too calm. He listened to my explanation without interruption. He didn’t yell, didn’t strike me, didn’t raise his voice even once. He didn’t add to my punishment or express his fury for the elders to see. It made my skin crawl in a way shouting never could.

When the tribunal concluded, the elders dispersed, and the decision was final… Father didn’t even look at me until we were alone, sitting face-to-face across the tatami mat. He was quiet for so long I thought I might suffocate.

Then, in a low, quivering voice, he said the words I feared most:

“I’m disappointed in you, Tadashi. Very disappointed.”

It stung more deeply than any flogging ever could.

All thoughts fled my mind. I couldn’t summon a single syllable. My throat was dry, my chest too tight to force out any words. I could only sit there, staring down at the mat beneath my knees as his disappointment pressed on me.

“Think about what you’ve done,” Father said, his voice trembling slightly. “Think about whether you’d do the same if given another chance. Do you understand the consequences of your choices?”

A hundred answers warred in my head. I wanted to tell him that I had no regrets about protecting Shiro. I wanted to tell him how I tried so hard to honor Mother’s memory, to uphold her final wish to protect our family.

But no words came. No defense. No argument.

No excuses.

I just sat there, my guilt and shame swimming in the pool of his disappointment. My throat felt tight, my vision blurring with tears as I lowered my head, unable to meet his eyes.

“This family has made you weak,” he said plainly, his tone steady again, as if he’d come to a quiet conclusion. “I’ve failed to make you independent, and you still depend too much on your siblings. That’s why this punishment must go beyond what the elders have given.”

I lifted my teary gaze slightly, confused.

“Pardon?” I croaked.

Father’s chilling words followed:

“When your eighteenth birthday comes, you will leave our house permanently.You will live your own life, away from this family, until you prove your independence. We are not holding a place for you anymore, Tadashi.”

For a long, aching moment, I was speechless. My mind was blank.

Did I hear him right? Was he kicking me out?

“I will not help you,” he finished. “You are on your own.”

The air shuttered.

I wanted to tell him how sorry I was. I would promise him I’d fix things. But no words came—not as I sat there, trembling, tears streaking my face.

I prostrated myself on the ground, bowing deep, pressing my forehead against the tatami mat.

“Father,” I whispered hoarsely, choking. “ Please … Please forgive me.”

For five agonizingly long minutes, he said nothing. He didn’t move, didn’t make a noise.

Finally, I heard the soft scrape of his steps as he rose. The door slid open, and seconds later—it slid shut again.

He left me there, bowing and struggling just to breathe. Just walked away without acknowledgment, as if I wasn’t his son anymore.

Eighteen months.

Eighteen months of Father’s silence stretching between us like a frozen river. He never let his voice thaw. Even when I knelt in front of him to submit reports, he never lifted his gaze from the scrolls.

Father never looked at me again. Not really.

He’d stare at the wall behind my shoulder, his eyes glazed. My greetings dissolved in the space between us, unanswered.

The pain didn’t burst. It crystallized—a jagged, permanent thing wedged beneath my ribs. Some nights, I’d press my palm to my sternum, half-expecting to feel his disappointment splintering through my bone.

I’d hear his voice sometimes: Disappointed, Tadashi. Very disappointed.

It echoed in empty rooms. In market streets. In the rasp of my own breath.

And when I finally dared to imagine his face… all I could see was the back of his head, turned away forever.

I used to believe the hardest thing in the world was enduring Father’s disappointment. But I was wrong. This was harder. This was worse.

Yet in the stillness between missions, I remembered—his hands guiding mine over a practice sword, his voice firm but steady: A true leader protects what matters, Tadashi. Even when it’s hard.

So I bowed lower, fought harder, endured longer.

If proving my worth meant silence, I would bite my tongue.

If it meant obedience, I would kneel.

If it meant waiting…

I’d press my forehead to the floor until the tatami lines imprinted on my skin.

Because one day, perhaps, somewhere in the space between my father’s silence and my own resolve, the frozen river would thin—just enough to imagine spring.
