I thought the hardest thing in the world was enduring Father’s disappointment.

My head felt like it was going to burst.

It had been a brutal day, one that started before sunrise. The ache in my head pulsed in time with the gash on my upper arm—a memento from last night’s mission, throbbing like a second heartbeat. I’d barely managed two hours of fevered sleep, nowhere near enough to recover. But rest was a luxury beyond my reach. As long as I managed to stay upright and functional, there’d be no relief in my schedule.

Father dragged me out for morning drills before rushing me to the academy, the taste of blood still sharp in my tongue. Classes blurred into afternoon training, followed by duties at the clan’s main hall. By the time the sun dipped below the horizon, I was bone-tired, but the day wasn’t over. Dragging myself home, I clung to the faint hope of collapsing onto my futon. But I knew better. A mountain of textbooks and half-finished assignments waited, a looming reminder of tomorrow’s quiz.

It’s okay. One step at a time. Just breathe.

“I’m home,” I rasped, slipping off my sandals and placing them neatly in the genkan .

In the kitchen, Shiro stood behind the counter, her gaze fixed on a piece of paper. As I entered, she quickly folded it and slipped it into a thick book.

“Welcome back,” she said, her tone curt.

I hesitated. Was that sharpness in her voice, or was it my imagination? Had I done something to upset her? She turned back to the kitchen table, ignoring me. I bowed my head and mumbled an apology. Maybe she was irritated because my lateness had delayed dinner.

“Food!” Raito’s voice erupted from the living room.

An energetic blur shot past as he tossed his homework onto the dining table and darted into the kitchen, eyes wide with curiosity as he peered into the pot on the stove.

Shiro scowled and rapped him on the head with a rice ladle. “Sit down. I’ll serve dinner,” she said firmly.

Pushing aside my fatigue, I slipped to the hallway sink and splashed cold water to my face, trying to jolt myself awake and scrub away the weight of the day. When I sat down opposite Raito, I offered him a small smile.

“How was your day?”

“Great!” His eyes lit up. “We learned how to give first aid to unconscious people! Like heroes!”

I chuckled, warmth stirring deep inside me. He launched into another topic, reminding me of the upcoming camping trip. His words tumbled out in an eager rush, his voice rising with insistence.

“It’s super-duper special, so you have to bring someone special too! You’ll be the worst big brother in the world if you don’t come!!”

Mustering a faint smile, I promised him I’d be there—once I finished my duties at the main hall.

Soon, the comforting aroma of fish soup wafted through the dining area.

“Do we need to wait for Father to get home?” Raito asked hesitantly, his stomach rumbling loudly in protest.

Our father’s schedule was unpredictable. Some nights, he came home early for dinner; other times, he didn’t make it at all. I didn’t want my siblings to go hungry, so I nudged Raito and Shiro toward their bowls.

“Eat. I’ll hold the table.”

I sipped bitter green tea instead, trying to ignore the hollow ache in my stomach. I hadn’t eaten since dawn—skipping breakfast for Father’s drills and forgetting the bento Shiro had packed for lunch. But I couldn’t bring myself to eat before him. Someone had to be there to greet him when he returned, to share the meal. Leaving him to eat alone felt… disrespectful.

Father arrived just as Shiro and Raito were finishing. I rose immediately and greeted him with a deep bow.

“Welcome home, Father,” I said, taking his jacket and work bag. “My apologies for starting dinner in your absence.”

Shiro plated his meal while Raito launched into an animated account of his day. Normally, Father would listen with a small smile, but tonight, his face was grim. A pang of unease slithered down my spine.

Something bad had happened in the clan.

My gut clenched. Something involving me.

My appetite vanished. Years of reading the storm in his silence told me to brace myself. This would end in his study, with me kneeling on tatami as he dissected my failures. But tonight, he broke the pattern.

He waited until Raito’s footsteps faded upstairs.

“Akito claims Sadi reported your injury.” His chopsticks clicked against the bowl—a swordsman testing his blade. “That you hindered the team and compromised the mission.” Father’s gaze flicked to my bandaged arm. “Is this true?”

The accusation struck like a shuriken. My mind went white in static.

“I… didn’t—” I stammered, unable to finish.

How could I explain without sounding pathetic? Yes, I’d taken a knife meant for Sadi’s flank. No, I hadn’t faltered until the extraction point. The bloodied part of the bandages hidden beneath my sleeve itched in betrayal.

Explain, a part of me begged. Tell him you patched the gash yourself, that you bled silently.

I dug my nails into my palms.

Be quiet and take the hit. Excuses are for the weak.

“I thought you were capable of handling difficulties in missions and knew how to conduct yourself.” Father’s voice bore down on me, each word heavier than the last. “Do I need to remind you—again—that the clan heir evaluation is in just a few days?”

My neck burned in submission. As if I could forget. Elder Mei had drilled it into me repeatedly. This month, the scrutiny had been relentless. Every move critiqued, every mistake punished—often with lashes or canes. I had barely kept up with the elders’ endless demands.

“Listen, Tadashi,” Father continued, his voice firm. “You only need to maintain your accomplishments. Always strive for excellence. For the sake of our clan, do not let Sadi take that position from you. Do you understand?”

My throat tightened. No matter how hard I tried, the elders only saw my shortcomings. And Sadi? He was his father, Akito’s, shadow—polished, cunning, sharpened like a katana. His only flaw was laughing too loud, too free, in halls that demanded whispers. The elders and Father despised his brashness. But to Akito’s supporters, it was hardly a flaw.

I didn’t want to make promises I couldn’t keep. But there was nothing else to say.

I forced myself to nod. “Yes, Father. I understand.”

Father finished his meal in silence. When he rose, he didn’t call me to his study.

A small mercy.

I bowed as he left the room and remained rooted in place, lost in thought.

“You’re welcome. Enjoy your rest,” Shiro quipped dryly from the far end of the table, jolting me from my reverie. I’d almost forgotten she was still there.

“Ah. S-Sorry, Shiro,” I croaked. “Thank you for the meal. It was delicious.”

Shiro snorted.

“You didn’t even eat. Save the lies for Father.” She swept up the bowls, porcelain clattering like thrown dice.

Heat scorched my cheeks. The praise was automatic, but I never meant to lie. Shiro had a talent for cooking; her meals always reminded me of Mother’s. Did she know how much I truly appreciated her? I often ate slowly, savoring it to preserve Mother’s memory just a little longer.

Not wanting to hurt her more, I swallowed the guilt and quietly helped her with the dishes. Lately, Shiro had felt… distant. She seemed more irritable than usual, though I couldn’t figure out why.

Did she feel neglected?

I bit my lip. In my free moments, I spent more time with Raito. But Shiro did so much for the household, taking on responsibilities no one else noticed.

Had I forgotten something important to her? Her birthday? No, I’d remembered this year, and she’d liked the gift. Was something bothering her? Did she need something? New clothes? Makeup? Perhaps there was a book she wanted? Or maybe an issue with her friends? Maybe I should ask Anko—she and Shiro occasionally chatted at the clan library.

I used to read alongside Shiro, sharing newspapers or magazines and laughing over silly articles. But now… Now I couldn’t even recall the last time we simply talked about her interests.

What kind of brother have I become? I clenched my fists. How could I be so blind to my sister’s needs?

When we finished cleaning, Shiro hurried upstairs to her room without a word. I followed, my concern growing with each step.

“Shiro,” I called softly as we reached her door. “Are you alright? You look pale.”

She turned to me sharply, her expression flickering between frustration and something else—pain, perhaps. A hint of tears threatened in her eyes, and for a moment, I couldn’t tell whether she was about to break down or lash out.

Instead, Shiro shook her head and forced a tight smile.

“I’m fine . Good night, Tadashi,” she said briskly, slamming her door shut before I could reply.

I stood frozen on the landing, staring at the unyielding wood, the sound echoing in the sudden silence. A knot tightened in my chest.

Calm down. It’s okay. Breathe.

I stumbled to my own room, closing the door behind me with a soft click. Hunger and the throbbing in my arm gnawed at me, but they were muted. My mind was elsewhere, consumed by a single, urgent thought.

Something is going on with my sister.

I have to find out what it is.

The more I observed my sister, the more she seemed to slip away, like sand through my fingers. Every time I tried to ask if something was wrong, she either ignored me or brushed me off. Even Anko couldn’t offer any insight, but I knew, deep down, that something was seriously wrong. I just… didn’t know how to make her open up.

Meanwhile, the clan’s demands consumed what little energy I had left. Night after night, the elders summoned me to meetings that stretched on for hours, each one a grueling test of endurance. They seemed to find fault with my every move, their harsh words a constant, stinging reminder of my perceived inadequacies. And Father—well, Father rarely missed the chance to call me into his office afterward, his tone heavy with advice. The kind of advice that sounded suspiciously close to veiled reprimands.

I… I couldn’t breathe.

By the final day before the successor evaluation, I was running on fumes. That afternoon, preparing for the final meeting felt like preparing for my own execution. Tomorrow, judgment would be passed.

I hadn’t managed a full night’s sleep in two days. My body was a wreck, a collection of aching joints and frayed nerves, protesting each movement. No position in bed brought comfort, only a deeper awareness of the throbbing pain. My mind refused to rest, locked in an endless loop of worst-case scenarios. Every potential misstep, every imagined disappointment in Father’s eyes, every condemning look from the elders—these fears crushed the air from my lungs.

Failure wasn’t an option. I had to keep the title of successor. Not for my own sake, but for Shiro’s.

I thought about Mother for the hundredth time that week. Her face hovered in my memory, her voice gentle yet firm. She had envisioned a different life for Shiro and had taken the risks to change her daughter’s path.

Shiro had scored the highest on the successor selection test and was the rightful candidate to inherit the role, but she rejected the position. Sadi, who scored second, should’ve been next, but Mother had recommended me to the elders as Shiro’s replacement—a bold decision that Father endorsed, because he didn’t want Sadi to take the place. He proposed a vote and managed to secure the elders’ support for me, who ranked third.

So they chose me as the clan heir.

I didn’t want the position, but everyone had placed their faith in me. I wish not to disappoint them, especially my parents.

Mother’s decision couldn’t have been easy, but she must have believed I could bear this burden.

…So I had to be strong. For Shiro.

I’d promised myself she wouldn’t have to endure this life.

I had to carry this weight.

It was tough, but I must keep going. Just a little longer, right? Once the evaluation was over, I could finally rest. Everything… everything would be alright.

On my way to the main hall, I passed through the kitchen to grab a drink. That was when I saw her.

Shiro was at the stove, cooking dinner—but her shoulders shook with quiet sobs. Tears streamed down her face, spilling silently as she stirred the food.

I stopped dead in my tracks.

My stomach churned, twisting.

“Shiro,” I whispered, stepping closer. “What’s wrong?”

She gasped and immediately tried to compose herself, wiping at her tears with the back of her hand. I reached out to touch her arm, hoping to comfort her, but she shoved me away with a force that stunned me. For a moment, I just stood there, staring, unsure of what to say or do.

“You know, Shiro…” I began softly, struggling to keep my voice steady. “You can tell me anything. I’ll listen. Always.”

A bitter laugh escaped her lips, sharp and jagged.

“It’s not important,” she snapped, her words cutting like shards of glass. “Besides, you don’t have time. Don’t you have somewhere to be?”

The words stung, but I pushed past the hurt. “I-I’ll make time tomorrow, I promise,” I said earnestly. “After the evaluation, I’ll come straight home. I’ll spend the whole day with you. We can—”

BANG!

The sound of metal on metal reverberated through the room as Shiro slammed down the spatula, flipping the fish she was cooking so forcefully that the pan rattled. Without missing a beat, she stabbed the fish with the spatula in a jerking, almost violent motion.

I swallowed, my heart pounding.

“There’s nothing you need to worry about,” she barked, her back still turned to me. “Just go. They’re waiting for you.”

I remained rooted to the spot, paralyzed. I didn’t want to leave her like this… but I couldn’t abandon my duties either. For a painful moment, I just stood frozen in the kitchen, behind her. She must have sensed my hesitation because she let out an impatient huff.

I bit my lip.

“Shiro,” I whispered, my voice trembling. “Are you… mad at me?”

She shook her head without turning around.

“Did I do something to upset you?”

This time, she snorted.

“I just don’t want to talk to you right now.”

Her words hit harder than any blow I’d ever taken during training.

I fell silent. For the first time in years, I felt utterly helpless with her. What had I done to push her away like this?

“Shiro,” I whispered quietly, “I’m really… I’m really sorry.”

But Shiro didn’t respond. Eventually, I had no choice but to leave her there, alone.

I should have known something wasn’t right.

The morning of the evaluation arrived like a gut punch. My empty stomach convulsed as I hunched over in the bathroom and retched violently until bile burned my throat. My ribs ached as I wheezed. Cold sweat slicked my palms, and the room spun. Every muscle screamed, not from exertion, but from dread.

I staggered back to my room, lightheaded and weak, each step an effort.

When I gathered myself enough to tell Shiro I was leaving, her gaze lingered on me, unusually intense. She studied me quietly, her expression unreadable, yet heavy with unspoken emotion. Then, without a word, she led me to the small hearth in the kitchen and knelt by the tea set.

It was a ceremonial gesture, a ritual reserved for those heading off on an important mission. The air between us thickened, becoming hushed and reverent. The quiet scrape of the whisk against the bowl, the gentle splash of pouring tea—each sound was deliberate, grounding me.

I didn’t question the herbs she’d used. The first sip was earthy, a subtle bitterness giving way to a soothing warmth that spread through me. My racing heart began to slow. My breath steadied.

For a fleeting moment, I felt anchored. It was as if nothing else existed but this quiet moment, shared with her. Her presence was a sudden, sharp reminder of Mother…

I inhaled deeply, tears stinging my eyes.

As always, I felt unworthy of her kindness, her unwavering support. What could I possibly offer in return? I did the only thing I could: I took her hand, holding it firmly, and met her gaze.

Up close, I noticed the shadows beneath her eyes, the paleness of her skin. She must have been worried about me. All this time, I had wanted to ease her burdens, to see her smile… and here she was, grounding me in my moment of vulnerability, despite her own hidden struggles.

She held my gaze, deep and knowing. Then, her face softened—as if she understood the unspoken words trapped in my throat.

“You’ve worked so hard, Tadashi,” she said, her voice a low murmur. There was warmth in her tone, but also sadness, a weight I couldn’t decipher. She gave my hand a gentle squeeze. “You’ll pass the evaluation. You’ll be a great leader for the clan. I know it.”

Her faith in me was a knot in my throat.

“Thank you, Shiro,” I murmured. The words were not enough… nowhere near enough, but they were all I could manage.

She stood with me at the entrance of the house, seeing me off. A strange, almost wistful softness softened her eyes as she smiled.

I should have realized then.

Something was deeply wrong.

If I hadn’t forgotten something and returned to my room, I would never have discovered Shiro’s plan.

That morning, my sister had decided to run away from home.

Silence.

The kitchen was empty. The dining room and living area were eerily still. A prickle of unease ran down my spine. Something wasn’t right . Why was it so quiet?

I quickened my pace, taking the stairs two at a time to retrieve the heirloom sword from my room. The tantō —a family treasure Father himself had handed down—had been passed through generations of our clan leaders. More than just a weapon, it was a symbol of the clan’s blessing, a tangible representation of their trust. Wielding it was an honor, one I had to carry to today’s evaluation. If I passed, they would return it to me, the official mark of my continuation as heir.

I sighed, shaking my head at my carelessness. How could I have forgotten something so vital? At least I hadn’t gone far; I’d only reached the halfway point to the main hall before realizing it was missing.

With the sword now secure, I turned to leave, but a nagging feeling stopped me, an itch at the back of my mind.

The silence.

Raito would have left for the academy, but where was Shiro?

I turned on my heel and headed to her room, deciding to say goodbye again.

“Shiro?” I called, knocking softly on her door. Silence. No answer, even after a second and third knock.

A knot of apprehension tightened in my stomach. I eased the door open.

Her room was immaculate, as always. The polar opposite of Raito’s space—which usually resembled the aftermath of a natural disaster. Not a single item was out of place. No messy clothes, no scattered books. Shiro’s meticulous neatness had always reflected her personality—controlled, deliberate.

It was precisely because of this order that the single piece of white paper on her desk stood out. I stepped closer, my chest constricting. Then, I saw the small, delicate handwriting.

Tadashi,

By the time you read this, I’ll be long gone. I’m sorry I had to sneak away, especially on such an important day for you. But I know you’ll excel in the evaluation and be chosen as our clan’s next leader—congratulations, in advance. I wish I could be there to witness it, to give you the hug you deserve. No one else truly knows the sacrifices you’ve made to reach this moment, but I do. I’m so proud of you.

But I can’t stay.

Don’t try to find me.

Don’t send anyone to find me.

Don’t help anyone search for me.

Shiro Masamune is now a ghost—a name that will fade into whispers, a figure who moved away as a child to live with her mother’s distant relatives, untraceable and forgotten. Let it stay that way.

Akarui Masamune? She’s nobody. A quiet maid. A shadow in the room. A girl whose classmates barely noticed her absence. Akarui got the highest scores in her sewing classes, surpassed her teacher in cooking, crafted more goods than anyone else in less time. But no one truly saw her, knew her, or cared where she was going.

I don’t want to be Akarui anymore. And while I cherished being Shiro and the freedom to explore my skills, I can’t live under the shadow of the clan’s expectations. I’ve seen the burden they’ve placed on you, the crushing weight of responsibility, and the thought of carrying that myself… it suffocates me.

I can’t be Shiro. I won’t be Akarui.

I need to write my own story, one not dictated by Father or the clan. Please understand.

You don’t need me. I’ve made sure of that. You can handle the house, do your laundry, eat out if you must. Raito is old enough to take care of himself. And Father? He’s indifferent. My absence won’t matter to him. As long as Raito is there, everything will be fine.

Goodbye, Tadashi.

Sending all my love, from afar,

Shiro.

The letter slipped from my trembling fingers.

My mind blanked.

The only thought echoing through my entire being was: Find Shiro, now!

How could I not worry about her? She was my sister. Yes, she’d scold me for calling her fragile—her combat skills were leagues ahead of mine. But physical strength wasn’t everything, not when the world was filled with deceit and danger.

I crushed the letter in my hand, panic surging. She couldn’t have gone far. It had only been about thirty minutes since I’d left home.

But where would she go?

If she was heading out of town, she’d need a horse—that much was certain. But even if she got one, which direction?

Frantically, I scanned the room for clues. Anything that could tell me where she might have gone.

I yanked open her closet. Still full. She’d only taken a few clothes and a blanket. That ruled out a colder region.

Her friends? No, she’d said herself she wasn’t close to any of them. Wait… Anko . Yesterday, she’d mentioned Shiro borrowing a lot of books recently—research, she’d said.

I turned to Shiro’s bookshelves, which filled two entire walls. Her personal collection was arranged alphabetically, with meticulous precision. But the library books—she always kept them separate. Where would she put her most recent loans?

The middle shelf. And there they were. Books on psychology. Mental abilities. Hypnosis.

I frowned, furrowing my brow as I read the titles:

How to Unlock Hypnosis

Mastering the Power of Your Mind

Limitless Potential

Influencing the Subconscious of Others

The Invisible Force

The Touch of Mind Control

…and many more.

“What are these?” I murmured, flipping through the pages.

Shiro was different—special. I’d known that for years. She had an uncanny intuition, a way of understanding situations on a level that defied explanation. It wasn’t fortune-telling. It was more like… she comprehended everything from the inside out, allowing her to make eerily accurate predictions.

Did she actually have the power to read minds? Communicate without speaking? Influence others’ actions?

Whatever it was, she was clearly determined to enhance these abilities. She’d been studying. Preparing. It seemed she’d been searching for someone to guide her—and now, it looked like she’d found them.

But who? And where?

My gaze flicked back to the bookcase, landing on a thick, familiar volume: Emotional Understanding: A Guide. I’d seen her reading this very book in the kitchen—the same day I’d noticed her hiding a piece of paper.

Curious, I pulled it down and opened it to the bookmarked page. And there it was: a torn piece of an envelope, specifically the back. It looked blank, but… I held it up to the sunlight streaming through the window, hoping to catch any faint indentations.

—sighur.

—kina.

The first word… a name? And the second—likely a location. “Kina…” What could that mean? Ujakina Valley to the south of the Kawa Kingdom? Or Arkina City on the Fizto border?

It wasn’t enough. I needed more. My best bet was the stables. If Shiro had taken a horse, the stable master might have seen her, might know her destination.

Clutching the heirloom sword, I bolted out of the house, sprinting towards the stables.

Shiro hadn’t taken a horse from the clan stables.

I confirmed it by checking the stable guard’s ledger. Every clan member was required to fill out a form before borrowing a horse. Shiro’s name wasn’t there, which left only two possibilities: She could’ve rented a horse from the market, or she’d procured one from a ranch. Market horses were fast to acquire—pay some coins and ride away, no questions asked. But they were often poorly trained and not strong enough for rough terrain.

No, for the kind of journey she seemed to be on, she would’ve needed something better. A horse built for endurance. Which meant only one place—Arai Valley.

I urged my own horse toward the valley with a tightening in my chest. She couldn’t have gotten far. If I hurried, maybe I could catch up to her. Maybe I could convince her to come back, to come home.

But even as the hope flickered, doubt crept in.

Would she listen? Would she even care?

When I reached the Arai Valley ranch, an older man lingered near the stables, his hands calloused and weathered.

“No Shiro or Akarui Masamune rented a horse here today,” he said, shaking his head.

“But someone did take a horse this morning, right?” I pressed forward, a thread of desperation pulling at each word. Shiro must’ve been here. She had to have been here.

The old man squinted, considering, and then gave a slow shake of his head. “Only the carpenter’s son came early this morning. He sometimes picks up orders for other folks.”

It clicked. Of course. Shiro wouldn’t have come here herself—not if she was trying to keep her movements hidden. She’d used someone else. Paid them to handle it for her.

The old man, kind enough not to ask further questions, gave me directions to the carpenter’s son’s house. It wasn’t far from the ranch, close to the market district. I urged my horse faster, clinging to the fleeting hope that I was still on the right trail.

The teenager who answered the door had a mop of wild, curly hair and sharp eyes that swept over me with faint amusement. He was older than me, likely around Sadi’s age. He leaned casually against the doorframe, his smirk widening.

“Whoever my customer was, that’s none of your business,” he said, his voice dripping with mockery. “Unless, of course… you’re willing to pay.”

For a moment, I only stared at him, silently cursing my own idiocy. The clan successor evaluation was no ordinary event, and I’d dressed the part. I was still dressed in my finest formal clan kimono, its delicate embroidery screaming status and wealth. But I hadn’t brought any actual money. Not a coin. Not a means to barter.

The thought settled over me like a chilling weight. The only thing of value I had on me was the clan’s heirloom sword—the sacred blade resting at my side. For generations, it had been a relic of our family’s legacy, passed down to each generation as a symbol of leadership and responsibility. Its worth couldn’t be measured, not in money and certainly not in sentimental value.

For a fleeting, horrifying moment, the thought crossed my mind: Could I trade it? Could I give the sword away… for her?

The very idea made my stomach churn. It would stain my name forever. The clan’s sacred heirloom—gone, lost because of me. Father would never forgive me. The clan’d have every right to strip me of my title. It’d be a betrayal so huge, I’d surely be disowned.

Tortured for three days and three nights, then disowned.

…And yet, a smaller voice whispered.

Shiro’s safety is worth any price.

I would give anything, if it means Shiro is safe.

The thought lingered, quiet but unrelenting, as my fingers unconsciously tightened around the hilt of the blade.

“How about it?” Curly drawled, leaning lazily against the doorframe. His smirk was maddening. “There was a girl this morning. Maybe I know where she’s headed. Maybe I don’t. You want the details? You’ll have to pay.”

I clenched my fists, willing myself to stay calm. “I don’t have money,” I said through gritted teeth. “But I can offer you weapons—kodachi, shuriken, kunai. The finest quality.”

Curly let out a short, mocking laugh. “Do I look stupid? That junk’s worth nothing to me.” His sharpened gaze flicked down to the sword at my side—the heirloom. “But that , on the other hand… now that’s something special. I’ll take it.”

I stepped back instinctively, gripping the hilt tighter. “Absolutely not,” I replied sharply.

“Then we’ve got nothing to talk about.” He shrugged, spinning on his heel to leave. “Good luck asking around. Go ahead, waste more time. But I’ll tell you this—no one else knows what I know. You’ve got one shot, and it’s me.”

My heart thundered. There was no time for this. Shiro was out there, alone, heading toward… where? My grip on Curly’s arm stopped him mid-stride.

“Wait!”

The teen raised an eyebrow, clearly enjoying the desperation he saw in my face. “Change your mind?”

I exhaled slowly, trying to steady my voice. “Fine,” I hissed. “I will pawn it. But just as collateral. I’ll redeem it tomorrow. Don’t even think about selling it.”

His grin widened. “Sure, sure,” he said. “Collateral. Ten gold coins, twenty-four hours. If you don’t show up, the sword’s mine.”

Ten gold coins. Twenty-four hours. It was laughable—impossible. The savings Father had carefully put away for me since I was named heir wouldn’t come close. But he saw the ceremonial attire I was wearing and assumed I came from wealth. I couldn’t let him take advantage of me like that.

“Five gold coins,” I countered, narrowing my eyes.

Curly’s smirk vanished. He studied me. “Five gold coins,” he finally conceded with an exaggerated sigh, “but only twelve hours. A second longer, and I send it straight to the black market. Trust me, you’ll never see it again.”

I bit the inside of my cheek, bitter frustration threatening to bubble over. Once something hit the black market, it was as good as gone.

Unless…

My mind reeled, zeroing on the tiny calculations of a plan. It wasn’t ideal. Not by any means. But I would make it work.

Before handing over the sword, I carefully, discreetly slid my fingers over the hilt, embedding a small tracking seal into the wood. The teen then activated a transaction protection seal, binding us both to the terms. He held the sword up, admiring it before slipping it carefully into a cloth wrap.

It’s going to be fine, I assured myself silently. I’d put the tracking seal. I’d locate and retrieve the sword after the transaction seal expired.

“Now about that girl,” he said lazily, “her name’s Eri. White robes, veil, hair like yours. She headed toward Arkina on the Fizto Border. I suggested the Heinna Forest route. Risky territory, what with the bandits and all, but it’ll shave a day off her travel.”

My stomach twisted, but I nodded stiffly. “Thank you,” I muttered hollowly. Turning to my horse, I swung into the saddle.

The world blurred as I pushed the horse into a hard gallop, the cold slicing into my skin. Each breath burned my lungs.

The shadows of my failures loomed. Father would be waiting back at the evaluation ceremony, but I wouldn’t come.

I had failed him.

I had failed my duty.

The heirloom sword—the legacy I was supposed to protect—was now in the hands of a stranger I couldn’t trust. It felt like I’d torn a hole in the fabric of my duty.

But I didn’t have any choice. Shiro was on her way, putting distance between us with every passing moment. Every second mattered. There was no time to stop. No time to regret. No time to weigh consequences.

I shrugged. “The truth—well, most of it. I’ll say I returned to retrieve something I left behind and stumbled upon traffickers trying to drug and abduct Akarui. You didn’t know I was following you. We only met on the train and managed to escape together. It’ll align with what actually happened. Don’t worry, Shiro. Just corroborate the story when they question you.”

Her lips pressed into a tight line. But after a moment of deliberation, she nodded. Without another word, she turned and headed home.

I stood motionless under the tree, watching her retreat until she reached the intersection and disappeared.

With her gone, I finally allowed myself to take a breath.

The world seemed to tilt. My legs buckled as the full weight of exhaustion, hunger, and pain slammed into me. Leaning heavily against the tree trunk, I covered my burning eyes with my hands, struggling to steady myself.

Everything came crashing down.

Father’s anger. The scorn of the elders. The punishments waiting for me.

My head spun.

“Mother…” I whispered.

I’m scared.

I squeezed my eyes shut.

Come on, Tadashi. Don’t crumble now.

I don’t know if I can bear their wrath.

Your task isn’t over yet.

What if I’m not strong enough?

Get up. Don’t let them wait any longer.

Help me… please.

Don’t make them angrier. They’ve been waiting for you since morning.

Please, lend me some strength….

Stand up. Now!

I shuddered as I pushed myself upright, my breath shallow. I swallowed hard and fixed my gaze on the imposing silhouette of the main hall.

It’s okay.

I’m still alive.

Shiro is safe. That’s what matters.

I can do this…

I repeated the mantra as I forced my battered body forward. With unsteady steps, I made my way toward the main hall, where the storm was waiting.

I never imagined that when I’d next see my home, it would be after such a long time.

The elders didn’t strip me of my title as clan heir outright. No, that would’ve been too simple, too merciful. Instead, they devised something crueler. They made it clear where my loyalties should lie: the clan first, always . My family second—if at all.

After the main hall hearing that stretched until dawn, the verdict was cold and final: physical punishment, confinement in the isolation room, exile from home, and an atonement mission.

The punishment began immediately. I was thrown into the confinement room—a cold, dark cube that seemed to swallow all sound, all light, all time. They wanted me to think and rethink my betrayal .

Once released, the exile began. I would leave my family—not for a time, but indefinitely. I was forbidden from entering the house, speaking to my siblings, or even catching a fleeting glimpse of their faces. I wasn’t allowed near them, even in their most vulnerable moments.

And my atonement… was a mission unlike any I had ever been assigned.

Stripped of my name, rank, and identity, I was sent to serve every household in the clan. Disguised and under a vow of silence, I was to wander from door to door, carrying a letter bearing the clan leader’s seal—Father’s seal.

My orders were crystal clear: obey without hesitation . Clean their floors. Tend their fields. Watch their children. Stock their shops. Whatever anyone asked of me, I was to do it without a word of protest or refusal.

It was hell.

Every day brought a new task: humbling labor disguised as duty. I served in silence, bending to the whims of families who rarely extended even the basic kindness of food or shelter. Some nights I slept outside, lucky if I was thrown a blanket or some scraps.

Some families treated me indifferently, barely acknowledging my presence. Others took advantage of my punishment and silence, giving me grueling, meaningless jobs—or worse, for their amusement. Sadi and his father were particularly unrelenting. They summoned me time and again, assigning menial or grueling tasks just to see me bend to their will. Something about the smug way Sadi loomed over me in those moments felt more punishing than the actual work.

But, surprisingly, none of that was the hardest part.

The harsh and degrading treatment, the aching muscles, the constant hunger, the endless work—the servitude … they didn’t break me.

The silence did.

All this time, my siblings had been the brightest part of my day. Knowing they were safe gave me strength. But the clan severed that. I had no one to turn to.

I lost hours of sleep worrying about Shiro. Was she all right? Was Father angry with her? Was she punished, too? Could she continue her Empath training? I told myself that maybe with me gone, she wouldn’t be burdened with my emotions. That she might feel a little more at ease. Something about that thought made my chest hurt a little less.

And Raito—gods, Raito. I’d promised him a camping trip. But instead, I vanished. No explanation, no goodbyes. What did he think of me now? The memory of his accident haunted me. I had done nothing when he was hospitalized—not because I didn’t care, but because I wasn’t allowed to. I couldn’t hold his hand and comfort him.

I wasn’t there when he needed me most. Would he ever forgive me?

But none of it compared to Father.

Always, it came back to Father.

The memories of him from that night clawed at my mind.

During the hearing, Father had been calm. Too calm. He listened to my explanation without interruption. He didn’t yell, didn’t strike me, didn’t raise his voice even once. He didn’t add to my punishment or express his fury for the elders to see. It made my skin crawl in a way shouting never could.

When the tribunal concluded, the elders dispersed, and the decision was final… Father didn’t even look at me until we were alone, sitting face-to-face across the tatami mat. He was quiet for so long I thought I might suffocate.

Then, in a low, quivering voice, he said the words I feared most:

“I’m disappointed in you, Tadashi. Very disappointed.”

It stung more deeply than any flogging ever could.

All thoughts fled my mind. I couldn’t summon a single syllable. My throat was dry, my chest too tight to force out any words. I could only sit there, staring down at the mat beneath my knees as his disappointment pressed on me.

“Think about what you’ve done,” Father said, his voice trembling slightly. “Think about whether you’d do the same if given another chance. Do you understand the consequences of your choices?”

A hundred answers warred in my head. I wanted to tell him that I had no regrets about protecting Shiro. I wanted to tell him how I tried so hard to honor Mother’s memory, to uphold her final wish to protect our family.

But no words came. No defense. No argument.

No excuses.

I just sat there, my guilt and shame swimming in the pool of his disappointment. My throat felt tight, my vision blurring with tears as I lowered my head, unable to meet his eyes.

“This family has made you weak,” he said plainly, his tone steady again, as if he’d come to a quiet conclusion. “I’ve failed to make you independent, and you still depend too much on your siblings. That’s why this punishment must go beyond what the elders have given.”

I lifted my teary gaze slightly, confused.

“Pardon?” I croaked.

Father’s chilling words followed:

“When your eighteenth birthday comes, you will leave our house permanently.You will live your own life, away from this family, until you prove your independence. We are not holding a place for you anymore, Tadashi.”

For a long, aching moment, I was speechless. My mind was blank.

Did I hear him right? Was he kicking me out?

“I will not help you,” he finished. “You are on your own.”

The air shuttered.

I wanted to tell him how sorry I was. I would promise him I’d fix things. But no words came—not as I sat there, trembling, tears streaking my face.

I prostrated myself on the ground, bowing deep, pressing my forehead against the tatami mat.

“Father,” I whispered hoarsely, choking. “ Please … Please forgive me.”

For five agonizingly long minutes, he said nothing. He didn’t move, didn’t make a noise.

Finally, I heard the soft scrape of his steps as he rose. The door slid open, and seconds later—it slid shut again.

He left me there, bowing and struggling just to breathe. Just walked away without acknowledgment, as if I wasn’t his son anymore.

Eighteen months.

Eighteen months of Father’s silence stretching between us like a frozen river. He never let his voice thaw. Even when I knelt in front of him to submit reports, he never lifted his gaze from the scrolls.

Father never looked at me again. Not really.

He’d stare at the wall behind my shoulder, his eyes glazed. My greetings dissolved in the space between us, unanswered.

The pain didn’t burst. It crystallized—a jagged, permanent thing wedged beneath my ribs. Some nights, I’d press my palm to my sternum, half-expecting to feel his disappointment splintering through my bone.

I’d hear his voice sometimes: Disappointed, Tadashi. Very disappointed.

It echoed in empty rooms. In market streets. In the rasp of my own breath.

And when I finally dared to imagine his face… all I could see was the back of his head, turned away forever.

I used to believe the hardest thing in the world was enduring Father’s disappointment. But I was wrong. This was harder. This was worse.

Yet in the stillness between missions, I remembered—his hands guiding mine over a practice sword, his voice firm but steady: A true leader protects what matters, Tadashi. Even when it’s hard.

So I bowed lower, fought harder, endured longer.

If proving my worth meant silence, I would bite my tongue.

If it meant obedience, I would kneel.

If it meant waiting…

I’d press my forehead to the floor until the tatami lines imprinted on my skin.

Because one day, perhaps, somewhere in the space between my father’s silence and my own resolve, the frozen river would thin—just enough to imagine spring.

Stepping back into the room I thought I’d left behind felt surreal. Just yesterday, I had sworn never to return, yet here I was, surrounded by inescapable memories. The house was eerily quiet. Raito was already asleep, and no one else was home. For once, the absence of others’ emotions offered a strange, fleeting comfort.

Now, it was just my own feelings—tangled, chaotic, and relentless. Every thread led back to Tadashi.

Why did he always take everything on himself? Why did he bear every consequence alone, even when today’s events weren’t entirely his fault? He had done his best with impossible choices. Part of me was frustrated, even resentful, at being treated as though I were helpless. I could take responsibility for my actions. I should have been the one to stand before the elders, not him.

Tadashi had to stop making these choices that cast him as the sole bearer of blame. Even Father and the elders knew it, I thought bitterly. No one could afford a clan leader torn between family and clan responsibilities during critical moments.

Fairness had no place here—not in the sacrifices demanded of a leader.

Father understood this. He had always accepted the unfairness. But Mother? She had opposed it with all her fire. She had once fought fiercely against the elders’ decision to appoint me as clan heir.

At the time, I hadn’t understood. I rejected the role because Mother ordered me to, and I wanted to please her. Deep down, there was a pang of envy watching Tadashi take the position. Father’s gaze lingered on him, not me. I mastered everything Tadashi was taught faster, yet I wasn’t in the spotlight. I wasn’t the rising star.

All I wanted was to be seen. To be acknowledged.

But as the years passed, and I watched Tadashi’s transformation under the crushing expectations, I finally understood what Mother had seen.

One day, I might have my own children, my own family. The thought of sacrificing their needs to an endless cycle of clan politics felt unbearable. That price was too high. A price Mother knew no parent should have to pay.

Watching Tadashi, I realized I could never live as he did. I could never let others control every aspect of my life—when I ate, how much I rested, whom I associated with. Tadashi had no close friends, no leisure, no personal time, no true freedom. The elders intentionally forged him this way; that’s what they demanded of a clan leader: complete and utter dedication.

For years, my brother endured this unrelenting pressure.

Tadashi was strong. An incredible, unyielding kind of strong. Stronger than me.

I didn’t wish this life on him. But he did it for my sake. He took the burden. Deep down, I was glad he chose to protect me.

When I was around him, I felt unconditionally loved. His love was a bond that grounded me, tethered me to him. A love so fierce it gave me strength, but also shackled me, chaining me to his storm of emotions—his stress, his fear, his pain.

My emotions swirled in contradictions. Relief and guilt tangled together. I didn’t want him hurt, but I was grateful for his protection. I cherished his love, but it suffocated me—trapped in these walls, entangled in his emotional tides.

But it didn’t have to continue like that, right? I could learn to control my empathic abilities, even without a direct teacher. I could face my weaknesses, overcome them on my own.

Someday, I vowed, I would control the powers that overwhelmed me. I would break free—not only from the weight of family expectations but from my own dependence.

But what about Tadashi? Could he ever escape his chains?

That night, Father and Tadashi didn’t come home.

I told myself it was nothing unusual. Father often stayed overnight at the main hall. But Tadashi… no matter how late, he always came back. As the hours dragged on and morning arrived, his absence gnawed at me. Why hadn’t anyone contacted me? The elders should have at least summoned me for a testimony.

By the second day, I paced endlessly around the kitchen, tension coiling. Anko eventually broke the silence, telling me the elders had requested a written account. I scribbled it out in a rush, hands shaking with frustration. But even after submitting it, there was no sign of Tadashi or Father.

Raito began to grow restless, his usual brightness dimming as unease settled over our home like a heavy fog.

By the end of the third day, I couldn’t take it anymore. I couldn’t just sit there, not knowing what had happened to my brother.

That night, I snuck into the main hall, determined to find answers.

The isolation ward was poorly secured; no guards, just a locked door. It wasn’t difficult to break through. This wasn’t a prison—just a place for disciplining wayward clan members.

Using a small tool hidden in my sleeve, I picked the lock and slipped into the narrow, dimly lit hallway. The air was cold and oppressive as I descended the steep stairs to the main underground level, which served as both a waiting area and a rudimentary kitchen.

The emptiness struck me like a blow. Nothing—no warmth, no trace of life, just a cold, utilitarian space.

I found a ledger on the table, listing the names of those held in isolation. My heart clenched as I saw Tadashi’s name under Room A1.

With trembling hands, I retrieved the key from the box near the first-aid supplies and made my way to the door.

As I unlocked the heavy iron door, a wave of emotions nearly knocked me off my feet. Tadashi’s raw feelings crashed into me, tearing through my defenses.

PAIN.

Hunger .

Cold.

Sadness.

Regret.

Guilt.

Fear.

Loneliness —it all hit in a relentless torrent.

And then, something I’d never felt from him: despair. So crushing, my breath caught. Tadashi knew no one was coming to save him.

I staggered, clutching the doorframe as I gasped, his emotions consuming me like an unstoppable tide.

The small, dim room smelled of damp stone and neglect. A single oil lantern flickered weakly on the wall beside the door, casting dancing shadows on the gray walls. No furnishings—no futon, no blanket, no table or chair, not even a shelf. No comfort. A short dividing wall barely concealed the toilet with its endlessly running tap. No windows, just a few iron hooks embedded high in the walls.

In the far corner, Tadashi sat huddled, knees drawn to his chest. His white inner kimono was filthy, stained with blood, dirt, and sweat, clinging like a tattered shroud. His outer kimono—the ceremonial one—was gone.

I closed the door softly. The sound made him flinch, and his head snapped up, his red, swollen eyes locking onto mine. He recoiled.

For a moment, emotions bombarded me again—shame, humiliation, fear—each sharper than the last. They radiated in intense waves. He seemed to shrink under my gaze, his anxiety spiking so high it was almost a scream in my ears.

He was ashamed for me to see him like this. He was terrified I was being punished with him.

“Tadashi,” I whispered, my voice trembling as I crossed the room.

“Shiro?” he whispered hoarsely, his voice fragile and uncertain. “What are you doing here?”

I didn’t answer immediately. Slowly, I walked over, sinking to my knees at his side. He flinched, his body tensing. Gently, I reached for his wrist, noticing the red, chafed marks left by the chains. His fingers were icy. The splint on his thumb hung loose, and his bandages were in desperate need of replacing.

“You didn’t come home,” I said quietly. “I was worried. So I snuck in.”

Tadashi’s unfocused gaze dropped to the hand I was holding. “Is this… not a dream?” he murmured, confusion in his trembling voice.

“No. It’s not a dream,” I replied softly.

Reaching into my bag, I pulled out the onigiri I’d packed, along with a thermos of hot medicinal tea. The moment his eyes fell on the food, I felt a rush of emotions so sharp and sudden it cut through me like a blade. Wonder, joy, relief, gratitude—overwhelming and pure.

How could something so simple mean so much?

My jaw clenched. Had they not fed him at all?

I poured tea into a small cup and held it out, urging him to eat. His hands trembled as he reached for the food, but despite his hunger, Tadashi ate with the deliberate, measured grace of someone accustomed to hiding his suffering. The etiquette drilled into him since childhood kept him eating slowly, without a trace of desperation.

The longing he felt—with every bite—stabbed at my chest. I recognized it. I felt it every time Tadashi thought of Mother. He was remembering her by savoring the food.

I looked away, swallowing the knot of emotion, trying to ignore the faint clink of the chains binding his wrists.

The onigiri didn’t last long. He finished, his emotions swirling—grateful, yet still hungry.

Why , why did I only bring one?

Tadashi’s hand gently touched my arm, pulling my attention back. His dark eyes were filled with an earnestness that brought me to the verge of tears.

“Thank you for the meal,” he whispered softly, his voice laden with quiet sincerity. It wasn’t just thanks for the food—it was a thank-you for remembering him, for treating him with kindness. “It was delicious, Shiro.” His gaze held mine, his tone genuine. “Your cooking is always so good… I’m not lying. It’s always good.”

Oh, Tadashi…

Tears welled up as I quickly covered my mouth to muffle a sob. Memories flooded back, painfully vivid. I remembered accusing him of lying when he’d once complimented my cooking.

It was just a couple of weeks ago. He hadn’t been eating, yet he still thanked me and praised my cooking. I was in a bad headspace; Tadashi’s stress over the clan heir evaluation meeting was becoming too much. I told him he didn’t have to pretend, that he didn’t need to lie.

But that wasn’t the truth.

Even then, I knew. Tadashi wasn’t eating because Father had scolded him harshly—his appetite had vanished out of stress and guilt, not because of my food. He’d said it was delicious back then, too… and I hadn’t believed him.

Now I felt the depth of that sincerity, his genuine love for what I made. He wasn’t lying—not then, not now. I’d always known. I shouldn’t have accused him. I shouldn’t have taken out my frustration on him. I began to cry openly, tears rolling down my cheeks.

Tadashi’s face twisted with fear and worry, distressed by my outburst.

“Shiro… why are you crying?” he murmured, his voice trembling with concern.

I couldn’t answer. Words felt too hollow, too insufficient. Without thinking, I leaned forward and wrapped my arms around him, burying my face in his shoulder.

He froze, stiffening with pain. Startled, I withdrew, but before I could apologize, he reached out weakly and pulled me close.

“It’s okay,” he whispered, his voice soft and soothing, like Mother’s used to be. His movements were restricted by the chains—he couldn’t hug me back properly or stroke my hair—but his words still carried warmth.

“Shh… It’s okay. I’m here. Don’t worry, Shiro. Everything’s going to be okay.”

Oh, Mother …

Guilt consumed me.

Tadashi had always protected me. He had always been there. I’d been selfish, blind to the sacrifices he bore. He was scared, lonely, cold, in pain, and starving—all because of me. Yet despite everything, his love was pure and overwhelming. It never faltered. He still poured all of that love into me.

I didn’t deserve him.

I pulled away, slowly wiping my tears.

“I’m sorry,” I whispered softly, though I knew it was not enough.

But Tadashi didn’t blame me. Instead, he leaned forward and kissed my forehead, a gesture so gentle, so tender, it broke whatever wall I had left.

I took his hand and held it firmly, offering the small comfort of my touch.

“Tadashi,” I said after a moment, unable to keep the worry from my voice. “When are you coming home?”

He didn’t answer immediately. He glanced down at his clothing—the dirtied, torn remnants of his ceremonial robes.

A sharp pain stabbed into my temple, signaling something unpleasant and familiar. I’d felt it during other moments when I asked Tadashi about his missions.

The speech seal .

I gritted my teeth. The elders—or rather, Father—had placed a seal on Tadashi to prevent him from freely discussing certain topics. It had weighed on him since he was named heir.

He never mentioned it to Mother. He hadn’t told anyone. Not even when the seal caused so much pain, he nearly passed out.

“Not for a long time, Shiro,” he finally said, his voice low and strained. When the seal was active, he could barely speak. It physically hurt him. That was how the seal worked. “I… Can I trust you to watch over Raito and Father?”

The air left my lungs. Tadashi had always looked out for our family. After our mother died and our father buried himself in work, Tadashi kept our family together. Father wasn’t there. He only checked on me and Raito through Tadashi. He made us Tadashi’s responsibility and scolded him if there was any trouble. Tadashi always prioritized our well-being and never asked anything for himself. Anything he ever asked for was always for the family.

I nodded numbly, unable to speak.

Silence settled, but Tadashi’s unease didn’t. His breathing quickened slightly, and I could feel him fighting—against the seal. He was pushing himself harder, forcing his message through sheer will.

“You know, Shiro…” His voice was faint. He struggled.

I stared, confused, as his pain bled into me.

“What is it?” I asked softly. What was so important?

His suffering mirrored, making my head feel like it was splitting. His breath hitched, his voice a raw whisper.

“Whatever happens… to me… Promise you’ll remember, Shiro.” His breathing stuttered. “I’ll always love you. You and Raito… and Father. I love you all so much.”

For a moment, I couldn’t breathe.

Tadashi…

His emotions washed over me—fierce, powerfully full of love, the words echoing infinitely. I squeezed his hand tightly, my throat too tight with emotion to speak.

Finally, I choked out, “I love you too.”

His gratitude flooded my senses, blanketing me in warmth. He bent his head and kissed my fingers lightly, unable to speak. His hands trembled as he bowed and gasped, visibly trying to regulate his breathing. I realized what he was doing. He remembered my Empath ability and was trying to hold his emotions in check… so he wouldn’t hurt me.

Oh, brother …

I took a deep breath, trying to control my own feelings. I stayed until he calmed. I changed the bandages on his wounds and helped ease his pain before finally leaving as he fell asleep.

When I silently walked out that night, I was an emotional wreck. This wasn’t right. This wasn’t fair. But the clan had decided and there was nothing I could do.

The next day, Tadashi didn’t come home.

Nor the day after.

Nor the one after that.

Again, and again, and again.

He was gone.

The days stretched into weeks. Tadashi didn’t come home.

Each day, Raito became more frustrated, his worry curdling into anger. Father told us Tadashi was on a mission, but I wasn’t sure I believed him.

I was lost in my own problems. But with Tadashi gone, the emotional intensity lessened. I started corresponding with my Empath teacher. I didn’t want to be controlled by others’ emotions. I trained hard. I wouldn’t be weak. Not anymore. Every day, I thought of my brother and prayed. For his safety. For his freedom. For him to find peace.

A year and a half passed before Tadashi finally came home.

Walking through the door, he looked different—thinner, paler, more weathered. The familiar warmth of his love was there, but his shoulders were tense, as if he expected the world to crash down on him. His eyes held relief and fear, glistening faintly under unspoken emotions. I could read him easily.

He was grateful to see us, hopeful that we would accept him. But he was also aware of the severity of his situation and dreaded that we might have turned away.

He didn’t say much. He didn’t explain anything. It wasn’t that he didn’t want to—his expressions made that clear. But the speech-seal still bound him, chains wrapped tightly around every word.

Raito, however, didn’t know about the seal and couldn’t care less about any restrictions from the clan. Seeing Tadashi walk through that door after disappearing—without a word, without explanation—was the spark that ignited a storm.

Raito exploded.

Every ounce of anger, betrayal, and grief he’d kept buried came rushing out like a dam bursting. His anger had simmered for months, unleashed in a single, furious torrent. His voice was loud, sharp, and unforgiving, echoing through the house as he hurled accusations that made Tadashi flinch.

Tadashi stood there, silent, taking it all. He faced Raito’s fury without defense, accepting every word, every shout, as punishment. He bowed his head and apologized, asking for forgiveness in a raw tone.

“Don’t you dare!” Raito yelled, his voice shaking. “You don’t get to apologize like everything’s fine! You LEFT us! You abandoned me!”

Raito demanded answers—answers Tadashi couldn’t give. He grew more furious when Tadashi didn’t—couldn’t—explain. “Say something!” he screamed. “You owe me an answer!”

When no answer came, the rift became irreversible. The bond they had shared, once strong, splintered. Raito hated Tadashi. Hated him fiercely, with all the passion of the love they had once shared.

I didn’t hear everything Raito said—I was too overwhelmed by their emotions. What I did hear made my chest constrict. When Raito finally finished and stormed away, there was nothing but silence.

And Tadashi… my brother stood unmoving, shoulders hunched, posture heavy as if the weight of the world rested on him. He didn’t speak. He didn’t cry. He just… stood there.

The pain radiating from him was unbearable.

Even with the mental shield I had painstakingly built—one my teacher had taught me to strengthen over months of practice—Tadashi’s emotions pierced through. They bled into me, unrelenting, like an open wound.

He never spoke about how much Raito’s words hurt. Raito spoke of him with bitterness, avoided him when he could. On the surface, Tadashi remained calm, patient, and composed around Raito, enduring every cold shoulder, biting remark, or moment of disdain. But I felt everything .

It was carved into my chest—a gaping, unhealable wound. The heartbreak, guilt, overwhelming regret, the desperate yearning to fix something that could never be undone, and unrelenting loneliness. A loneliness that deepened every day.

It was suffocating.

It was slowly, surely breaking him.

Two weeks passed, and I couldn’t bear to watch it any longer. I decided to teach him the same shielding techniques. I didn’t want to block him entirely—no matter his emotions, I didn’t want him abandoned. But I taught him to control what he emanated, to mask the sharpest spikes of pain.

To my surprise, Tadashi learned quickly. Within a week, his emotional signals faded. The ceaseless, jagged waves became softer, muted—like a storm quieted into gentle rain.

But his silence brought no relief.

Because for all his new control, Tadashi looked lonely.

And it tore me apart.

Some nights, I stayed awake, wondering if things would have been different if I hadn’t run away. If I had been more careful and thorough in my planning. If I hadn’t left that note, making it easy for him to follow, to abandon his duties, and face the consequences alone.

Would the clan still punish him?

Would Father still ignore him?

Would Raito still hate him?

Would any of this have happened if I had never allowed myself to become a burden?

All of it felt like my fault.

A failure I couldn’t fix.

One rainy afternoon, the two of us sat at the kitchen table, sipping tea. The house was quiet except for Raito’s muffled footsteps upstairs.

Tadashi was unusually restless. The silence wasn’t peaceful; it was heavy, weighed down by unspoken thoughts and feelings. His emotions brushed the edges of my senses, restrained—frustration, sadness, regret.

I didn’t know what had happened, what he’d encountered in his missions, what was bothering him, but I could tell it was too much for him to process. He stared into his cup of tea, lost.

“Tadashi,” I said softly, breaking the silence.

He glanced up, startled. His shadowed gaze met mine. He did that cursory sweep he always did, checking to see if we were okay.

Before he could answer, I reached out and placed my hand gently over his.

“You know, Tadashi,” I repeated, my voice quiet but firm. I looked at him, trying to convey the one truth I needed him to understand. “No matter what happened… I still love you. We all do. Me, Raito, Father… all of us. We all love you, Tadashi.”

His expression slowly shifted. His lips quivered, his eyes glossing over as the emotions he’d locked away rippled beneath the surface. He looked like he wanted to cry.

But instead, Tadashi smiled. Not the bright, carefree smile he wore as a child, but a small, fragile one—like the first ray of sunlight breaking through heavy clouds.

“Thank you, Shiro,” he murmured softly. He squeezed my hand and stood, taking his tea with him to his room.

From that day on, I made it a point to sit with him for tea whenever I could.

Often, we didn’t exchange many words. There wasn’t much that needed to be said. Sitting together, offering a quiet presence that told him I knew—truly knew—how deeply he cared… That was enough.

I did my best to remind him, day after day, he wasn’t alone.

That his family was still here, still loving him, still waiting.

I made sure he felt it, helped him hold on, so he could find the strength to rise, even if only for one more day.

Today.

Tomorrow.

The next day.

And the day after that.

For as long as he needed.

Over and over again.
