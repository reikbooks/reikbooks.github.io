Over and Over Again

Book 8 of Masamune Clan Series

A test of skill. A demand for perfection. Everything he gives for the ones he loves.

For five years, Tadashi Masamune has been trained to obey, endure, and prove himself worthy of his clan’s legacy. His father’s command is absolute: succeed—failure is not an option. But on the day of his performance evaluation, fate intervenes, forcing him to make an impossible choice—one that will test the limits of loyalty, sacrifice, and the true meaning of family.

I thought the hardest thing in the world was enduring Father’s disappointment.

My head felt like it was going to burst.

It had been a brutal day, one that started before sunrise. The ache in my head pulsed in time with the gash on my upper arm—a memento from last night’s mission, throbbing like a second heartbeat. I’d barely managed two hours of fevered sleep, nowhere near enough to recover. But rest was a luxury beyond my reach. As long as I managed to stay upright and functional, there’d be no relief in my schedule.

Father dragged me out for morning drills before rushing me to the academy, the taste of blood still sharp in my tongue. Classes blurred into afternoon training, followed by duties at the clan’s main hall. By the time the sun dipped below the horizon, I was bone-tired, but the day wasn’t over. Dragging myself home, I clung to the faint hope of collapsing onto my futon. But I knew better. A mountain of textbooks and half-finished assignments waited, a looming reminder of tomorrow’s quiz.

It’s okay. One step at a time. Just breathe.

“I’m home,” I rasped, slipping off my sandals and placing them neatly in the genkan .

In the kitchen, Shiro stood behind the counter, her gaze fixed on a piece of paper. As I entered, she quickly folded it and slipped it into a thick book.

“Welcome back,” she said, her tone curt.

I hesitated. Was that sharpness in her voice, or was it my imagination? Had I done something to upset her? She turned back to the kitchen table, ignoring me. I bowed my head and mumbled an apology. Maybe she was irritated because my lateness had delayed dinner.

“Food!” Raito’s voice erupted from the living room.

An energetic blur shot past as he tossed his homework onto the dining table and darted into the kitchen, eyes wide with curiosity as he peered into the pot on the stove.

Shiro scowled and rapped him on the head with a rice ladle. “Sit down. I’ll serve dinner,” she said firmly.

Pushing aside my fatigue, I slipped to the hallway sink and splashed cold water to my face, trying to jolt myself awake and scrub away the weight of the day. When I sat down opposite Raito, I offered him a small smile.

“How was your day?”

“Great!” His eyes lit up. “We learned how to give first aid to unconscious people! Like heroes!”

I chuckled, warmth stirring deep inside me. He launched into another topic, reminding me of the upcoming camping trip. His words tumbled out in an eager rush, his voice rising with insistence.

“It’s super-duper special, so you have to bring someone special too! You’ll be the worst big brother in the world if you don’t come!!”

Mustering a faint smile, I promised him I’d be there—once I finished my duties at the main hall.

Soon, the comforting aroma of fish soup wafted through the dining area.

“Do we need to wait for Father to get home?” Raito asked hesitantly, his stomach rumbling loudly in protest.

Our father’s schedule was unpredictable. Some nights, he came home early for dinner; other times, he didn’t make it at all. I didn’t want my siblings to go hungry, so I nudged Raito and Shiro toward their bowls.

“Eat. I’ll hold the table.”

I sipped bitter green tea instead, trying to ignore the hollow ache in my stomach. I hadn’t eaten since dawn—skipping breakfast for Father’s drills and forgetting the bento Shiro had packed for lunch. But I couldn’t bring myself to eat before him. Someone had to be there to greet him when he returned, to share the meal. Leaving him to eat alone felt… disrespectful.

Father arrived just as Shiro and Raito were finishing. I rose immediately and greeted him with a deep bow.

“Welcome home, Father,” I said, taking his jacket and work bag. “My apologies for starting dinner in your absence.”

Shiro plated his meal while Raito launched into an animated account of his day. Normally, Father would listen with a small smile, but tonight, his face was grim. A pang of unease slithered down my spine.

Something bad had happened in the clan.

My gut clenched. Something involving me.

My appetite vanished. Years of reading the storm in his silence told me to brace myself. This would end in his study, with me kneeling on tatami as he dissected my failures. But tonight, he broke the pattern.

He waited until Raito’s footsteps faded upstairs.

“Akito claims Sadi reported your injury.” His chopsticks clicked against the bowl—a swordsman testing his blade. “That you hindered the team and compromised the mission.” Father’s gaze flicked to my bandaged arm. “Is this true?”

The accusation struck like a shuriken. My mind went white in static.

“I… didn’t—” I stammered, unable to finish.

How could I explain without sounding pathetic? Yes, I’d taken a knife meant for Sadi’s flank. No, I hadn’t faltered until the extraction point. The bloodied part of the bandages hidden beneath my sleeve itched in betrayal.

Explain, a part of me begged. Tell him you patched the gash yourself, that you bled silently.

I dug my nails into my palms.

Be quiet and take the hit. Excuses are for the weak.

“I thought you were capable of handling difficulties in missions and knew how to conduct yourself.” Father’s voice bore down on me, each word heavier than the last. “Do I need to remind you—again—that the clan heir evaluation is in just a few days?”

My neck burned in submission. As if I could forget. Elder Mei had drilled it into me repeatedly. This month, the scrutiny had been relentless. Every move critiqued, every mistake punished—often with lashes or canes. I had barely kept up with the elders’ endless demands.

“Listen, Tadashi,” Father continued, his voice firm. “You only need to maintain your accomplishments. Always strive for excellence. For the sake of our clan, do not let Sadi take that position from you. Do you understand?”

My throat tightened. No matter how hard I tried, the elders only saw my shortcomings. And Sadi? He was his father, Akito’s, shadow—polished, cunning, sharpened like a katana. His only flaw was laughing too loud, too free, in halls that demanded whispers. The elders and Father despised his brashness. But to Akito’s supporters, it was hardly a flaw.

I didn’t want to make promises I couldn’t keep. But there was nothing else to say.

I forced myself to nod. “Yes, Father. I understand.”

Father finished his meal in silence. When he rose, he didn’t call me to his study.

A small mercy.

I bowed as he left the room and remained rooted in place, lost in thought.

“You’re welcome. Enjoy your rest,” Shiro quipped dryly from the far end of the table, jolting me from my reverie. I’d almost forgotten she was still there.

“Ah. S-Sorry, Shiro,” I croaked. “Thank you for the meal. It was delicious.”

Shiro snorted.

“You didn’t even eat. Save the lies for Father.” She swept up the bowls, porcelain clattering like thrown dice.

Heat scorched my cheeks. The praise was automatic, but I never meant to lie. Shiro had a talent for cooking; her meals always reminded me of Mother’s. Did she know how much I truly appreciated her? I often ate slowly, savoring it to preserve Mother’s memory just a little longer.

Not wanting to hurt her more, I swallowed the guilt and quietly helped her with the dishes. Lately, Shiro had felt… distant. She seemed more irritable than usual, though I couldn’t figure out why.

Did she feel neglected?

I bit my lip. In my free moments, I spent more time with Raito. But Shiro did so much for the household, taking on responsibilities no one else noticed.

Had I forgotten something important to her? Her birthday? No, I’d remembered this year, and she’d liked the gift. Was something bothering her? Did she need something? New clothes? Makeup? Perhaps there was a book she wanted? Or maybe an issue with her friends? Maybe I should ask Anko—she and Shiro occasionally chatted at the clan library.

I used to read alongside Shiro, sharing newspapers or magazines and laughing over silly articles. But now… Now I couldn’t even recall the last time we simply talked about her interests.

What kind of brother have I become? I clenched my fists. How could I be so blind to my sister’s needs?

When we finished cleaning, Shiro hurried upstairs to her room without a word. I followed, my concern growing with each step.

“Shiro,” I called softly as we reached her door. “Are you alright? You look pale.”

She turned to me sharply, her expression flickering between frustration and something else—pain, perhaps. A hint of tears threatened in her eyes, and for a moment, I couldn’t tell whether she was about to break down or lash out.

Instead, Shiro shook her head and forced a tight smile.

“I’m fine . Good night, Tadashi,” she said briskly, slamming her door shut before I could reply.

I stood frozen on the landing, staring at the unyielding wood, the sound echoing in the sudden silence. A knot tightened in my chest.

Calm down. It’s okay. Breathe.

I stumbled to my own room, closing the door behind me with a soft click. Hunger and the throbbing in my arm gnawed at me, but they were muted. My mind was elsewhere, consumed by a single, urgent thought.

Something is going on with my sister.

I have to find out what it is.

The more I observed my sister, the more she seemed to slip away, like sand through my fingers. Every time I tried to ask if something was wrong, she either ignored me or brushed me off. Even Anko couldn’t offer any insight, but I knew, deep down, that something was seriously wrong. I just… didn’t know how to make her open up.

Meanwhile, the clan’s demands consumed what little energy I had left. Night after night, the elders summoned me to meetings that stretched on for hours, each one a grueling test of endurance. They seemed to find fault with my every move, their harsh words a constant, stinging reminder of my perceived inadequacies. And Father—well, Father rarely missed the chance to call me into his office afterward, his tone heavy with advice. The kind of advice that sounded suspiciously close to veiled reprimands.

I… I couldn’t breathe.

By the final day before the successor evaluation, I was running on fumes. That afternoon, preparing for the final meeting felt like preparing for my own execution. Tomorrow, judgment would be passed.

I hadn’t managed a full night’s sleep in two days. My body was a wreck, a collection of aching joints and frayed nerves, protesting each movement. No position in bed brought comfort, only a deeper awareness of the throbbing pain. My mind refused to rest, locked in an endless loop of worst-case scenarios. Every potential misstep, every imagined disappointment in Father’s eyes, every condemning look from the elders—these fears crushed the air from my lungs.

Failure wasn’t an option. I had to keep the title of successor. Not for my own sake, but for Shiro’s.

I thought about Mother for the hundredth time that week. Her face hovered in my memory, her voice gentle yet firm. She had envisioned a different life for Shiro and had taken the risks to change her daughter’s path.

Shiro had scored the highest on the successor selection test and was the rightful candidate to inherit the role, but she rejected the position. Sadi, who scored second, should’ve been next, but Mother had recommended me to the elders as Shiro’s replacement—a bold decision that Father endorsed, because he didn’t want Sadi to take the place. He proposed a vote and managed to secure the elders’ support for me, who ranked third.

So they chose me as the clan heir.

I didn’t want the position, but everyone had placed their faith in me. I wish not to disappoint them, especially my parents.

Mother’s decision couldn’t have been easy, but she must have believed I could bear this burden.

…So I had to be strong. For Shiro.

I’d promised myself she wouldn’t have to endure this life.

I had to carry this weight.

It was tough, but I must keep going. Just a little longer, right? Once the evaluation was over, I could finally rest. Everything… everything would be alright.

On my way to the main hall, I passed through the kitchen to grab a drink. That was when I saw her.

Shiro was at the stove, cooking dinner—but her shoulders shook with quiet sobs. Tears streamed down her face, spilling silently as she stirred the food.

I stopped dead in my tracks.

My stomach churned, twisting.

“Shiro,” I whispered, stepping closer. “What’s wrong?”

She gasped and immediately tried to compose herself, wiping at her tears with the back of her hand. I reached out to touch her arm, hoping to comfort her, but she shoved me away with a force that stunned me. For a moment, I just stood there, staring, unsure of what to say or do.

“You know, Shiro…” I began softly, struggling to keep my voice steady. “You can tell me anything. I’ll listen. Always.”

A bitter laugh escaped her lips, sharp and jagged.

“It’s not important,” she snapped, her words cutting like shards of glass. “Besides, you don’t have time. Don’t you have somewhere to be?”

The words stung, but I pushed past the hurt. “I-I’ll make time tomorrow, I promise,” I said earnestly. “After the evaluation, I’ll come straight home. I’ll spend the whole day with you. We can—”

BANG!

The sound of metal on metal reverberated through the room as Shiro slammed down the spatula, flipping the fish she was cooking so forcefully that the pan rattled. Without missing a beat, she stabbed the fish with the spatula in a jerking, almost violent motion.

I swallowed, my heart pounding.

“There’s nothing you need to worry about,” she barked, her back still turned to me. “Just go. They’re waiting for you.”

I remained rooted to the spot, paralyzed. I didn’t want to leave her like this… but I couldn’t abandon my duties either. For a painful moment, I just stood frozen in the kitchen, behind her. She must have sensed my hesitation because she let out an impatient huff.

I bit my lip.

“Shiro,” I whispered, my voice trembling. “Are you… mad at me?”

She shook her head without turning around.

“Did I do something to upset you?”

This time, she snorted.

“I just don’t want to talk to you right now.”

Her words hit harder than any blow I’d ever taken during training.

I fell silent. For the first time in years, I felt utterly helpless with her. What had I done to push her away like this?

“Shiro,” I whispered quietly, “I’m really… I’m really sorry.”

But Shiro didn’t respond. Eventually, I had no choice but to leave her there, alone.

I should have known something wasn’t right.

The morning of the evaluation arrived like a gut punch. My empty stomach convulsed as I hunched over in the bathroom and retched violently until bile burned my throat. My ribs ached as I wheezed. Cold sweat slicked my palms, and the room spun. Every muscle screamed, not from exertion, but from dread.

I staggered back to my room, lightheaded and weak, each step an effort.

When I gathered myself enough to tell Shiro I was leaving, her gaze lingered on me, unusually intense. She studied me quietly, her expression unreadable, yet heavy with unspoken emotion. Then, without a word, she led me to the small hearth in the kitchen and knelt by the tea set.

It was a ceremonial gesture, a ritual reserved for those heading off on an important mission. The air between us thickened, becoming hushed and reverent. The quiet scrape of the whisk against the bowl, the gentle splash of pouring tea—each sound was deliberate, grounding me.

I didn’t question the herbs she’d used. The first sip was earthy, a subtle bitterness giving way to a soothing warmth that spread through me. My racing heart began to slow. My breath steadied.

For a fleeting moment, I felt anchored. It was as if nothing else existed but this quiet moment, shared with her. Her presence was a sudden, sharp reminder of Mother…

I inhaled deeply, tears stinging my eyes.

As always, I felt unworthy of her kindness, her unwavering support. What could I possibly offer in return? I did the only thing I could: I took her hand, holding it firmly, and met her gaze.

Up close, I noticed the shadows beneath her eyes, the paleness of her skin. She must have been worried about me. All this time, I had wanted to ease her burdens, to see her smile… and here she was, grounding me in my moment of vulnerability, despite her own hidden struggles.

She held my gaze, deep and knowing. Then, her face softened—as if she understood the unspoken words trapped in my throat.

“You’ve worked so hard, Tadashi,” she said, her voice a low murmur. There was warmth in her tone, but also sadness, a weight I couldn’t decipher. She gave my hand a gentle squeeze. “You’ll pass the evaluation. You’ll be a great leader for the clan. I know it.”

Her faith in me was a knot in my throat.

“Thank you, Shiro,” I murmured. The words were not enough… nowhere near enough, but they were all I could manage.

She stood with me at the entrance of the house, seeing me off. A strange, almost wistful softness softened her eyes as she smiled.

I should have realized then.

Something was deeply wrong.

If I hadn’t forgotten something and returned to my room, I would never have discovered Shiro’s plan.

That morning, my sister had decided to run away from home.

Silence.

The kitchen was empty. The dining room and living area were eerily still. A prickle of unease ran down my spine. Something wasn’t right . Why was it so quiet?

I quickened my pace, taking the stairs two at a time to retrieve the heirloom sword from my room. The tantō —a family treasure Father himself had handed down—had been passed through generations of our clan leaders. More than just a weapon, it was a symbol of the clan’s blessing, a tangible representation of their trust. Wielding it was an honor, one I had to carry to today’s evaluation. If I passed, they would return it to me, the official mark of my continuation as heir.

I sighed, shaking my head at my carelessness. How could I have forgotten something so vital? At least I hadn’t gone far; I’d only reached the halfway point to the main hall before realizing it was missing.

With the sword now secure, I turned to leave, but a nagging feeling stopped me, an itch at the back of my mind.

The silence.

Raito would have left for the academy, but where was Shiro?

I turned on my heel and headed to her room, deciding to say goodbye again.

“Shiro?” I called, knocking softly on her door. Silence. No answer, even after a second and third knock.

A knot of apprehension tightened in my stomach. I eased the door open.

Her room was immaculate, as always. The polar opposite of Raito’s space—which usually resembled the aftermath of a natural disaster. Not a single item was out of place. No messy clothes, no scattered books. Shiro’s meticulous neatness had always reflected her personality—controlled, deliberate.

It was precisely because of this order that the single piece of white paper on her desk stood out. I stepped closer, my chest constricting. Then, I saw the small, delicate handwriting.

Tadashi,

By the time you read this, I’ll be long gone. I’m sorry I had to sneak away, especially on such an important day for you. But I know you’ll excel in the evaluation and be chosen as our clan’s next leader—congratulations, in advance. I wish I could be there to witness it, to give you the hug you deserve. No one else truly knows the sacrifices you’ve made to reach this moment, but I do. I’m so proud of you.

But I can’t stay.

Don’t try to find me.

Don’t send anyone to find me.

Don’t help anyone search for me.

Shiro Masamune is now a ghost—a name that will fade into whispers, a figure who moved away as a child to live with her mother’s distant relatives, untraceable and forgotten. Let it stay that way.

Akarui Masamune? She’s nobody. A quiet maid. A shadow in the room. A girl whose classmates barely noticed her absence. Akarui got the highest scores in her sewing classes, surpassed her teacher in cooking, crafted more goods than anyone else in less time. But no one truly saw her, knew her, or cared where she was going.

I don’t want to be Akarui anymore. And while I cherished being Shiro and the freedom to explore my skills, I can’t live under the shadow of the clan’s expectations. I’ve seen the burden they’ve placed on you, the crushing weight of responsibility, and the thought of carrying that myself… it suffocates me.

I can’t be Shiro. I won’t be Akarui.

I need to write my own story, one not dictated by Father or the clan. Please understand.

You don’t need me. I’ve made sure of that. You can handle the house, do your laundry, eat out if you must. Raito is old enough to take care of himself. And Father? He’s indifferent. My absence won’t matter to him. As long as Raito is there, everything will be fine.

Goodbye, Tadashi.

Sending all my love, from afar,

Shiro.

The letter slipped from my trembling fingers.

My mind blanked.

The only thought echoing through my entire being was: Find Shiro, now!

How could I not worry about her? She was my sister. Yes, she’d scold me for calling her fragile—her combat skills were leagues ahead of mine. But physical strength wasn’t everything, not when the world was filled with deceit and danger.

I crushed the letter in my hand, panic surging. She couldn’t have gone far. It had only been about thirty minutes since I’d left home.

But where would she go?

If she was heading out of town, she’d need a horse—that much was certain. But even if she got one, which direction?

Frantically, I scanned the room for clues. Anything that could tell me where she might have gone.

I yanked open her closet. Still full. She’d only taken a few clothes and a blanket. That ruled out a colder region.

Her friends? No, she’d said herself she wasn’t close to any of them. Wait… Anko . Yesterday, she’d mentioned Shiro borrowing a lot of books recently—research, she’d said.

I turned to Shiro’s bookshelves, which filled two entire walls. Her personal collection was arranged alphabetically, with meticulous precision. But the library books—she always kept them separate. Where would she put her most recent loans?

The middle shelf. And there they were. Books on psychology. Mental abilities. Hypnosis.

I frowned, furrowing my brow as I read the titles:

How to Unlock Hypnosis

Mastering the Power of Your Mind

Limitless Potential

Influencing the Subconscious of Others

The Invisible Force

The Touch of Mind Control

…and many more.

“What are these?” I murmured, flipping through the pages.

Shiro was different—special. I’d known that for years. She had an uncanny intuition, a way of understanding situations on a level that defied explanation. It wasn’t fortune-telling. It was more like… she comprehended everything from the inside out, allowing her to make eerily accurate predictions.

Did she actually have the power to read minds? Communicate without speaking? Influence others’ actions?

Whatever it was, she was clearly determined to enhance these abilities. She’d been studying. Preparing. It seemed she’d been searching for someone to guide her—and now, it looked like she’d found them.

But who? And where?

My gaze flicked back to the bookcase, landing on a thick, familiar volume: Emotional Understanding: A Guide. I’d seen her reading this very book in the kitchen—the same day I’d noticed her hiding a piece of paper.

Curious, I pulled it down and opened it to the bookmarked page. And there it was: a torn piece of an envelope, specifically the back. It looked blank, but… I held it up to the sunlight streaming through the window, hoping to catch any faint indentations.

—sighur.

—kina.

The first word… a name? And the second—likely a location. “Kina…” What could that mean? Ujakina Valley to the south of the Kawa Kingdom? Or Arkina City on the Fizto border?

It wasn’t enough. I needed more. My best bet was the stables. If Shiro had taken a horse, the stable master might have seen her, might know her destination.

Clutching the heirloom sword, I bolted out of the house, sprinting towards the stables.

Shiro hadn’t taken a horse from the clan stables.

I confirmed it by checking the stable guard’s ledger. Every clan member was required to fill out a form before borrowing a horse. Shiro’s name wasn’t there, which left only two possibilities: She could’ve rented a horse from the market, or she’d procured one from a ranch. Market horses were fast to acquire—pay some coins and ride away, no questions asked. But they were often poorly trained and not strong enough for rough terrain.

No, for the kind of journey she seemed to be on, she would’ve needed something better. A horse built for endurance. Which meant only one place—Arai Valley.

I urged my own horse toward the valley with a tightening in my chest. She couldn’t have gotten far. If I hurried, maybe I could catch up to her. Maybe I could convince her to come back, to come home.

But even as the hope flickered, doubt crept in.

Would she listen? Would she even care?

When I reached the Arai Valley ranch, an older man lingered near the stables, his hands calloused and weathered.

“No Shiro or Akarui Masamune rented a horse here today,” he said, shaking his head.

“But someone did take a horse this morning, right?” I pressed forward, a thread of desperation pulling at each word. Shiro must’ve been here. She had to have been here.

The old man squinted, considering, and then gave a slow shake of his head. “Only the carpenter’s son came early this morning. He sometimes picks up orders for other folks.”

It clicked. Of course. Shiro wouldn’t have come here herself—not if she was trying to keep her movements hidden. She’d used someone else. Paid them to handle it for her.

The old man, kind enough not to ask further questions, gave me directions to the carpenter’s son’s house. It wasn’t far from the ranch, close to the market district. I urged my horse faster, clinging to the fleeting hope that I was still on the right trail.

The teenager who answered the door had a mop of wild, curly hair and sharp eyes that swept over me with faint amusement. He was older than me, likely around Sadi’s age. He leaned casually against the doorframe, his smirk widening.

“Whoever my customer was, that’s none of your business,” he said, his voice dripping with mockery. “Unless, of course… you’re willing to pay.”

For a moment, I only stared at him, silently cursing my own idiocy. The clan successor evaluation was no ordinary event, and I’d dressed the part. I was still dressed in my finest formal clan kimono, its delicate embroidery screaming status and wealth. But I hadn’t brought any actual money. Not a coin. Not a means to barter.

The thought settled over me like a chilling weight. The only thing of value I had on me was the clan’s heirloom sword—the sacred blade resting at my side. For generations, it had been a relic of our family’s legacy, passed down to each generation as a symbol of leadership and responsibility. Its worth couldn’t be measured, not in money and certainly not in sentimental value.

For a fleeting, horrifying moment, the thought crossed my mind: Could I trade it? Could I give the sword away… for her?

The very idea made my stomach churn. It would stain my name forever. The clan’s sacred heirloom—gone, lost because of me. Father would never forgive me. The clan’d have every right to strip me of my title. It’d be a betrayal so huge, I’d surely be disowned.

Tortured for three days and three nights, then disowned.

…And yet, a smaller voice whispered.

Shiro’s safety is worth any price.

I would give anything, if it means Shiro is safe.

The thought lingered, quiet but unrelenting, as my fingers unconsciously tightened around the hilt of the blade.

“How about it?” Curly drawled, leaning lazily against the doorframe. His smirk was maddening. “There was a girl this morning. Maybe I know where she’s headed. Maybe I don’t. You want the details? You’ll have to pay.”

I clenched my fists, willing myself to stay calm. “I don’t have money,” I said through gritted teeth. “But I can offer you weapons—kodachi, shuriken, kunai. The finest quality.”

Curly let out a short, mocking laugh. “Do I look stupid? That junk’s worth nothing to me.” His sharpened gaze flicked down to the sword at my side—the heirloom. “But that , on the other hand… now that’s something special. I’ll take it.”

I stepped back instinctively, gripping the hilt tighter. “Absolutely not,” I replied sharply.

“Then we’ve got nothing to talk about.” He shrugged, spinning on his heel to leave. “Good luck asking around. Go ahead, waste more time. But I’ll tell you this—no one else knows what I know. You’ve got one shot, and it’s me.”

My heart thundered. There was no time for this. Shiro was out there, alone, heading toward… where? My grip on Curly’s arm stopped him mid-stride.

“Wait!”

The teen raised an eyebrow, clearly enjoying the desperation he saw in my face. “Change your mind?”

I exhaled slowly, trying to steady my voice. “Fine,” I hissed. “I will pawn it. But just as collateral. I’ll redeem it tomorrow. Don’t even think about selling it.”

His grin widened. “Sure, sure,” he said. “Collateral. Ten gold coins, twenty-four hours. If you don’t show up, the sword’s mine.”

Ten gold coins. Twenty-four hours. It was laughable—impossible. The savings Father had carefully put away for me since I was named heir wouldn’t come close. But he saw the ceremonial attire I was wearing and assumed I came from wealth. I couldn’t let him take advantage of me like that.

“Five gold coins,” I countered, narrowing my eyes.

Curly’s smirk vanished. He studied me. “Five gold coins,” he finally conceded with an exaggerated sigh, “but only twelve hours. A second longer, and I send it straight to the black market. Trust me, you’ll never see it again.”

I bit the inside of my cheek, bitter frustration threatening to bubble over. Once something hit the black market, it was as good as gone.

Unless…

My mind reeled, zeroing on the tiny calculations of a plan. It wasn’t ideal. Not by any means. But I would make it work.

Before handing over the sword, I carefully, discreetly slid my fingers over the hilt, embedding a small tracking seal into the wood. The teen then activated a transaction protection seal, binding us both to the terms. He held the sword up, admiring it before slipping it carefully into a cloth wrap.

It’s going to be fine, I assured myself silently. I’d put the tracking seal. I’d locate and retrieve the sword after the transaction seal expired.

“Now about that girl,” he said lazily, “her name’s Eri. White robes, veil, hair like yours. She headed toward Arkina on the Fizto Border. I suggested the Heinna Forest route. Risky territory, what with the bandits and all, but it’ll shave a day off her travel.”

My stomach twisted, but I nodded stiffly. “Thank you,” I muttered hollowly. Turning to my horse, I swung into the saddle.

The world blurred as I pushed the horse into a hard gallop, the cold slicing into my skin. Each breath burned my lungs.

The shadows of my failures loomed. Father would be waiting back at the evaluation ceremony, but I wouldn’t come.

I had failed him.

I had failed my duty.

The heirloom sword—the legacy I was supposed to protect—was now in the hands of a stranger I couldn’t trust. It felt like I’d torn a hole in the fabric of my duty.

But I didn’t have any choice. Shiro was on her way, putting distance between us with every passing moment. Every second mattered. There was no time to stop. No time to regret. No time to weigh consequences.

No time to think of what Father would say, what punishment awaited me.

None of it mattered more than the fact that my sister was out there. Somewhere on a road where bandits and monsters ruled the night. Alone. Vulnerable. Headed straight into danger.

I had to find her.

I had to bring her home.

I rode through the dense trails of Heinna Forest for hours. My horse’s hooves pounded against the earth, each step jarring through my bones. Time blurred into the rhythm of my own ragged breaths. My whole body ached, but I couldn’t stop.

I couldn’t stop until I found my sister.

Then my horse faltered. Its legs trembled before it let out a heavy, labored exhale. I barely managed to pull the reins in time, my grip sluggish, my fingers stiff with cold and tension. Guilt twisted in my chest. I had pushed it too hard—just as I had pushed myself. But this was the main path in the forest, and I’d hoped that I could catch up with my sister.

I slid off the saddle, my legs nearly buckling. The world spun, and I pressed my forehead against my horse’s neck, willing the dizziness away.

Mumbling an apology, I ran my hands along its body.

“Let’s… rest for a bit and get you something to drink,” I rasped.

I trudged forward, guiding my horse toward the lake’s edge. My muscles screamed in protest, exhaustion weighing down every step. As my horse drank, I let out a slow breath. My throat burned, my stomach twisted with hunger—but in my haste, I had left with no provisions.

I knelt by the waterline, plunging my hands into the cool depths before dragging them over my face, my arms, the back of my neck. My breath came in sharp gasps, but the icy touch steadied me. I raked a hand through my damp hair, trying—failing—to quiet the storm inside my head.

Where are you, Shiro?

Fear clawed at my ribs. My mind retraced the route I took. Was there any other shortcut Shiro might take? She must’ve rested somewhere, right? Maybe I should look around. This lake was a resting spot for travelers.

I walked along the path in the lake’s edge. A gust of wind stirred the trees, rustling the leaves like whispers in the dark. And then—movement. Across the clearing, a figure in a white cloak stood out against the deep green of the forest.

Shiro.

She was kneeling next to her grazing horse, adjusting her blanket. The golden edge of the sunlight caught in her hair. My heart surged—a messy tangle of relief, determination, and the quiet ache of unfinished words.

Without thinking, I bolted toward her.

“Shiro!”

Her head jerked up. For a fleeting second, our eyes locked. Panic flashed across her face before she leapt to her feet and ran.

“Shiro! Wait!” I shouted, sprinting after her. I tore through the underbrush, branches lashing at my arms. She was fast—faster than I remembered—but so was I. I reached for her arm just as I closed the distance.

“Don’t touch me!” she snarled, twisting out of my grip with surprising strength. She stumbled back, putting several feet between us. “And don’t call me that name anymore.”

I froze. “Shiro—”

“Eri,” she snapped, her voice hard as flint, her eyes narrowing. “My name is Eri now.”

The name—so foreign, so wrong—lodged itself uneasily on my tongue. It didn’t belong to her. Not the sister I knew. I swallowed the lump in my throat and tried again.

“Eri,” I said softly. The word tasted like ash. “Why are you running? What are you doing here?”

She laughed, bitter and hollow. “Isn’t it obvious? I’m leaving.”

The finality in her tone made my chest tighten. “Leaving? Why? Eri, what’s wrong?”

Her face twisted, anger and pain flaring.

“What’s wrong?” she echoed, her voice cracking. “ Everything , Tadashi. Everything is wrong. That house is suffocating me. I have no friends. No one knows who I really am. All I’ve ever done is pretend to be someone else. Someone quiet. Someone invisible. I’m nothing there. And if I stay, I’ll disappear completely.”

“That’s not true,” I said, trying to keep my voice steady. “Nothing’s stopping you from being yourself. Nothing’s stopping you from—”

“From what?” she cut in, her voice rising. “From going out? From going to school? Raito was still a toddler when Mom died, Tadashi. Someone had to care for him while Father buried himself in work. Someone had to keep the house running while you were off on clan duties.”

Her breath hitched, hands trembling. “Who cooked your meals? Washed your clothes? Held Raito when he cried looking for you?”

I had no answer.

“I did,” she whispered. “I carried everything. And what do I get in return? Father can’t even look at me without feeling ashamed. Don’t tell me you don’t see it. Don’t lie to me .”

I opened my mouth, but no words came.

“Do you know what that’s like?” she pressed on, voice breaking. “To feel that shame every single time he looks at me? His disappointment? I can’t take it anymore, Tadashi. I just… I can’t .”

Her confession knocked the air from my lungs. But I forced myself to speak. “Father isn’t ashamed of you, Eri—”

“ Shut it .”

Her fists clenched, her glare cutting like ice.

“Do you even know what it means to be an Empath?” she demanded. “I don’t just sense emotions—I feel them. I absorb them. I live with them. Every. Single. Second. Do you have any idea how exhausting it is to carry sadness that isn’t yours? To feel someone else’s anger burn inside your chest?”

I fell silent.

She let out a ragged breath, her shoulders trembling.

“I’ve felt it all, Tadashi. Your stress. Your doubts. Your fear that you’re not good enough. The exhaustion. The pain you never talk about.” She exhaled sharply. “I’ve carried your burdens. And Father’s? He doesn’t even see me. He never has. Everything goes to you. His pride. His attention. His plans. You’re the heir. I’m just… nothing .”

Her words slammed into me, shattering the fragile walls I had built around my own guilt.

I thought of the years I had spent silently as the clan heir. The pressure. The sleepless nights. The constant battle with my own inadequacy.

I thought I had carried it for them. For her.

But I hadn’t seen her struggle. I hadn’t noticed how every crack in my own facade must have weighed heavier on her.

She wasn’t just drowning in her own pain. She was drowning in mine, too.

“Eri,” I whispered, my voice barely audible. “I’m sorry.”

Her expression softened, just for a second. But she quickly shook her head and stepped back.

“The more I care about someone, the harder it becomes to shut them out,” she said quietly. “Your emotions. Father’s. Raito’s. It’s too much. They’re killing me.” She swallowed hard. “I need time. Time to quiet this. To control it. If I stay…” Her voice wavered. “If I stay, I’ll drown , Tadashi. And I don’t think I’ll ever come back up. Don’t you understand? I have to go.”

I reached for her hand.

She flinched.

Pulled away.

“Eri, wait! Please,” I pleaded, my voice breaking.

“Go home, Tadashi,” Shiro said, her voice razor-thin. She didn’t stop walking, her back rigid. “Father must be furious by now. You’re supposed to be at the evaluation. How did it go? Did you pass? The clan needs you.”

Her words hit like a blade to my chest.

The evaluation.

The thought sliced through me, fresh as if I’d only just realized it. The most important day of my life—the day everything I’d trained for had come to a head. Father waiting in the grand hall, the elders seated in judgment, the weight of the clan pressing on my every movement. And I wasn’t there.

I’d abandoned it all the moment I’d read her letter.

I stared at the ground, my throat dry, unable to answer. She must’ve sensed my guilt because she suddenly stopped walking, turned, and grabbed my arm.

Pain shot through me instantly as her fingers pressed against the gash beneath my sleeve, still tender from the mission days ago. I couldn’t hide my wince.

“You didn’t go.” Her voice dropped to a whisper, disbelief coating every word.

I hesitated.

Her mouth twisted into a frown. “You just left?”

“I… I forgot something,” I admitted quietly. My voice barely carried over the wind. “Went home to get it. That’s when I saw your letter.”

She froze, her eyes narrowing. “You came after me at once.” Her tone wasn’t a question—it was part frustration, part disbelief.

I nodded faintly.

Her sigh was sharp. She released my arm but stayed close, her jaw clenched. “You have to go back, Tadashi. Now. They’ll … understand. Maybe—maybe they’ll forgive you.”

I said nothing. Couldn’t. The truth twisted in my chest. Her “maybe” couldn’t touch reality. If I let Shiro go, if I kept her runaway a secret, what was I going to tell them? Sorry Father, I woke up late, forgot what day it was, and went strolling around the city?

No. I had no excuse either way.

What I had done was unforgivable—not just disobedience but dereliction of duty. The elders would not allow this disrespect to go unpunished. Father wouldn’t, either. Sadi would revel in my failure, waiting for the moment they named him my replacement.

And yet.

None of it mattered—not as much as her.

“I’m not going back,” I said, quiet but firm. “You need someone to teach you how to control your powers, and I’m not letting you face this alone.”

Shiro’s jaw tightened. Her expression hardened. “I don’t need you. I can take care of myself.”

For a split second, her words felt like a sharp punch to the ribs. I took a shaky breath.

“I know,” I said softly. “But I need to know where you’re going, Eri. Because wherever it is… I’ll want to visit my sister.”

Her breaths came fast, anger thickening the air. “Eri has no siblings,” she hissed bitterly. “Eri was abandoned—left in an orphanage to rot. She has no brother. No family. No ties. Understand?”

She didn’t want me to be her brother anymore .

It shouldn’t have hurt as much as it did, but the rejection stung. I swallowed it all down and forced a small, brittle smile.

“It’s okay,” I whispered, my voice hoarse, “I’ll just be Eri’s friend.”

She didn’t respond. The silence between us stretched painfully. Without saying another word, she turned away, leaving me to follow her in quiet despair.

As if things couldn’t get worse, this journey turned into more than just a family dispute.

While resting in a tavern of a quiet town later, we were ambushed. Smoke bombs exploded, filling the room with a noxious gas that burned my lungs. My vision blurred; I stumbled, disoriented, before everything went black.

I woke groggy and sick, my lungs heavy with the remnants of the gas. My head throbbed, and my hands were bound tightly behind my back. Bars of steel surrounded me. I was locked in the back of a train carriage alongside other frightened captives.

My stomach sank.

We had been captured by human traffickers.

I didn’t see Shiro.

Part of me felt relieved—if I couldn’t see her, maybe she had managed to escape. But the other part of me was clawed apart by anxiety. We’d been sitting together when they drugged us, hadn’t we?

I tugged against the bindings holding my wrists. Too tight. My hands throbbed, and my head was still foggy from whatever drug they’d used. Around me, the other captives were slumped in defeated silence, and the air in the carriage felt oppressive under the watchful eyes of three armed guards.

One of the guards, a man with a red bandana tied over his greasy hair, suddenly sauntered in, his gaze sweeping across the room. His eyes landed on me—and his lips curled into a smirk.

“Ah, the fancy one,” Bandana sneered. “Get up.”

Before I could react, he grabbed me roughly by the shirt, practically yanking me off the ground. My movements were sluggish; the drug was still dulling my senses. Desperate, I attempted to kick him, but my coordination failed, and my foot missed its target entirely.

He laughed—loud and cruel—before dragging me out of the carriage. My body was heavy, my head spinning with a nauseating combination of dizziness and panic.

“Come on, don’t play dead now,” Bandana taunted as we moved.

I stumbled along, forced to keep up as he dragged me further through the train. Outside, the sound of the wheels clattering over the tracks echoed like a drumbeat of doom. The carriages here were filled with stolen goods—boxes and crates stacked high—though I couldn’t tell what was inside. I counted four carriages in total, including the one crammed with the other captives.

Bandana pulled me into the last carriage, where two other men lounged lazily with cruel smirks. One of them was wiry and tall, with a thick mustache and rectangular glasses perched on his nose. His name, I later learned, was Minho.

“Junji, no need to waste time on that runt,” Minho said with a laugh. “Skinny as a twig. What’s he supposed to do?”

“Came into my carriage, acting like he had guts,” Bandana—apparently Junji—spat. “Tried to kick me, he did. Thought little ol’ Junji was scared of some boy in silk robes.”

My stomach twisted. My clothes. Of course, the fine silk fabric of my clan attire stuck out.

Without waiting for a response from Minho, Junji slammed his fist into my face. Pain flared hot and sharp in my jaw, knocking me to the ground. I groaned, trying to scramble away, but Junji grabbed me by the arm and punched me in the stomach with enough force to make me gag.

“Still feel fancy, little prince?” he spat.

“Enough, Junji,” Minho interjected. “The kid’s not going anywhere. Let’s figure out who we’re dealing with first.”

Junji shoved me backward, his laugh grating. Minho stepped forward, yanking me up by my injured arm. My breath hissed through gritted teeth as fresh pain shot through the old wound.

“You’re a long way from where you belong, aren’t you?” Minho asked mockingly, taking in my fine robes. “What fool wanders into Heinna Forest wearing silk like that? Running away from home with your girlfriend, hmm? What’s your name, prince?”

I clenched my teeth, refusing to answer. His impatience grew until he slapped me hard across the face.

“Answer me!” Minho barked. “Or should I drag that little girlfriend of yours in here?!”

Panic surged through me, and I shook my head frantically, but it was too late. Minho shouted for someone to fetch her.

Moments later, they dragged her in.

Shiro looked furious. Her clothes were disheveled, her hands tied behind her, and blood trickled from the corner of her lip. My heart sank.

Minho grabbed a fistful of my hair, forcing my head up roughly so I couldn’t look away.

“Now stay quiet,” he hissed, his voice low and sinister. “Unless you want me to hurt her right here in front of you.”

He released me and turned his attention to Shiro, circling her like a vulture.

“Hmm… You don’t dress as fancy as your boyfriend here, but you’re carrying a good stash of money, aren’t you, sweetheart?” he said, voice dripping with mockery. “Is it his money? Were you planning to take all of it and run off, leaving him behind?”

Shiro’s fiery glare met his as she boldly fired back, “Yes. It’s his life savings. But I wasn’t planning on leaving him.”

Minho’s laugh was a sharp bark. “Aww, how sweet! Romantic escapees. Tell me, darling, what’s your name? Are your parents rich?”

Shiro shrugged. “My name is Akarui,” she lied smoothly. “My father owns a bakery.”

“And your boyfriend?” Minho pressed.

She glanced at me, her expression unreadable. Then she moved her attention back to Minho. “You plan to ransom us?” she asked casually.

Minho smirked. “Depends on orders from my boss. This idiot didn’t have any identification on him, but judging by his clothes, I’d bet his family’s loaded. Question is, what’s he worth alive, hmm? Enough not to break his arm just yet?”

“No,” Shiro said flatly. “If they know you’ve hurt him, his clan will hunt you and your entire group to the ends of the earth. And they’ll make sure every single one of you suffers before you die. So don’t touch him.”

Minho’s laugh deepened. “Oh, feisty. I see why this idiot is smitten with you.”

He shoved Shiro down roughly to sit beside me. Then he yanked me backward, seating me behind her so we were back-to-back.

“That’s enough chit-chat,” he growled. “Sit still, and don’t make any noise.”

Junji knelt to tie our feet together in front of us, ensuring we couldn’t move.

“Are you okay, Akarui?” I whispered over my shoulder, turning slightly to glimpse her.

Before I could hear her response, a burst of searing pain exploded in my head. Stars danced in my blurred vision as I realized Junji had struck me with a wooden stick.

“Keep quiet, or I’ll gag you,” Junji growled.

I nodded quickly, forcing myself to stay still despite the pounding ache in my skull.

Don’t think about the pain. Shiro can feel you. Block it out. It isn’t important. Just keep her safe.

As Junji sauntered back toward Minho, I felt Shiro fumbling behind me. Slowly, carefully, her bound hands reached mine. Her fingers pressed lightly against my palm, tracing letters one by one.

I’m okay.

Relief washed over me.

Her fingers moved again, spelling out another message: The train will pass the Doori River. That’s our chance to escape.
