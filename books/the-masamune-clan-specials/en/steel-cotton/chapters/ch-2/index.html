Chapter 2

← Back to The Masamune Clan: Specials [EN]

M aster Oni kept the leash short as he led me down the silent corridor.

Akito’s mansion felt wrong compared to the rest of the compound—too quiet, too hollow. The paper doors were all closed, the lamps unlit, and the air was heavy with dust and something sharper underneath: ink, old sake, and the faint, metallic tang of blood that had been scrubbed but not entirely erased.

We stopped before a set of double doors. Oni gave the chain a sharp tug.

“Kneel,” he said.

I sank onto the polished wood, head bowed, the rope still biting my wrists behind my back. The doors slid open with a clean, oiled whisper.

“Akito-sama,” Oni said smoothly. “I have brought the boy, as requested.”

“You may enter,” came the reply.

Akito Masamune’s voice was not loud. It didn’t need to be. Each word was clipped and precise, as if he were conserving energy for something else.

Oni stepped forward, dragging me the last half-step over the threshold.

Akito sat on a low cushion by a lacquered table, scrolls stacked neatly to one side. His robes were immaculate—dark brown with a subtle geometric pattern, a lighter haori thrown over his shoulders. His hair was tied back with a plain cord. His face, as always, looked carved from fine stone: long, narrow, with sharp cheekbones and thin lips that never quite relaxed.

His eyes, when they settled on me, were colder than the winter outside.

“So,” he said, inspecting me. “This is the… asset Elder Low spoke of.”

“Just so,” Oni said. “This slave is currently six months into his atonement mission. Pain tolerance and obedience are high. The collar ensures compliance.”

Akito’s gaze lingered on the metal at my throat. “Explain.”

Oni’s tone shifted into his merchant’s cadence, proud and smooth. “The collar carries several seals, Akito-sama. A disguise seal masking his identity from all but designated viewers—yourself, Elder Low, Clan Head Masada, and me. A restraint seal that delivers electrical punishment on command. There are also conditional bindings: if he attempts to flee or directly disobey a clear order from his current master, the collar will activate automatically.”

Akito extended one hand. “Show me.”

“Of course.”

Oni moved behind me. I heard the soft rustle of his sleeve, then felt his fingers brush the side of the collar. Power flared, cold and quick.

The zap hit like a swarm of hornets burrowing into my muscles.

My back arched despite the bindings, a strangled breath ripping from my lungs before I could stop it. Every nerve lit up in a bright, buzzing agony that numbed the very edges of itself. My fingers clenched uselessly against the rope; my vision went white at the edges.

Then it was gone, leaving behind a trembling, hollow ache and the taste of bile in my mouth.

“Moderate level,” Oni said, as if describing the weather. “He can withstand significantly higher.”

“Hmm.” Akito’s eyes had not left my face. I could feel his scrutiny, clinical and unblinking. “May I?”

“Please.”

I heard movement as Oni stepped aside. Akito rose, the soft brush of his hakama against the tatami somehow sharper than the sound of the earlier shock.

He came to stand directly before me.

“Look up,” he said.

I obeyed, lifting my gaze to his throat, careful not to meet his eyes directly.

He reached down, fingers cool and dry as they curled under my jaw, tilting my head to one side. His touch was not rough like Master Oni’s or Master Kenta’s. It was detached, the way a physician might examine a new tool.

“The marks on his neck?” he asked.

“From prior punishments,” Oni replied. “The collar leaves no permanent scars if managed correctly.”

“Mm.” Akito’s thumb pressed lightly along the bruise. Pain bloomed, small and sharp.

His fingers slipped from my jaw to the collar itself. Power gathered—denser and more refined than Oni’s.

The shock that followed was smaller than Oni’s demonstration, but more focused, like a needle of lightning driven straight into the base of my skull. My vision flared, then snapped tight. My stomach lurched; my shoulders jerked against the bindings.

I didn’t make a sound.

Akito watched closely, then let go. “Responsive,” he said. “Your craftsmanship is… adequate, Oni.”

“You honor me, Akito-sama,” Oni said, bowing. I could almost hear the smile in his voice.

Akito returned to his cushion, smooth and unhurried. “You may leave him,” he said. “I will note any deficiencies and report them directly to Clan Head Masada and Elder Low.”

“I am at your service,” Oni murmured. He gave the leash one last sharp tug—more a reminder than a necessity—then dropped it, bowed toward Akito, and swept out of the room.

The doors slid shut.

Silence pressed in, thick as damp wool.

I stayed kneeling, head bowed, feeling the faint tremors still rippling through my muscles. The leash lay slack against my chest, the chain coiled on the tatami.

“Slave,” Akito said.

The word cut deeper the shocks.

“Yes, Master,” I answered.

“You will address me as ‘Master’ at all times,” he said. “You have no other name in this house. You will work in silence. You are not to utter any sound unless I or Sadi give you explicit permission. You will not speak, you will not question, you will not answer anyone—not the neighbors, not any visitor, not even the elders—unless I order you to. Do you understand?”

“Yes, Master.”

The blow came faster than I could brace.

His hand cracked across my face, the force of the slap snapping my head to the side. My teeth clicked painfully together; copper flooded my mouth where I’d bitten my cheek.

“Rule one,” Akito said, voice still calm. “You will obey precisely. When I say you will not utter any sound unless I grant permission, that includes answering questions. You will wait until I say: ‘Speak.’ The fact that you failed such a simple instruction…” He let the sentence trail off with a soft, derisive exhale. “Master Oni’s training is clearly overrated.”

I lowered my forehead to the floor. Blood tickled at the corner of my lip. “This unworthy one—”

The collar flared, a quick, stinging zap. Not enough to make me convulse, just enough to cut the words off in my throat. My breath hitched.

Akito’s silence was heavy, and when he spoke again, a thread of irritation had woven into his calm. “Every mistake you make here,” he said, “every failure, every tardy task, I will record. And at the end of your assignment, I will deliver that report personally to Masada.”

The name landed like a stone in my gut.

He let the implications hang between us for a beat.

“Do you understand,” he said again, “Slave?”

I met the tatami with my forehead once, a small, controlled bow.

He waited, clearly expecting more. When nothing came, the faint crease in his brow smoothed.

“Good. At least you can learn.”

Paper rustled. He picked up a bamboo slip, running his eyes down it.

“Due to a recent breach of security, I dismissed the servants several months ago,” he said. “Their replacements have not yet been selected. This house is… beneath my standards.” A faint curl of disgust touched his lips. “You will correct that.”

He began to read.

“Entry hall: sweep, scrub the threshold stones, polish the door runners. Main corridor: dust the beams, wipe all shōji frames, remove any cobwebs. Guest rooms: air the futons, beat the bedding, wipe the tatami with vinegar water, polish the metal fittings. Storage rooms: inventory loose items, arrange them properly, sweep. Kitchen: scour the hearth, clean the pots, scrub the floor. Washroom: clean the tub, replenish firewood and water. Garden paths: clear snow and debris from the stepping stones. Stables: no, those are empty for now.”

The list went on. Each task stacked on the last until it felt less like chores and more like a mountain.

A whole household’s worth of work, and only one pair of hands.

He rolled the slip closed. “All of it is to be completed before dawn,” he said. “If I find a single area neglected, I will assume willful disobedience.”

My ribs ached with each breath. My arms throbbed from where the rope had held them too long. I had not truly rested since Master Kenta’s shop. The thought of scrubbing floors and carrying water through the day made my vision sway.

But I pressed my forehead more firmly to the floor.

“As for boundaries,” Akito continued, rising, “you are forbidden to enter my study or my private room unless I call you or if there is an emergency. If I find you there uninvited, I will consider it a violation of house security.”

He stepped around me. I heard the faint click of something against the collar, felt his power gather again.

“One more thing,” he murmured.

The seal he laid onto the collar this time felt different—smaller, more intricate. Threads of energy sank into the metal, then into my skin, like a net of fine wires embedding themselves.

“You will not fall asleep on the job,” he said. “This auxiliary seal will deliver low-grade shocks at irregular intervals. A reminder. Motivation.” The corner of his mouth lifted fractionally. “You should be grateful. It will make you more efficient.”

I forced my jaw to unclench and simply bowed again, silently. Akito made a soft, satisfied sound. “I have a meeting in town. I expect the work to be done when I return.”

He took a step, then paused. “When you finish, you will wait in the kitchen. Kneeling, in silence. Do not move from there unless called.”

His sandals whispered over the tatami. The door slid open, then closed.

For a moment I stayed kneeling, eyes closed, counting my breaths until the echo of his presence faded from the room. Then, I pushed myself slowly to my feet.

My wrists burned where the rope had been. I flexed my fingers, testing them, before reaching for the loose end of the binding. Suta had left it intentionally easy to undo; they wanted work from me, after all, not a trussed ornament.

The rope fell away. Pins and needles shot up my forearms.

I found the cleaning tools stacked haphazardly in a side cupboard off the kitchen. The disarray told me how long the house had gone without staff: buckets nested crookedly, brooms with broken bristles, rags stiff with old grease.

I started with the entry hall.

Water was heavy. The bucket’s wooden handle bit into my fingers as I carried it—again and again—from the well in the rear courtyard. Each trip sent a fresh ache through my ribs. Each time I bent to scrub, my back burned where the whip marks split and wept under the thin cloth of my shirt.

The new seal in the collar did its work.

Just as my movements began to dull, just as my mind floated toward exhaustion, a small, sharp shock would nip at my throat or the base of my skull. Not enough to make me cry out. Enough to jolt my heart and steal the comfort of numbness.

I moved from stones to tatami, from wood to paper frames. Dust clung to my clothes, my hair, and the inside of my nose. My hands reddened, the skin softening and then roughening as the water leeched the oils away.

Outside, the winter sky darkened from steel to ink.

At some point my world narrowed to the next stroke of the rag, the next squeak of the bucket, the next faint, unpredictable snap of current along my nerves. My body shook even when the collar was quiet.

By the time I put the last bucket away and wrung out the final rag, my arms felt like they belonged to someone else. My knees threatened to fold.

The house looked different, though. The entry stones gleamed faintly. The corridors smelled of vinegar and wet wood instead of dust. The kitchen hearth was scraped clean and stacked with fresh wood. It was not perfect—no single person could do the work of an entire staff in one day—but it was as close as I could get without collapsing.

I shuffled to the center of the kitchen and sank to my knees.

The cold from the floor seeped up through my thin clothes. My back throbbed with each breath. My head felt light, detached, as if it might float away if not for the weight of the collar.

I clasped my hands loosely in my lap and lowered my gaze to the clean, worn boards.

The collar nipped me once when my eyes slipped closed. I gritted my teeth and focused on the surrounding sounds instead: the faint crack and hiss of embers dying in the stove, the whisper of wind outside the shutters, the distant drip of water in the pipes.

Time blurred.

At some point, the quiet snapped.

The front door slid open hard enough that I heard it from the kitchen—a sharp thud of wood against its frame. Cold air and noise tumbled in together.

Bootsteps. Two sets, uneven. One heavy, controlled. The other… dragging.

A muffled curse. Someone hit a wall with a dull thud. A pained sound, bitten off.

Every muscle in my body went tight.

Order: Wait in the kitchen. Do not move unless called.

The command was a cold weight in my mind, reinforced by the primary seal on my collar. It judged intent. To disobey was to invite a shock that would drop me where I stood.

Clan training: A disturbance at a clan official’s residence after dark is a potential threat.

I swallowed. My heart hammered, each beat sending a throb through my ribs. The collar lay cold and still against my neck.

If it was a thief, a spy, an assassin, and I did nothing…

If it was Akito returning with trouble, and I left my post…

My hands shook. I pressed them flat to my thighs until the tremors steadied.

Akito had not forbidden me from leaving the kitchen, I told myself. He had forbidden me from moving unless called. But if there was an emergency or an immediate threat to clan security, the higher duty was to investigate.

A body hit the floor down the corridor with a meaty thump. This time, the sound that followed was not quite stifled: a hoarse, familiar voice, rough with pain.

“—no… please—”

Sadi.

The decision made itself.

I rose, every movement careful, and padded toward the sound.

The collar did not react at first. Halfway down the dark corridor, it gave me a small, warning sting. I paused, muscles twitching.

The seal’s logic in my collar was absolute, but it wasn’t omniscient. It read my conscious purpose. So I closed my eyes and shaped my thoughts. Emergency. Threat to the household. Duty to the clan . I repeated it like a mantra, burying my fear beneath the cold, clean imperative of protocol. Then I moved again. Another few steps. No shock. I took a shaky breath.

The light in Akito’s study leaked through a narrow gap where the shōji was not fully closed. I slipped to the side of the door, bare feet silent on the boards, and peered through the narrow wedge.

It wasn’t an intruder.

Akito stood in the center of the room, his haori off, sleeves tied back. His hair had come slightly loose from its cord. Sadi knelt on the floor before him, slumped, one arm wrapped around his ribs. His mission blacks were torn and stained dark over one shoulder and thigh. Blood had soaked through the cloth. His face was bruised, his lip split, and one eye was already swelling shut.

“So,” Akito said, his voice very soft. “You failed.”

Sadi coughed, a wet, painful sound. “Target… escaped. I can still—”

“You can do nothing,” Akito snapped. It was the first real heat I’d heard in his voice all day. “You compromised the operation. You exposed yourself. You allowed witnesses to live. Do you understand how much effort went into arranging that contact?”

“I—I’ll fix it,” Sadi said quickly, curling in on himself as if expecting another blow. “Give me another chance, Father. I’ll go back out tonight. I won’t come home until—”

The kick caught him under the ribs, hard enough to lift him from the floor.

He folded around it with a strangled wheeze. I flinched in the shadows, my own ribs sparking with sympathetic pain.

Akito paced, hands clasped behind his back. “You are Masada’s deputy heir,” he said. “You bear my blood. Yet you fail at the simplest of assignments. Again.” He stopped, looking down at Sadi with cool contempt. “You disgust me.”

Sadi forced himself onto his hands and knees, coughing, each breath a sharp, ragged drag. “I—won’t—fail you—again—”

“Won’t you?” Akito knelt suddenly, one hand snapping out to grip the back of Sadi’s neck, forcing his face toward the floor.

Sadi went still, every line in his body going rigid.

I saw why when Akito’s other hand moved lower, shoving Sadi’s torn shirt up to expose the small of his back. Right at the base of his spine, burned into the skin like a brand, was a seal mark—small, intricate, dark against pale flesh. I could only see part of it from my angle, but the pattern made my stomach clench: interlocking circles, binding lines, the unmistakable flow of a compulsion array.

Akito didn’t look angry; he looked disappointed, which was far worse. He looked at Sadi the way a master swordsmith looks at a blade that has warped in the forge.

“A blade that cannot hold its edge under pressure is merely scrap metal, Sadi,” Akito said softly, his fingers hovering near the brand. “I am not being cruel; I am being thorough. If you cannot survive the fire I build for you here, you will never survive the world outside.”

He looked toward the door. “Even waste like Tadashi has a purpose. He is the whetstone. Use his failure to sharpen your resolve. If you cannot even be superior to a broken slave, then you are of no use to this clan.”

Akito’s fingers pressed, two of them settling precisely over the center sigil.

“Let me remind you,” he murmured, “what happens when you fail me.”

“No—” Sadi’s voice strangled into a single syllable. “Fath—”

Akito’s energy signature flared.

The seal ignited.

Sadi’s scream tore through the room, sharp and high and raw. His back arched violently, muscles seizing, fingers clawing uselessly at the tatami. His heels drummed against the floor as if trying to escape his own skin.

The noise seemed to vibrate through the collar around my neck. It buzzed abruptly, the sharp jolt making me clamp a hand over my own throat. My vision spotted black. Every instinct screamed at me to move, to do something.

But my legs would not obey.

Even if I stepped in—what then? Interpose myself between Akito and his son? Between a father and the discipline of his heir? Between a clan elder and his own blood?

The collar’s presence loomed large and invisible. There wasn’t a threat to the household, and Akito’s study was a restricted area for me. The longer I stood in that corridor, disobeying the order to wait in the kitchen, the more I expected its bite to turn from a nip to a full, skull-shaking shock.

Akito kept his fingers on Sadi’s seal for a breath longer, two, three—an eternity of shrieks.

Then, he released it.

Sadi collapsed like a puppet with its strings cut, limbs twitching weakly. Foam traced the corner of his mouth. His eyes rolled half-open, unfocused, then slid shut as his chest heaved.

“Pathetic,” Akito said, standing. He wiped his hand fastidiously on a hank of Sadi’s torn sleeve, as if discarding some filth. “Useless. Sometimes I wonder whether it is worth keeping you at all.”

He stepped over Sadi’s body without looking down, walked to the door on the far side of the study—his private room—and disappeared inside. The shōji slid closed behind him with careful, quiet precision.

Silence rushed back in, loud in my ears.

I waited, counting heartbeats. Ten. Twenty. Thirty. No sound came from behind the inner door. No flicker of movement.

The collar lay against my neck like a coiled snake, quiet. I gathered my thoughts. The son of my master was wounded. I didn’t mean to disobey. Hoping that it would be enough to stave off the collar’s strike, I moved.

Crossing the threshold felt like stepping into a forbidden shrine. The study smelled of ink, paper, and the harsh tang of burned power—the scent of active seals. Scrolls lined the walls in neat racks. The lamp on the desk still burned, its light pooling gold over an open report. I stepped around it, barefoot on tatami, and knelt beside Sadi.

He lay curled awkwardly on his side, one hand still half-clenched under his cheek. His breathing was shallow but steady, his face slack in unconsciousness. Sweat matted his hair to his temples.

“Forgive me,” I whispered as I lifted the torn edge of his shirt, the words barely air. The collar gave a sharp, warning zap—a stinging reminder of Akito’s first rule. I flinched, biting down on my tongue.

The seal at the base of his spine was clearer now, the skin around it reddened and slightly raised from recent activation. It wasn’t like the control array carved into my collar—those were metal-bound, adjustable. This was a direct brand upon flesh.

My training with seals, limited as it was, was enough to recognize the structure.

A slavery array.

Not the simple kind used for temporary restraints or short-term commands. This was deeper, multi-layered: obedience, pain induction, loyalty enforcement, likely even memory redaction if certain conditions were met. The lines of it spiraled inward, anchoring around a central character.

My fingers hovered a hair’s breadth above the mark, not quite touching as I sent the faintest thread of energy toward it.

The array quivered in response, humming like a plucked string. Information fluttered back along the line—a whisper of its bindings, the signature of the one who held the other end.

Master.

The sigil that formed at the core of the array was precise, unmistakable.

Akito’s personal mark.

My hand dropped to my thigh.

My own collar prickled in sympathy. My heart hammered against my ribs, the ache in my side forgotten for a moment beneath a different kind of shock.

All this time, Akito had not just been training his son harshly.

He had enslaved him.

Not metaphorically. Not in the way the elders liked to say we all belonged to the clan. Literally. A full obedience seal, keyed to his energy signature, burned into his own child’s flesh.

I stared at the mark at the base of Sadi’s spine, the delicate cruelty of its lines, and for the first time since this mission began, the pain in my body and the cold in my bones felt far away.

Akito Masamune was the master of his own son’s slavery.
