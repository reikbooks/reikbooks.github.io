Hidden in Silk

Book 3 of The Masamune Clan: Specials

Some roles are meant to deceive. Some stages are built to kill.As the heir of the Fuma clan, Saki is assigned a solo mission to locate a noble’s missing daughter. Disguised in silk as an entertainer, she moves through whispered rumors and carefully guarded secrets, knowing that if her cover is torn, the clan will deny all ties to her.Her mission grows far more complicated when she encounters Tadashi Masamune, the heir of a rival clan, sent for reasons he refuses to share. On a stage woven from silk and secrets, can Saki afford to trust him at all?***

T he incense in the Fuma clan’s council room had burned down to a thin coil of ash, but the sweet and heavy smoke still clung to the air; the spiced scent of the year-end blend, used only in these final, darkening days.

Saki shifted on her cushion, resisting the urge to tap her fingers on the low table. Around her, the team leaders sat in two neat rows, dark uniforms and darker expressions, all eyes on the map spread out between them. Elder Nobumasa, who represented her father at these weekly briefings, sat on the dais.

“…the northern patrol will rotate with Captain Daichi’s unit at dawn,” Koji was saying, his voice as even and unhurried as ever. “Any objections?”

Murmurs of assent. No one objected to Koji. The head of security always spoke like he had already weighed every possible argument.

Saki straightened her back and scanned the faces: weathered, scarred, bored, alert. A few of the older captains still avoided meeting her gaze, as if acknowledging the clan head’s daughter at a strategy table made them nervous.

Or maybe they just don’t want to see the kid who “only got here because of her father.”

The thought tasted bitter. She swallowed it.

Koji slid a wooden tablet aside and drew out another, its lacquered surface catching the candlelight. “Next. A request from Lord Fuku of the Western Court.”

The change in the room was subtle but instant. Backs straightened. Someone scoffed under their breath.

“Another noble wanting us to find a misplaced trinket?” muttered Rento, the captain to Saki’s right. “Maybe his favorite sake cup ran away.”

A few of the men chuckled.

“Not this time,” Koji said. His gaze flicked briefly to Saki before returning to the tablet. “Lord Fuku’s daughter has gone missing.”

A hush settled. Even the crackle of the brazier seemed to quiet.

Saki leaned forward despite herself. Missing daughters were always a bad omen. In this kingdom, powerful men didn’t simply misplace their girls. They sold them, hid them, or killed them.

“What do we know?” she asked.

Several heads turned toward her. She kept her face carefully neutral, as if she wasn’t thrilled just to be the first to speak.

Koji didn’t rebuke her for interrupting. He rarely did. “Her name is Kiku. Seventeen. Married last year to General Guhdo.”

That name dropped into the room like a stone into a still pond.

General Guhdo.

Even Saki, who’d never seen the man, knew the stories. The war hero who crushed rebellions with one hand and drank with the other. A man whose temper was as famous as his sword arm. Men who crossed him vanished. Servants who angered him were found at the bottom of wells.

“Lord Fuku says his daughter wrote faithfully every week after her marriage,” Koji continued. “Letters ceased three months ago. No messenger. No explanation.”

“Maybe she’s simply busy playing dutiful wife to a general,” Rento said lightly, though there was a crease between his brows. “Or maybe Guhdo forbade her from writing to Daddy.”

“Lord Fuku believes otherwise,” Koji replied. “He claims Kiku hinted, in small ways, that she was…unhappy. Afraid.” His eyes lifted. “He wants answers. Quietly.”

Of course he does. A noble can’t just accuse the king’s favorite general of harming his daughter.

Koji set the tablet down. “The king has not ordered an inquiry. There will be no official search. If we act, we do so in shadow. If discovered, we will be disavowed.”

The words hung there. Standard for delicate missions, but hearing them always made Saki’s skin prickle. Fail, and you didn’t just die—you died as a criminal.

“We’ll need an infiltration,” Hana said from the left row. She was one of the few female captains. “Someone inside the mansion.”

At nineteen, she had the effortless poise of a courtesan and the killing speed of a veteran assassin. Her hair flowed like black ink down her back, glossy, pinned with a single red camellia. The faintest hint of rouge colored her lips and the corners of her eyes.

Her uniform was regulation… technically. The top had been adjusted to fit tighter along the waist, the neckline angled just low enough to suggest without offending propriety. She wore it like armor, every seam a calculated weapon.

When Hana’s gaze met Saki’s, her painted mouth curved. Not quite a smile. Not yet.

“General Guhdo is hosting a celebration three nights from now,” Koji said. “A victory feast for his returning troops. Musicians, dancers, entertainers. Extra staff brought in from the city.” He looked directly at Saki. “Saki, you’ll go solo.”

Saki’s pulse jumped. A solo infiltration into General Guhdo’s estate. No brothers hovering at her back. No senior captain “coincidentally” assigned to the same mission.

“You will first visit Lord Fuku for details. He may have letters, small clues, anything that will help you recognize his daughter’s handwriting, scent, habits. Then you will attend General Guhdo’s feast as an entertainer.”

Warm satisfaction flared in her chest.

“And why,” Hana said smoothly, “is this being given to the princess?”

The title dropped like a pebble in a still pond: small, but the ripples spread.

Hana’s tone wasn’t overtly rude. The venom lay in the emphasis, the tiny uplift of chin that suggested a difference between princesses and professionals.

Koji’s face did not change. “Do you have a question regarding the assignment, Hana?”

Hana’s lips pursed, hands folded demurely upon her lap. Her sleeves slid back just enough to reveal the pale line of her wrist and the small jade charm that hung there. Even that had been chosen to catch the eye.

“Head of Security, with respect, Princess Saki is not trained in the arts of entertainment.”

Saki felt every gaze in the room shift, like arrows turning toward a target.

“I can blend in,” she said quickly, her shoulders tense. “I’ve done infiltration before.”

“As a courier, a servant, a guard,” Hana said. “You’re good at disappearing in plain sight, yes. But this is different. Infiltration as an entertainer, or a concubine, requires certain… assets. An understanding of men’s desires. A way of walking and speaking that encourages them to underestimate you. It is not only a matter of combat.”

“Saki is aware of that,” Koji said.

“But is she capable of that? Especially with someone like Guhdo?”

Silence.

Hana’s smile, Saki thought savagely, was the worst part. The question wore a cloak of concern, as if Hana were merely pointing out an unfortunate practical truth.

“We all know Princess Saki is strong,” Hana continued. “Her skills with the naginata, with the kakute? Impressive.” A few men nodded reflexively.

Hana let the acknowledgment linger an instant too long. “But the General is not looking for a sparring partner. He wants a flower. If his suspicions are raised, if he senses that his ‘entertainer’ is truly a soldier, she will never get close enough to find the Lady. The mission will fail before it begins.”

Her gaze dipped, deliberately, to Saki’s bare shoulders and sturdy arms, then back up to her plain, unpainted face.

“A flower,” Hana said gently, “must at least look like a flower.”

Heat flared in Saki’s cheeks.

She became acutely aware of the way her training top flattened what little curve she had. Of her tanned skin, the faint rope-scar along her left forearm from a childhood climbing accident, the calluses that had never fully faded from her palms. Beside Hana’s willow-slim perfection, she felt like a carved block of wood.

Not beautiful. Not delicate. Not the kind of girl who slipped into a room and made men’s eyes go soft.

“And of course,” Hana added, her voice mild, “there is the question of… impartiality. Sending the clan leader’s daughter alone into a Mitsurugi vassal’s bedchamber is… brave. Some might say reckless. If anything were to happen to her…”

“The same ‘anything’ that happens to any of us on assignment?” Saki cut in.

Every head turned again, this time toward her.

She hadn’t meant to speak. The words just shoved their way past the tightness in her chest.

She bowed her head hastily toward Koji and Elder Nobumasa. “Forgive my interruption. But if risk is the concern, I wonder why it is always my risk that draws such discussion.”

Hana’s eyes glittered. “Princess, no one doubts your courage. Only your suitability to this particular—”

“You mean my chest is too small,” Saki said flatly. “And my arms are too big.”

Several men coughed. Someone choked on a laugh and turned it into a throat-clear.

Hana’s lips parted in shocked protest. “I would never say something so… crude.”

“But you would imply it until the roof beams rotted,” Saki muttered.

“Saki,” Koji said, and there was enough warning in his tone that she forced herself to breathe.

She steadied her voice. “Security Coordinator, I accept the assignment. My infiltration record at the eastern gambling dens is clean. My seduction rating may not be as high as some,” she did not look at Hana, “but I have completed three missions requiring disguised service roles without detection. If General Guhdo wants a flower, I can be a flower for as many nights as necessary.”

“Princess—” Hana began.

“And as for impartiality,” Saki continued, louder, “our clan’s duty is to the kingdom, not to the comfort of my brothers. I am heir. If I cannot do what every agent here does, then remove my name from the succession and be done with it.”

That landed differently. Even Elder Nobumasa’s narrowed eyes shifted.

Across from her, Hana’s polite mask slipped just enough to show a flash of annoyance. There it is, Saki thought bitterly. You were counting on them to coddle me, so you could step in as the obvious choice.

Koji finally raised a hand.

“Enough.”

The word settled over them like a seal, cutting off further argument.

He turned his gaze on Hana first. “Your concerns are noted. And not without merit. However, the mission parameters are broader than you suggest. Subtlety is only the first layer of defense. There is a high probability this will turn, without warning, into a close-quarters engagement.”

“We are not sending Princess Saki to seduce General Guhdo,” he continued, matter-of-fact. “We are sending her in case we need someone who can fight their way out.”

He inclined his head toward Saki. “If Lady Kiku is being held against her will, if there are hidden dungeons or private guards, if this becomes an extraction under pressure, I want the Fuma heir in that house.”

Hana’s jaw tightened. “If it is combat you want, why send an entertainer at all? Send in a strike team and—”

“And announce our intentions to Guhdo’s force in the north?” Koji’s voice sharpened. He faced Saki fully. “You will visit Lord Fuku tomorrow. His estate is on the western ridge above the lotus pond district. Use the merchant route. No clan colors.”

“It will be done.” Saki bowed.

“Report to the quartermaster for specialized gear. Hana—”

Hana looked up, hope flickering.

“—you will assist with her cover. Basic posture, walk, how to carry trays without looking like you’re about to throw them.”

Laughter rippled around the table. A few of the men didn’t bother to hide it.

Saki’s jaw clenched.

Of course. Funny. The tomboy princess playing at being a flower.

Hana inclined her head, mouth tight. To be made teacher instead of chosen weapon was a small humiliation, but a humiliation nonetheless. “As you command.”

Koji’s gaze swept the room. “Remember: this is off the record. No gossip. No rumors. We do not provoke General Guhdo. We simply… look for a missing girl.”

Koji rolled up the map. “That’s all. Dismissed.”

Elder Nobumasa stood and cleared his throat. “Do not fail this, child,” he said to Saki. “You carry more than your own reputation into that den.”

“Yes, Elder,” Saki replied, bowing low enough her forehead brushed the tatami.

As the meeting broke apart, scrolls rolling shut and men rising in stiff, rustling lines, Saki stayed kneeling a moment longer, letting the buzzing in her ears fade.

“Princess.”

Hana’s voice again. Of course.

Saki rose.

Hana stood at the edge of the table, arms folded, weight tilted elegantly to one hip. Up close, the faint perfume she wore—cherry blossom and something sharper underneath—scraped at Saki’s nerves.

“That was…” Hana’s smile tried to arrange itself into something gracious. It never quite made it. “Bold. Even for you.”

“If you came to tell me my courage is impressive but my breasts are not, you can save your breath,” Saki said. “The hall heard you the first time.”

A flash of temper brightened Hana’s eyes, quickly masked. “You think I spoke from jealousy. Maybe I did. I know this mission’s value. I know what it would mean to succeed under Koji’s eye. Under the elders’ eyes.” Her lips twisted. “But I also know what Lord Guhdo demands of his ‘flowers.’ He tears petals for the fun of it.”

“I’m not a flower,” Saki said. “I’m a blade disguised as one.”

“Let’s hope he doesn’t look too closely at the disguise,” Hana murmured. “We begin after lunch. If you’re late, I’ll add ten minutes to your posture training for every heartbeat you steal from me.”

“Afraid I’ll outshine you?” Saki asked.

Hana’s smile, this time, was thin as a knife. “Afraid you’ll embarrass us.” She paused at the door, glancing back. “A blade pretending to be a flower. Take care it doesn’t cut the hand that holds it.”

She glided away without waiting for a reply, the camellia in her hair bobbing like a drop of blood.

Saki watched her go, jaw clenched so tight it ached.

“Pay attention to her lessons,” Koji said quietly at her shoulder.

She hadn’t heard him approach. That, more than anything, irritated her; she prided herself on noticing movement.

“I’m not an idiot,” she muttered.

“I know.” Koji’s eyes were kinder than his tone. “That’s why you’re going. But being clever will not make your legs move like hers. Let her teach you where you lack, then show Lord Guhdo what he failed to see. I chose you, Saki. Don’t make me regret it.”

He left her then.

She stood alone in the emptying hall, the faint smoke from the brazier curling around the clan banner. Underestimated, coddled, thrown into the fire. It all swirled together until she could no longer tell which stung more.

B y the time Saki reached the family residence, the winter light had thinned to a cold gray-blue. Snow threatened in the air, sharp against her nostrils. Near the main gate, two disciples were already busy hanging the shimekazari —braided straw and bitter orange to ward off bad luck for the coming year.

The Fuma family house perched on a small rise overlooking the main compound, its sliding doors painted with stylized storms. Inside, warmth hit her in a wave: rice steam, grilled fish, the faint tang of soy. Underneath it all was the sharp, clean scent of fresh tatami and citrus oil; her mother had clearly started the year-end scrubbing early.

Her stomach rumbled. The rhythmic thwack-thwack of mochi being pounded in the kitchen echoed down the hall, a sound that usually meant celebration, but tonight only felt like a ticking clock.

She hadn’t realized how hungry the training with Hana had made her until the smell of food drifted down the hall.

Her brothers looked up when she slid the door open.

Jiro, the eldest, lounged at the far side with his usual easy sprawl, chopsticks idle in one hand, hair tied in a loose knot that always seemed on the verge of coming undone. Saito sat straight-backed beside him, his expression as calm and closed as a carved mask, eyes half-lidded but taking in everything. Eiji, the youngest of the three, leaned back on his hands, one leg stretched, grin wide enough to swallow the room.

“Saki!” Eiji waved with his chopsticks, nearly flinging a piece of pickled radish across the table. “We saved you the good spot, near the fish.”

“That’s because you already ate all the good fish,” Jiro said.

“Hey! I only ate what Saito didn’t want.”

Saito calmly picked a bone from his plate and set it aside. “I want peace and quiet,” he said. “You haven’t honored that once.”

“See?” Eiji told Saki. “He doesn’t want fish.”

Despite herself, Saki’s mouth tugged upward. The familiar bickering loosened the tight coil in her chest, if only a little.

She knelt at the open spot, adjusting her sword so it wouldn’t knock against the table. A servant poured her tea without a word.

For a few minutes, the clatter of chopsticks and soft murmur of conversation—Jiro asking about patrol rotations, Eiji complaining about cold training yards—filled the space.

Then Jiro said, too casually, “So. Security meeting today.”

Saki’s hand froze halfway to her bowl. “Mm.”

“I heard Lord Guhdo’s name,” Saito said.

Of course they had heard. Jiro led one of the outer squads; Saito managed internal intelligence. Even if Koji hadn’t briefed them directly, the clan’s rumor channels were efficient.

Eiji leaned forward, eyes bright. “Is it true you’re going in disguised as a geisha? With… with ribbons and everything?”

Saki set her bowl down carefully. “Traveling entertainer,” she corrected. “Not geisha. And yes. Probably ribbons.”

Eiji whistled low. “Our little storm princess, all painted up. The gods themselves will forget their prayers.”

“Eiji,” Saito said warningly.

“What? I’m complimenting her.”

“It’s not the kind of compliment she wants,” Jiro said quietly.

Saki’s chopsticks clicked together. “What kind of compliment do I want, then?”

“The kind that mentions how terrifying you are with a polearm,” Eiji said promptly.

She snorted. “I already know that.”

“That’s what worries me,” Jiro murmured.

She turned to him. “Excuse me?”

He held her gaze, brown eyes serious beneath their usual lazy droop. “Going alone into Lord Guhdo’s house, Saki? As… that.” His jaw tightened around the word. “It’s bad enough when you’re sneaking over roofs. This—”

“Is just another mission,” she said.

“It’s not,” Saito said softly.

Her patience, frayed since Hana’s little performance, snapped another thread.

“Why not?” she demanded. “Because I have to wear a dress? Because I’m supposed to bat my eyelashes instead of kick someone in the ribs? Or because you don’t like the idea of your littlest sister being anywhere near a man’s bedchamber?”

Eiji choked on a mouthful of rice. Jiro shot him a look. Saito’s face didn’t change, but his hand tightened around his cup.

“None of us,” Saito said, “like the idea of you being in a general’s bedchamber. There’s a difference.”

“You go into worse places every week,” Saki said. “Brothels. Gambling dens. Enemy camps.”

“Yes,” Jiro said. “As men.”

The silence after that was thick.

Saki’s throat burned. “So because I was unlucky enough to be born with the wrong body, I should stay home and embroider seals on talismans while you get stabbed in the dark?”

“You’d set the house on fire with your embroidery,” Eiji muttered.

She glared. “Not helping.”

Eiji lifted his free hand in surrender. “Just saying. We all have our talents.”

Jiro rubbed a hand over his face. “That’s not what I meant, Saki.”

“Then what did you mean?”

He met her eyes. “If word gets out that Fuma’s princess is being sold as a flower to Lord Guhdo—”

“I’m not being sold. It’s a cover.”

“Do you think rumor will care about the difference?” Jiro asked. “Our enemies will spin it as weakness. Our allies will question Father’s judgment. The elders will blame Koji, then forbid you from stepping outside the compound for the next decade.”

“Which is exactly what you want,” she snapped. “Me safe and useless and wrapped in paper like a broken seal.”

Saito exhaled slowly. “You are not useless. No one thinks that.”

“They do,” she shot back. “You weren’t in the hall when Hana asked why Koji would send someone as ‘ill-suited’ as me. You didn’t see the faces around the table.”

“Hana,” Eiji said, wrinkling his nose. “That fox. She’s just angry Koji didn’t choose her.”

“Maybe,” Saki said. “But she was also right. I don’t look like an entertainer. I don’t move like one. She was happy to say it out loud because it helped her, but that doesn’t make it untrue.”

Jiro’s brows drew together. “So you’re going to prove her wrong by walking into a snake’s den alone?”

“I’m going to do my duty,” Saki said. “Like you. Like Saito. Like everyone.”

Saito’s voice lowered. “We’re not questioning your duty. Only the terms.”

“I am,” Eiji said. “I think duty is an evil spirit that’s taken all of you and left me alone with my beautiful, carefree heart.”

Jiro flicked a grain of rice at his forehead. It stuck. Eiji went cross-eyed trying to see it.

Saki should have laughed. Normally she would have. Instead, she pushed away from the table so hard her chopsticks clattered.

“Saki,” Jiro said sharply. “Sit.”

“I’m not a dog,” she snapped. “And I’m not a glass bottle you can keep on a shelf. You all want me to be strong until it makes you uncomfortable. Then suddenly I’m fragile goods.”

Eiji’s grin faded. “We don’t think you’re fragile.”

“Then stop treating me like I am.”

“Saki,” Saito said. “We just—”

“—want me to stay alive. I know.” Her hands were shaking. She clenched them into fists. “But if I stay alive by never doing anything worth dying for, what’s the point? You don’t question each other’s missions. You only argue when it’s me.”

“Because you’re our sister,” Jiro said. “Not just a squad leader.”

“And I’m heir to Fuma,” she retorted. “Not just your sister.”

The words hung between them like a drawn blade.

Jiro flinched. Only slightly, but she saw it. Eiji stared at his bowl.

Saito closed his eyes briefly. “We’re saying this mission is too volatile to go in without a safety net. You should have one of us nearby, at least. I’ll talk to Koji—”

“No.” The word burst out. “You will not.”

You’re strong, but not yet. Skilled, but not enough. Trusted, but only within the walls we build around you.

It was the line she heard in a dozen different forms. Something hot and sharp surged up inside her.

Jiro’s gaze sharpened. “Saki—”

“I said no!” She slammed her hand on the table, sauce splattering the lacquered wood. “Every time I get something important, one of you runs to Father or Koji and suddenly there’s a ‘coincidence’ that puts you on my team. Or the mission gets altered. Or I get reassigned. Do you have any idea what that looks like to the others? The clan thinks I can’t go to the latrine without a bodyguard.” She shook her head.

They were afraid of losing her; she knew that. She loved them for it. But love didn’t mean she had to accept the cage they kept trying to build around her.

“If I fail, that’s on me,” she said. “If I succeed, maybe people will finally see something besides Father’s blood when they look at me.”

Without waiting for an answer, she bowed—too quickly to be polite, too shallow to be truly disrespectful—and slid the door open.

“Saki,” Jiro called.

She didn’t turn.

She didn’t trust herself not to cry if she did.

Her room was small by noble standards, large by those of the outer compound. A futon rolled in the corner, a low chest against one wall, a single shelf crowded with jars of herbs and small medical tools Master Toya had allowed her to keep for study. The sliding window looked out over the training yards, now ghostly in the twilight.

She shut the door, sliding the latch into place with more force than necessary.

For a long moment she stood in the center of the room, breathing hard, anger and shame and something sour and hollow all mixing in her gut.

Then she crossed to the corner and unrolled the battered standing mirror. The lacquer on the frame was chipped at the edges, the backing slightly warped, but the glass still held a clear reflection.

She didn’t like this mirror.

She made herself face it anyway.

A girl stared back at her. Not beautiful. Not, she thought, in the way Hana was.

Broad shoulders for her size, arms corded from hours of swinging a naginata. Her chest barely curved beneath the wrapped band that flattened it for training, the cloth sweat-stained from the day’s drills. Her skin was brown from sun and wind, not the pale porcelain prized in courtly tales.

She wasn’t wearing armor or training clothes, just her dark clan uniform—plain hakama, close-fitting sleeveless top. No powder on her skin. No paint on her lips. Her hair, hastily tied back for the meeting, had loosened, strands escaping to frame her face.

Hana’s voice from the council room echoed in her ears.

Blade, not petal.

“Ugly,” she muttered to the empty room. The word tasted foul. “Too rough. Too loud. Too…much.”

Did everyone think like that? Hana. Her brothers. The captains who chuckled when Koji mentioned her cover.

What makes you think you can walk into a hall full of silks and painted lips and not stand out like a boar at a crane dance?

Her gaze dropped to the table.

A flash of yellow broke the monochrome of wood and steel. A small, high-quality pouch lay there, its silk dyed a warm, bright gold, embroidery fine enough to belong in a noble’s sleeve.

Her senbon pouch.

The one she’d left there after the last royal mission, too distracted by new assignments to properly store it with the rest of her gear.

Saki reached for it.

The silk felt smooth under her fingertips, cool at first, then warming quickly.

Memory rose, vivid and sharp.

The royal bracelet mission six months ago. She’d worked with Tadashi, heir of the Masamune clan. The mission was a mess. Bloody, tangled, more complicated than it should have been. She found him in the aftermath, pale and half-conscious, body broken from infiltrating the Koga compound as a slave, the cursed brand burned into his chest. He’d flatlined. They’d dragged him back with desperation.

She’d thought that was it. They’d completed their tasks. They’d both lived. The king had his antique bracelet back. Life had lurched on.

He’d proved her wrong two weeks later, appearing at the Fuma gate in simple clothes, bandages still peeking from under his collar, clutching a carefully wrapped bundle.

Her brothers had descended on him like hawks, using every trick short of actual interrogation to probe his intentions. Who are you? Why are you here? What’s your relationship with our sister? Their protective aura had practically crackled.

Saki had wanted to sink into the floor from sheer embarrassment.

But he’d taken it all with that same quiet politeness, bowing when appropriate, meeting their gazes without flinching. When he finally managed to hand her the bundle, his fingers had brushed hers for the briefest second.

“A… small token,” he’d said, voice rough but earnest. “Thank you for your help on the last mission. I apologize for dragging you into that mess.”

Inside had been this yellow senbon pouch—finely made, reinforced at the seams so the needles wouldn’t pierce through, dyed that warm, familiar shade of gold. Her name stitched discreetly inside the flap.

“I think the color suits you,” he’d added, softer, as if it were a secret. He’d looked straight at her when he said it. Cheeks flushed, eyes solemn, no hint of teasing or mockery. “Bright. Strong. Beautiful.”

Beautiful.

The word had sneaked into her chest and lodged there, stubborn and warm. She’d been so flustered she’d nearly dropped the pouch.

Now she tightened her grip on the silk until her knuckles ached.

Maybe her brothers saw a reckless child. Maybe Hana saw a clumsy blade trying to play at being a flower. Maybe half the clan thought she only had her rank because of her father’s blood.

But that boy—the quiet, steady heir of another clan—had never once looked at her with pity or exasperation or thinly veiled amusement. In his gaze, she was someone worth listening to. Someone strong. Someone… stunning, in her own way.

Her reflection in the mirror hadn’t changed. Same sharp angles. Same tanned skin. Same scar along her chin.

Bright. Strong. Beautiful.

Saki drew a slow breath.

“Fine,” she whispered to the girl in the mirror. “Maybe you’re not some porcelain doll. Maybe you never will be. So what?”

She set the pouch down just long enough to start laying out her gear.

From the chest: spare dark clothes for travel. A simple, undecorated kimono in muted grey that could pass as a servant’s garment. A few strips of fine silk to wrap over her chest and bind it flatter if needed.

From the stand: not her full naginata—that would be impossible to hide in a noble’s house—but a shorter, collapsible staff she could assemble quickly if she got her hands on any spear-like pole. Her kakute rings, small and unassuming, slid into a hidden pocket. A compact medicine kit: salves, bandages, a vial of strong stimulants.

From the shelf above the herbs: wrapped packets of dried leaves and seeds—some to heal, some to poison. Hollow senbon prepared to carry liquids. She opened the yellow pouch and began to slot the needles in, the rhythmic motion calming.

Each click of metal against silk felt like a promise.

She would walk into General Guhdo’s hall as who she truly was: a ninja of the Fuma clan. A healer. A fighter.

A blade that could dance if it had to.

And she would make sure that when she walked back out of General Guhdo’s gates, no one—not Koji, not Hana, not her brothers, not even herself—would ever again doubt what she was capable of.

T he Western ridge smelled of plum blossoms and ink.

Lord Fuku’s estate rose in tiers above the lotus pond district, white walls pristine, roofs curved like unfolding scrolls. Where the Fuma compound crouched like a hawk, all hard lines and training yards, this place seemed to float: manicured pines, raked gravel, koi gliding in still ponds.

Saki adjusted the plain grey kimono Koji had insisted on—no clan colors, nothing to mark her as Fuma.

Pretty. Useless in a fight.

The thought flickered and faded as she followed the servant along the stone path to the main house. The servant slid open a door and bowed low.

“Princess Fuma. Lord Fuku awaits.”

Inside, the receiving room was airy and elegant, tatami unblemished, lacquer gleaming. A calligraphy scroll hung in the alcove: a single, bold character for “Loyalty.”

A man knelt before the low table.

He was older than her father by a few years, perhaps—hair threaded with silver and bound neatly, beard trimmed close. His silk robe was understated but expensive, patterns of cranes and waves woven so fine they were more suggestion than display. His eyes, when he lifted them to her, were reddened at the corners, the skin beneath them shadowed.

“Princess Saki Fuma,” he said, inclining his head. “You have my gratitude for coming.”

Saki bowed deeply, forehead almost to the floor. “It is an honor, Lord Fuku. I regret the circumstances.”

He gestured gracefully. “Sit. We have much to discuss and little time before the household grows curious.”

She folded herself onto the cushion opposite him, hands resting on her knees. A servant poured tea and withdrew, the door whispering shut behind her.

For a moment, they sat in silence, the only sound the faint clink of porcelain as Fuku set his cup down untouched.

“My daughter, Kiku,” he said at last. “You know her situation?”

“Only the basics, my lord.” Saki kept her tone neutral. “Married to General Guhdo last year. Her letters ceased three months ago.”

His jaw tightened at Guhdo’s name. “Yes. An auspicious match, they called it. A powerful general to shield a lower-ranked house. A dutiful daughter to warm his bed.” His mouth curved in something that wasn’t quite a smile. “We tell ourselves these stories so duty tastes less like ash.”

Saki stayed silent. She didn’t need him to explain how noble daughters were spent like coin. He reached to the side and lifted a small lacquered box onto the table. When he slid the lid aside, the faint smell of old ink rose. Inside, letters lay bundled in silk ribbons, neat and precise.

“I kept every one,” he said, voice soft. “She wrote weekly at first. Long, rambling things about the weather, the servants, how drafty the general’s estate is in winter.” His fingers hovered above the top bundle but didn’t quite touch. “Then… they changed.”

He untied the ribbon with care and selected one, unfolding the paper to face her. “May I?”

“Please.”

He cleared his throat.

“ Within painted walls

the crane folds her wings in fear—

tea grows cold untouched.

Laughter hides the wolf’s sharp teeth,

drums drown out a daughter’s plea. ”

The poem hung in the air between them, brittle and bright.

Saki frowned slightly. “That’s…not exactly a housekeeping report.”

Fuku’s mouth twisted. “At first, the poems were light. Seasonal. Plum blossoms and moonlight. But Kiku always loved wordplay. She began…layering.” He slid the letter closer, tapping the characters for crane , wolf , drums . “In our family, the crane is code for a woman bound by duty. The wolf for danger in human form. Drums for war—or rowdy company.”

“So she was frightened,” Saki said quietly. “Of something—or someone—in that house.”

He nodded once. “Guhdo holds frequent feasts. Victorious campaigns, visiting dignitaries, any pretext will do. Kiku wrote of…guests. Men with daimyo crests who should not meet in private. Foreigners with no court sanction. Priests whose sutras smelled of blood instead of incense.”

Saki’s fingers tightened against her knees. Rogues. Traitors. Smugglers. Or worse.

He drew another letter from the stack, this one creased more from rereading.

“ Lanterns sway like hearts

when the wind of whispers blows.

Sake spills like lies.

At the gate, masked shadows bow—

foxes in a tiger’s clothes. ”

“Foxes,” Saki murmured. “Tricksters. Disguised intentions.”

“Precisely.” Fuku’s gaze sharpened. “Kiku was not trained as a spy. But she is observant. She knew enough of politics to be afraid. She stopped describing people’s faces and began describing…their silences. Who sat too close. Who spoke too little.”

“And then the letters stopped.”

His hand tightened on the paper until it trembled. “Three months ago. This was the last.” He passed it to her. The paper was thinner, the brushstrokes rushed.

“ Ink runs thin at dawn.

Night birds circle shuttered rooms.

Footsteps stain the floors.

If petals fall and are swept,

will roots still remember spring? ”

Saki went still.

His voice was rough. “When I inquired, Guhdo sent a message claiming my daughter had…disgraced herself. That she formed an attachment to a lowly musician and ran away.” He spat the words like poison.

“The letter doesn’t sound like someone packing elopement bags,” she said.

Fuku’s head snapped up, eyes blazing. “My daughter is many things—gentle, soft-hearted, too eager to please—but she is not faithless. She married that man because I asked it of her. To secure this house, to win me the general’s favor and, through him, the king’s.” His voice cracked. “She would not betray my trust. She respects promises too deeply.”

Saki looked down at the letter in her hands. Ink runs thin at dawn. Footsteps stain the floors.

Not the words of someone in love with the idea of escape. More like someone bracing for a blow.

“You think Guhdo is lying to cover… what?” she asked carefully. “Her death? Her imprisonment?”

Fuku sagged a little, as if the question had been a weight he’d been holding too long. “I do not know. I see three paths. One, she tried to flee and was caught. Two, she spoke when she should have stayed silent about his…guests. Three”—his throat bobbed—“he tired of her and…disposed of her.”

The room seemed to darken around the edges.

“If it is the first,” Fuku continued, voice barely above a whisper, “she may yet live. If it is the second, she is in danger from forces beyond my reach. If it is the third…” He closed his eyes briefly. “Then I must know. I cannot sit in this fine house and drink my tea while my daughter’s bones are hidden under some brute’s floor.”

Saki thought of her own father, of the way he weighed risk and gain with that distant, calculating gaze. Her clan’s history was written in ink and blood, in contracts and marriages and silent takeovers.

The Fuma absorbed lesser clans by marrying their daughters, bringing new blood and land under their roof. A pleasant story on the surface. Less pleasant, when you’re the daughter. She’d heard the whispers—of course she had. Servants gossiped. Junior clansmen talked when they thought she wasn’t listening.

They said the clan head was waiting for the right price for her. That as the heir, she was worth more—to secure something that truly mattered. Once she was married off, he could name Jiro or Saito as successor, keep leadership in hands that wouldn’t leave the compound.

She hated those whispers. Hated how, sometimes, late at night, they made a terrible kind of sense.

“Lord Fuku,” she said, forcing her attention back to the man in front of her, “if Guhdo has mistreated her—”

Fuku’s composure cracked. His hands clenched into fists on his knees.

“If he has raised a hand to her, if he has locked her away, if he has allowed others to harm her under his roof—bring her back.” His gaze bored into hers, fierce and pleading. “I care nothing for the alliance anymore. Let my house fall, let my name be mud, if that is the price. I will not leave her in a nest of vipers to keep my seat at court.”

Saki swallowed.

A father who would burn his position to rescue his daughter.

Would her own father do the same? Or would he decide her marriage, her alliance, mattered more than her comfort? Than her consent?

She didn’t know. She’d never asked. She wasn’t sure she wanted the answer.

If she let herself be shuffled on the board the same way, agreeing to marry a feared general because her father asked it of her, would she face the same fate one day, too?

She wasn’t ready to confront her father. Clan heads didn’t share their plans with daughters, no matter how skilled with naginata or poison. If she asked, he’d smile that mild smile and say, “All in time, Saki.” And tell her nothing.

So she’d chosen another path: missions. Success. Proving, over and over, that she was more than a pretty name to sign on a marriage contract. That she could lead in the field, not just be led to an altar.

It stung that so many in the clan still doubted her. That her own brothers wrapped their doubt in love and called it protection. But doubt or not, Koji had given her this. Trusted her with something that mattered.

And sitting here, watching Lord Fuku’s careful composure fray, she realized this mission was more than a test of skill.

It was a glimpse of a future she refused to accept.

“This is not an official inquiry,” she said slowly. “If I am discovered, the Fuma will be disavowed. You understand that.”

He nodded. “I do. I ask not for justice through the courts. Only for truth.”

“If Lady Kiku is alive and wishes to leave, I will bring her back,” Saki said. “If she cannot leave, I will learn why. If she is…” The word snagged in her throat. “If she is beyond saving, you will know. I swear it.”

Fuku’s shoulders trembled once, then stilled. He bowed, low enough that his forelock brushed the tatami.

“You have my house’s gratitude, Princess Saki Fuma. And mine as her father.”

Saki rose to her knees and bowed just as deeply in return, forehead pressing against woven straw.

“I will not fail her,” she said quietly.

As she straightened, her eyes caught on the calligraphy in the alcove. Loyalty . Thick, unbroken strokes.

A daughter’s loyalty to her father. A father’s to his daughter.

She wasn’t sure which weighed more. But she knew this much: Kiku had bent herself to duty for her family’s sake. That sacrifice deserved more than to vanish into rumor and convenient lies.

A girl she’d never met had traded freedom for her father’s ambitions. Saki might one day be asked to do the same.

She touched the yellow senbon pouch at her waist, hidden beneath her plain kimono. It hummed with a presence that steadied her.

Not yet , she thought. Not if I can help it.
