Chapter 3

← Back to The Masamune Clan: Specials [EN]

B y the time I cleared the academy doors, the sun was already dropping toward late afternoon.

Too late.

Anko had kept me after class to quiz me on treaties I hadn’t been present to study when they were signed. I’d answered every question I could, apologized when I couldn’t, bowed, and then run.

Now my lungs burned and my legs felt hollow as I cut across the courtyard toward the main gate.

“Masamune?”

I skidded to a halt.

Saki Fuma stood just outside the academy gate. Her long hair was tied up in a high tail, clan crest clear on her uniform. A small leather satchel was slung across her chest.

She took one look at me and frowned.

“You look like you’re about to fall over,” she said. “You’re pale.”

“I’m fine,” I lied, trying to catch my breath without gasping. “I’m just—late.”

“For what?”

“Clan matters. I’m supposed to be at the clan hall in—” I checked the angle of the sun, swallowed. “Seven minutes.”

She winced. “Ouch.”

I inclined my head. “If you’ll excuse me—”

I stepped to go around her. She moved neatly into my path again, eyes narrowing.

“Have you eaten?” she asked.

I hesitated a fraction too long.

Her expression sharpened. “You haven’t. Of course you haven’t. Honestly, Masamune men…” She shook her head, already pulling her satchel around. “Here. One second.”

“Fuma, truly, I don’t—”

“Stop.” She fished out a small glass vial, its contents a dark, murky green. She uncorked it; a sharp, herbal smell hit my nose—bitter, with something warm underneath.

She held it out.

“Drink.”

I looked from the bottle to her face. “What is it?”

“Herbal tonic,” she said. “For stamina. I make it myself. It tastes terrible, but it works. Ask any of my cousins.”

I believed the first part immediately.

“I don’t wish to take your supplies,” I said.

Her eyes flashed. “Don’t make me order you as Fuma heir.”

There was humor in her tone, but also steel. And the clock was still ticking.

I bowed slightly and took the vial.

The liquid was worse than it smelled—bitter, astringent, with a strange, earthy aftertaste—but it was warm going down, heat unfurling in my stomach and then spreading outward in a slow wave. My fingers, chilled from adrenaline and fatigue, tingled.

I handed the vial back, trying not to cough. “Thank you, Fuma.”

Her expression softened. “You’re welcome. Try not to die in there, all right? I’d like you conscious at the interclan meeting so I’m not the only young heir being bored to death.”

The thought of tonight made my insides twist, but I managed a faint smile. “I’ll do my best.”

“See you later then,” she said brightly, tucking the vial away. “Go.”

I went.

The road to the Masamune compound blurred under my feet. The tonic’s warmth buoyed me just enough to keep my stride from breaking, the world narrowing to the rhythm of my breath and the slap of my sandals against stone.

Elder Low does not tolerate excuses.

Punishment mission or not, heir or not, I doubted “school ran late” would qualify as a viable excuse.

By the time the clan training hall came into view, my shirt clung damp to my back and my pulse thundered in my ears. The tall wooden doors of the arena annex were open, new recruits in grey training uniforms filing through another entrance under the guidance of senior operatives.

Did he intend to make my evaluation public? My insides twisted. I wouldn’t put it past him.

A large wall clock hung above the main doors.

One minute to the hour.

I forced myself not to sprint the last few steps. Running into Elder Low’s presence like a panicked child would make a worse impression than being fractionally winded.

A lone figure leaned against the wall beside the door, arms folded, one ankle hooked over the other.

Sadi.

His posture was relaxed on the surface, but his eyes when they flicked to me were anything but. He took in my disheveled state—the sweat, the slightly uneven breathing—and one corner of his mouth curled.

“Cutting it close, aren’t you, Heir,” he drawled.

I stopped a respectful distance away and bowed. “Sadi.”

Up close, the differences five years made were stark. He’d grown broader across the shoulders since I last saw him outside a servant’s uniform. The easy strength in the way he held himself wasn’t just youth; it was hard-earned muscle, the kind that came from constant, brutal fieldwork.

There was a faint tightness around his eyes I hadn’t seen before. I knew, too well, where some of that came from.

A burn, intricate and dark, at the base of his spine. A brand of obedience older than our clans.

He straightened off the wall with a push of his shoulder. “What, did they keep you late at the academy to polish your halo?”

“I had additional questions to answer,” I said. There was no point denying it. “Anko is thorough.”

He snorted. “Of course she is.”

For a heartbeat, flickers of another time overlaid the present. Sadi slumped against the edge of a low futon, shirt rucked up, the skin over his lower back raw and inflamed where the slave brand had flared under his father’s control.

Later that same day, his fingers closing around my wrist as I slipped on a wet roof tile, yanking me back from a three-story fall. Shoving food into my hand afterward, grumbling about “useless slaves dying on my watch.”

He’d pretended not to remember any of it when my punishment service at Akito’s house ended. It was easier that way. For both of us.

Now, whatever softness there had been was buried under the same armor he always wore in public: sarcasm, sharp edges, competitiveness honed to a spiteful gleam.

The clock ticked over to the hour.

The inner door slid open.

Elder Low stood there, slight and straight-backed in his simple dark robes, white hair pulled into a severe knot at the back of his head. His eyes moved from Sadi to me, weighing, measuring.

“You’re on time,” he said. “Good. Come.”

We bowed as one. “Yes, Elder Low.”

His private quarters behind the arena were austere. Tatami mats, a low table, shelves lined with scrolls and a few carefully placed weapons. No clutter. No softness.

We knelt opposite him as he sat.

I bowed, forehead to the mat. “Tadashi Masamune, heir to the Masamune clan, reporting for evaluation.”

“Mm.” His fingers tapped once, twice, against his knee. “You have been away for eighteen months. Your mental conditioning has been tested adequately.” A faint, bitter humor in the word “adequately.” “Today I will see what remains of your physical capacity.”

“Yes, Elder.”

He turned his head to Sadi. “Sadi.”

Sadi straightened. “Elder Low.”

“You will help me test him.”

A brief spark lit in Sadi’s eyes. “With pleasure, Elder.”

Low ignored the tone. “The new recruits are assembled in the arena. They need to see what is expected of them if they are to rise in this clan. You will spar with Tadashi, in stages.”

He held up a thin, veined hand, counting off with his fingers.

“First, hand to hand. Then bo staff. Then weighted chain. Finally, wooden sword. You—” his gaze pinned Sadi “—will go hard. No holding back. Use every advantage. Do you understand?”

“Yes, Elder.” Sadi’s smile was sharp. “I’ll make it… educational.”

Low’s eyes slid back to me. “You will fight back, defend yourself, and remain standing until I call an end. There will be no intervals for rest.” His gaze did not waver. “If you fall, you will rise. If you are disarmed, you will improvise. If you disgrace yourself, you will not do it in front of the recruits. Is that clear?”

My heartbeat thudded once, heavy.

“Yes, Elder,” I said. “Understood.”

He watched me for a moment longer, as if testing the words for weakness. Then he rose in one smooth motion.

“Very well. To the arena.”

The roar of the recruits hit us as soon as we stepped through the side entrance.

The Masamune training arena was a vast rectangle of packed earth and smoothed stone, ringed by tiered wooden platforms. The lowest tier, nearest the practice floor, was crowded with the new intake—young faces, some eager, some nervous, all turning as we entered. Above them, scattered among the higher tiers, stood older operatives and clan observers, arms folded, eyes intent.

In the shadowed corner of the uppermost tier, two figures sat side by side.

Father.

And Akito.

For a moment, my feet almost stopped moving.

Father’s profile was stark against the dimness—strong jaw, straight nose, eyes half-lidded as if merely observing, but I knew that look. He missed nothing. Every misstep, every lag in response time, would be noted and weighed.

Beside him, Akito lounged with deceptive ease, one leg crossed over the other, his hands resting lightly on his knee. His gaze, when it met mine, was flat and cold. I remembered that gaze from another angle—yanked up by the hair from the floor with a collar biting into my throat.

I tore my eyes away and followed Elder Low to the center of the arena.

He raised one hand; the noise subsided into a bright, humming silence.

“Recruits,” he said, voice carrying without effort, “today you will see what your years of work may one day allow you to attempt. Tadashi Masamune, clan heir. Sadi Masamune, deputy clan heir. Both have bled for this clan. Both continue to do so.”

A flicker of something passed over Sadi’s face at that—pride, resentment, both—but he rolled his shoulders, loosening his joints.

“This is not a performance,” Low went on. “This is instruction. Watch. Learn.”

He stepped back, leaving the space between us clear.

Sadi’s eyes found mine, dark and bright all at once. “Try not to embarrass yourself, Heir,” he murmured, just loud enough for me to hear.

I bowed. “I’ll do my best not to slow you down.”

His grin flashed, wolfish. “We’ll see.”

Elder Low’s hand chopped the air.

“Begin.”

Sadi exploded forward.

It was like being hit by a storm.

He closed the distance in two strides, fist arcing toward my temple in a strike that would have rattled my brain if it landed clean. I got my forearm up in time; the impact sent a jolt down to my shoulder.

No time to absorb. I pivoted, letting the force go past, driving a palm strike toward his ribs, but he turned with me, forearm smashing down on mine. His knee came up, hunting my midsection.

I dropped my elbow, catching him on the thigh instead of the gut. Still enough power to make the muscle scream.

He was stronger than I remembered. Broad field work had added weight to his blows, each hit a promise of bruises I’d be feeling for days.

I flowed back, two steps, three, letting his combinations skim air or graze instead of land full force. His style was aggressive, heavy—a bulldozer compared to my scalpel. That had always been true. Today, the difference felt starker.

I slipped inside a wide hook and drove my knuckles into his ribs. He grunted, short and sharp, and answered with a headbutt that came too fast to fully avoid.

Our foreheads collided with a crack. White flashed at the edge of my vision.

Dirty, I thought, not without a flicker of admiration. Elder Low had told him to use every advantage. He was obeying.

I staggered half a step. His fist was already on its way toward my jaw.

I snapped my head back just enough that his knuckles scraped skin instead of shattering bone. Pain bloomed, hot and immediate, but teeth stayed intact. I caught his wrist with my other hand, twisted, using his forward momentum to pull him off balance.

We traded in tight quarters—elbows, knees, short hooks to the body. My back screamed with every twist, the welts threatening to tear. One of his punches slid past my guard and buried itself in my stomach; air punched out of me in a hiss.

I dropped, letting my legs go loose under me.

He followed, overextending just enough that my sweep took his feet from under him. He hit the packed earth shoulder-first, rolled, came up already laughing.

“Not bad,” he said under his breath.

I didn’t answer. Couldn’t. My lungs were busy clawing air back inside.

Elder Low’s voice cut across the arena. “Staff.”

A pair of senior operatives stepped forward from the sidelines, each tossing a polished wooden staff into the ring with practiced arcs.

Sadi snatched his out of the air one-handed. I caught mine with both hands, the familiarity of the smooth wood under my palms a small anchor.

We circled for a breath, staffs held low.

Then we went in.

The first clash of wood on wood rang like a bell. Sadi struck high, then low, then high again, using his reach to rain blows from different angles. I parried, the impacts stinging my hands. Sliding step, inside his guard, snap the butt end toward his knee—

He jerked the leg back, my strike grazing rather than crippling. His return swing cracked across my knuckles. Pain shot up my fingers; I almost lost my grip.

Don’t. If you drop it now, he’ll break you.

I adjusted, shifting to a looser, more flexible hold, letting the staff roll against my skin instead of fighting every impact head-on. Strike, block, redirect. Feet moving constantly, never straight back—diagonal, always looking for an angle.

We danced across the arena, the rhythm of the fight building: strike-block-strike, the sharp whip of wood through air. A blow slipped past my defenses and smacked into my ribs—right over the fading bruise Erachi’s shove had aggravated earlier. Stars burst behind my eyes.

The crack of wood on flesh was too close, too familiar. It echoed the sound of the cane my masters used on me. Pain, fire, the electric burn of the collar—

“Count your breaths,” his voice murmured unbidden in my head. “If you scream, I will increase the voltage. If you stay quiet, it ends sooner.”

The world tilted for a heartbeat.

My guard dipped. Sadi’s staff slammed into my thigh. My leg buckled.

The ground surged up. I twisted as I fell, taking the impact on my side instead of my already-abused back. The shock still sent a lance of agony through me.

Up. Get up .

I rolled, staff an extension of my arm, blocking the follow-up strike by instinct more than sight. Sadi’s face loomed above me for a moment, sweat beading his brow, eyes bright with adrenaline.

And something else. A flicker of—question? Understanding that my mind wasn’t in the fight? It was gone before I could be sure, swallowed under a renewed snarl of effort as he bore down.

From the corner of my eye, I saw Father’s silhouette shift slightly on the upper tier.

Disappointment? Calculation? I couldn’t tell at this distance. My chest tightened anyway.

You’re being watched. Stand.

I pushed off the ground, forcing my trembling muscles to obey. The recruits’ murmurs swelled—dismay, excitement, I couldn’t separate them.

Elder Low’s voice sliced through again. “Chain.”

Relief and dread tangled in my throat.

The staff was taken from my hands as I straightened; a weighted chain was thrust into its place. The metal lay heavy and cold across my palms, dark links coiled around a dense iron weight at one end.

My stomach knotted.

Chains.

Images surged up, unbidden. Shackles clinking in a darkened dungeon cell. The slap of a length of chain across bare shoulders. The rattle as I’d dragged a too-heavy bucket across stone, collar biting, Master Oni’s fingers forming the activation seal whenever I slowed.

A voice in my head: “ You are property. Tools do not flinch from their own handles. ”

“Heir.”

Sadi’s voice, low, close.

I realized I’d frozen, chain hanging limp at my side. His eyes, locked on mine, were suddenly very sharp, very present.

“You with me?” he muttered, too quiet for the recruits to hear.

“Yes,” I said. The word scraped. “Apologies.”

I wrapped the chain around my forearm, the way I’d been taught. The links were smooth, oiled, indifferent to what they’d been used for before.

This is not that. This is now. Move .

We moved.

Sadi swung first, the weighted end whistling toward my shoulder. I ducked; the iron grazed my hair as it passed, the wind of it cold against my scalp.

I snapped my wrist, sending my own chain out low. He jumped; the weight bit into the back of his calf instead of his ankle. He hissed, momentum carrying him forward anyway, his chain looping back in a vicious arc.

Metal on metal rang as our weapons collided. Sparks spat where the weights kissed mid-air.

Chains crack like whips when they hit flesh. Every impact, every near miss, ricocheted against old bruises in my memory as much as my skin. I forced my world to shrink: trajectory, distance, Sadi’s shoulders, his hips, the minute shifts that telegraphed direction.

A loop of his chain snagged my wrist, the sudden drag threatening to yank me off my feet. I stepped into it instead of back, twisting, wrapping the chain around my forearm to shorten the lever. His eyes widened as I pulled, hard.

He stumbled in, off-balance. My knee came up, driving toward his thigh. He turned enough that it hit muscle, not joint. Still enough to make his leg falter.

For a moment, we were chest to chest, chains binding us both, breathing hard, sweat dripping into our eyes.

“Don’t make me look bad in front of my father,” he panted, barely audible.

The words hit deeper than any strike.

I opened my mouth, closed it again. There was nothing safe to say.

Elder Low clapped once, sharp.

“Enough. Swords.”

The chains were taken from us, coiled away. Wooden bokken were thrust into our hands—the familiar weight of them settling into my grip like an old friend.

I exhaled.

This, at least, was home.

We faced each other, brought the blades up in mirrored guards.

“Begin,” Low said again.

Our first exchange was like a conversation spoken in an old, half-forgotten language. Cut, parry, countercut. Sadi favored power—overhand blows meant to overwhelm. I answered with economy, turning his strength aside with minimal contact, letting his momentum carry him past.

Wood rang against wood in rapid succession. Sparks of pain bloomed where stray strikes slipped through—his tip snapping into my upper arm, my blade rapping his fingers hard enough to make him swear under his breath.

As the tempo rose, the clean lines of formal kata began to fray. Footing slipped on churned earth. Breath grew ragged. Fatigue made fine control harder with each passing exchange.

Sadi adapted first, or perhaps he simply reverted. He stepped in close on a bind, shoulder slamming into my chest, his bokken hooking behind my ankle even as he drove his knee toward my ribs.

I twisted, taking some of the force on my hip instead. Pain flared; my stance cracked open for a heartbeat.

He shoved, hard.

I went down.

The fall was ugly and it hurt, my already-bruised back slamming into the ground. For a fraction of a second, the sky spun overhead. Somewhere above, a figure on the upper tier shifted and stood.

Father?

By the time I blinked grit from my eyes and pushed up on one elbow, the seat where he’d been was empty.

A hollow space opened under my breastbone.

Of course he left.

I had fallen. I had faltered.

Weak. Inadequate.

Another shadow loomed. Sadi, bokken raised for a finishing strike.

I could have rolled away.

Instead, I rolled toward.

The downward cut, meant for my chest, bit into the earth just past my ribs as I twisted. Pain screamed up my spine as I deliberately let myself complete the motion into another bad fall, shoulder slamming again into hard ground—but my legs scissored around Sadi’s planted foot.

His balance, already committed forward, shattered.

He crashed down beside me with a curse, his sword arm momentarily tangled under his own weight. My hand snapped out, fingers closing around his wrist, pinning it.

We moved in the same instant—him trying to yank free, me rolling over him, using the torque of my already-rolling body to pry the bokken from his grip.

The wooden blade flew loose, skittering across the dirt.

I came up on one knee, Sadi half turned under me, his free hand scrambling for leverage.

My bokken hammered into the ground just beside his head, close enough that the wind of it stirred his hair.

He froze.

For a hanging second, we stared at each other. His chest heaved, sweat streaking his face, eyes blazing.

Then he moved—fast, going for my ankle with a grab that would topple me if it landed.

I shifted, letting his fingers close on air, and brought the hilt of my sword down in a short, sharp arc.

It connected with the side of his head, just above the ear. Not full force—enough to rattle, not to break.

His eyes rolled for a heartbeat. Then his body went limp.

Silence flooded the arena, deep and stunned.

Elder Low raised his hand.

“Enough.”

The word was both judgment and reprieve.

I pushed to my feet, every muscle screaming. My bokken felt ten times heavier than it had when we started. Sweat stung my eyes, soaked my clothes. My breath tore in and out in ragged pulls that I forced into something like control.

Do not curl. Do not fold. You are being watched .

I turned to the recruits and bowed, as low and as steady as I could make it.

I straightened. The faces lining the arena blurred at the edges—wide-eyed, awed, envious, afraid. I couldn’t tell.

I looked up, toward the upper tier.

Father’s seat was still empty.

Only Akito remained, a dark slash against the wood. His gaze was fixed on me with an intensity that made my skin crawl—cold, appraising, something like hatred simmering just below the surface.

The same look he’d given me as I knelt on his floor in chains, back bloody from his anger taken out on someone more convenient than his son. The same faint curl of the lip that had accompanied the snap of his fingers and the flash of pain.

I dropped my eyes, my wooden sword suddenly heavy enough to drag my arm toward the ground.

My lungs burned. My pulse roared. Every part of me ached.

I had stayed standing. I had not broken.

But as Akito’s gaze bored into me, and the empty space where Father had sat yawned above, it felt less like victory than another test I’d only barely survived.
