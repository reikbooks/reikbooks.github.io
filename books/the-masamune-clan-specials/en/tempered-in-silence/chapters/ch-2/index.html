Chapter 2

B y the time the break bell rang, my hand was cramping from writing.

The sharp clang cut through the low murmur of the classroom. Chairs scraped back, feet shuffled, the air filled with the rustle of books and paper. Students poured toward the door in loose clumps, already talking about bento boxes and the new dango stall by the east gate.

I didn’t move.

My pen hovered over the page a heartbeat longer as the room emptied around me, then dropped back into motion. The line of text I’d been writing blurred for a moment, then came back into focus.

Focus.

I wrote the next sentence of my geography summary in neat, careful strokes, ignoring the hollow ache gnawing at my stomach.

Father hadn’t given me my allowance this morning. He hadn’t said anything about it—just passed Shiro the grocery envelope and walked out without a glance in my direction.

It wasn’t the first time. It wouldn’t be the last.

I shoved back the hunger. My body was trained to operate on less. It always had.

I shifted slightly in my seat, careful of my back. The welts there had stiffened over the morning, each movement tugging something too tight. I rolled my shoulders once, then stilled as a twinge shot across my spine.

Endure.

Across the room, someone snickered. Another voice chimed in, low but not low enough.

“Look, he’s not moving. Guess the little lord isn’t allowed outside without his minder.”

“Maybe he’s too stupid to find the cafeteria,” another replied. “He had to redo the year, didn’t he?”

The words pricked, but only faintly. I’d heard worse, from mouths that came with whips and collars and contracts, not wooden desks and ink-stained fingers.

I kept my eyes on my paper and continued writing.

The acceleration class was half-empty now, sunlight slanting across the ordered rows of desks. It was a prestigious program; compressed curriculum, heavier assignments, higher expectations. Students came from all over the continent to enroll: heirs of minor clans, children of wealthy merchants, a few foreign scholarship kids with accents that still stood out.

Most of them were older than me. To them, I was new. A stranger. A name they knew from clan politics and rumor, attached to a face they’d never seen in their classroom.

“Did you hear?” a girl near the front whispered to her friend, not as quiet as she thought. “He disappeared for a year and a half because he failed his exams. His father bribed the principal to keep him in the acceleration track.”

“Seriously? That’s so unfair.”

“I mean, what else could it be? Nobody misses that much class and doesn’t get expelled. My cousin said the Masamune clan basically bribes half the board.”

I turned a page in my notebook, making no sign I’d heard.

The truth was stranger than the rumor, and less flattering.

I hadn’t failed exams. I’d just failed the clan.

The punishment mission hadn’t appeared on any academy record. As far as these students were concerned, I’d simply vanished one term and reappeared in theirs.

They were curious. I could feel their glances, quick and sideways, whenever I raised my hand to answer a question. But curiosity only went so far. No one had chosen the empty seat beside me in the back row.

Too much trouble, I supposed. Too many eyes on a Masamune heir.

Better to watch from a distance than risk getting entangled.

A shadow fell across my desk.

I finished the sentence I was writing—“The western trade routes were formalized under the third daimyo”—and only then looked up.

Ginzo stood there, one hand braced on the next desk over, the other shoved into his pocket with calculated casualness. Two of his usual satellites flanked him. A third hung back, lounging against the windowsill with arms folded.

Ginzo was the oldest in the class, seventeen and failing to hide it. He wore the standard academy uniform jacket, but the buttons were undone, showing a patterned shirt beneath that probably cost more than most students’ entire wardrobes. A gold ring winked on his thumb as he drummed it against the desk.

Son of a Kawa Kingdom merchant, I’d heard. Rich. Indulged. Too used to getting his way to accept that strength was something you had to earn instead of buy.

“Masamune,” he drawled, stretching my surname into something lazy and faintly mocking. “You planning to marry that textbook, or did your daddy forget to pack your lunch with your honor today?”

A couple of the lingering students snickered. Others glanced over and then quickly away.

I capped my pen and laid it neatly beside my notebook.

“I’m reviewing for the history exam,” I said, keeping my tone mild. “The last unit was extensive.”

It wasn’t an answer to his real question, and we both knew it.

He leaned in a little, eyes searching my face for a flinch, a crack.

“You deaf as well as dumb?” he asked. “I asked if you were too scared to go outside. Or is the big bad cafeteria too much for the clan heir?”

“I don’t think the cafeteria poses a threat to anyone,” I replied. “Unless the curry is particularly bad today.”

One of his lackeys snorted before catching himself.

Ginzo’s mouth tightened for a fraction of a second. Then he flashed a smile that didn’t touch his eyes.

“Funny,” he said. “So you can talk. I was starting to think maybe your father bought you a place here and forgot to include a brain in the package.”

My jaw wanted to clench. I kept it relaxed.

“If my presence here bothers you,” I said, “you’re welcome to take it up with the administration. I’m sure they’d be interested in hearing your concerns about their entrance criteria.”

His smile thinned.

“Administration doesn’t scare me,” he said. “Rich people talk to rich people. They understand each other.”

His gaze flicked to the crest embroidered on my jacket, then back to my face.

“But you… you just stand there and everyone bends over. Must be nice.”

“I assure you, very few people bend over for me,” I said before I could stop myself. “Most of them would prefer I disappear.”

A couple of the nearby students shifted, startled by the honesty.

Ginzo’s eyes narrowed. He straightened slightly, choosing a new angle.

“So which is it, then?” he asked, voice carrying now. “Are you redoing the year because you’re stupid, or because you were too busy polishing your family’s reputation to bother showing up?”

“I’m catching up on missed material,” I said. “I’m not redoing the year.”

“Right,” he said, drawing the word out. “Because you’re special. All of us have to pass our exams like peasants, but you—” he raised his voice theatrically— “you get to vanish for a year and a half and waltz right back into the acceleration class. Must be all that…Masamune money.”

Soft laughter rippled around the room. No one joined in with much enthusiasm, but no one objected either.

My fingers curled slightly against the underside of the desk, out of sight.

He wasn’t entirely wrong. Clan influence had smoothed paths for me that others had to claw their way along. They’d never see the cost of that smoothing.

“I’m sure if you asked the principal,” I said quietly, “he’d let you take eighteen months off and see how it affects your grades.”

Ginzo’s eyes sharpened. He’d heard the edge under the politeness.

Before he could answer, the boy by the window pushed off the sill and sauntered forward.

“Oi, Ginzo,” he said, voice higher, more nasal. “Why waste your time? He’s not worth it.”

He came into clearer view as he stepped around Ginzo’s shoulder.

My breath stopped.

Erachi.

Not the name I’d called him by then—I’d never been allowed to use the master’s son’s name—but the face was the same. Slightly rounder now, a little less baby fat around the cheeks, but the sharp little eyes, the crooked front tooth, the way his upper lip curled when he sneered—

His father’s son.

For a heartbeat, the classroom dissolved.

The clean wooden desks vanished, replaced by carpet rolls stacked to the ceiling, the air heavy with dust and dye. Master Kenta’s shop. The slap of his sandals on the stone floor. The electric crackle of the collar’s seal being activated.

“Erachi,” Kenta’s voice, oiled and amused. “Our pet thinks he can fold faster if we let him rest his hands. Why don’t you remind him what happens to lazy animals?”

A jolt through the collar, my body locking and going limp at the same time, the world reduced to white-hot static—

A foot slamming into my ribs. A boy’s laugh, too high and thin.

“Get up, dog,” Erachi had jeered, his face flushed with excitement. “Or I’ll tell Father you bit me again.”

I dragged myself back to the present with an effort that felt like lifting a boulder.

Erachi was in front of me now, in an academy uniform, not rich merchant silks. No collar on my neck.

My fingers rose, unthinking, to the base of my throat, grazing the fabric of my turtleneck. The faint, raised ring of scar beneath the cloth seemed to burn under my touch.

He doesn’t know, I told myself. The collar had a disguise seal. He never saw my real face. To him, I was a nameless slave with different eyes, different hair.

He can’t recognize you.

“Look at him,” Erachi said now, smirking. “Shaking. Maybe he really is scared to go outside.”

He stepped in close, crowding my space, the same way he’d done in the back room of the carpet shop when his father wasn’t looking. His hand shot out suddenly and shoved me in the shoulder.

I let the force carry me rather than resist it. It still sent me stumbling back against the wall.

Pain flared along my spine as abused skin hit solid wood. For a second, my vision tunneled.

Don’t move. Don’t react. Stay still and it’ll be over faster —

The slave training overlapped with long-suppressed instincts that screamed the opposite: break his wrist, drive your elbow into his throat, end the threat before it multiplies .

I dug my fingernails into my palms, the small pain a spur to clarity.

You can’t. If you hurt him, the elders will hear. Father will —

“Careful,” I said, my voice even despite the tightness in my chest. “If you push too hard, you might hurt yourself.”

Erachi blinked, thrown for a moment by the composure.

Ginzo laughed. “Oh? The little heir’s got a tongue after all.”

He reached casually across my desk and plucked up the top sheet of paper.

My essay.

My heart lurched.

I’d stayed up past midnight writing that, cramming paragraphs between evening drills and dawn exercises. Anko had assigned it as a make-up for the units I’d missed during my absence. “No extensions,” she’d said. “Being behind is your problem, not mine.”

If I didn’t hand it in today, my marks would suffer. The elders monitored my academy performance almost as closely as my mission reports. A failing grade here was another lash waiting at home.

“What’s this?” Ginzo asked, waving the paper lightly. “Love letter to the principal? Or an apology to the rest of us for wasting our time?”

“I’d appreciate it if you didn’t touch that,” I said. Somehow my voice stayed quiet. “It’s due this period.”

He ignored me, eyes scanning the neat lines.

“‘Analyze the political impact of the second trade war on interclan relations,’” he read aloud in a mock-ponderous tone. “Wow. Look at all these big words. Did your cousin write this for you?”

A flicker of movement at the corners of the room—students inching closer for a better view. The circle around us tightened, leaving me pinned between the wall and a half-ring of bodies.

“I’m fairly certain Anko-sensei would recognize her own writing,” I said.

His grin widened. “Nah. Everyone knows she does everything for you. She probably wipes your ass too, Heir.”

The laughter this time had more bite.

Erachi shifted his weight, shoulder pressing harder into my chest. The contact sent a spike of pain through my bruised ribs.

“Give it back,” I said.

Ginzo’s eyebrows went up. “And if I don’t?”

My stomach knotted. I could see how this would play out. He’d rip the essay in half, maybe into quarters, enjoying the spectacle. I’d stand there and endure, because raising a hand would make it worse.

Then I’d stand in front of Anko with nothing to turn in. Her disappointment—sharp, clinical—would cut deeper than any strap. The elders would hear. There would be questions about my “discipline,” about whether the punishment mission had taught me anything at all.

Either way, I bled.

“If you destroy it,” I said carefully, “then I’ll still have to explain to Anko-sensei why I don’t have my assignment. She will ask who last saw it.” I met his eyes. “Are you sure you want that attention?”

For a moment, something wavered. He hesitated, weighing the satisfaction of tearing it up against the nuisance of staff involvement.

Then his jaw set.

“Please,” he said. “Sensei loves me. All the teachers do. I’m their favorite merchant’s little boy, remember?”

His fingers tightened on the paper.

“Besides,” he added, voice dropping, “you really ought to learn what happens when you don’t keep your things close, Masamune.”

He raised his other hand to grip the top of the sheet.

The sound of paper about to be torn is subtle—a faint stretch, a soft protest of fibers under tension. It hit me like a blade across the throat.

Something inside me went very, very still.

If you stand here and let him, you accept the beating from the elders as deserved.

If you stop him, you risk another kind of punishment.

There is no path without pain.

So choose the one where something is saved.

My body moved before the thought fully finished.

I shifted my weight off the wall, slipping sideways under Erachi’s arm instead of pushing back against it. He stumbled half a step as his support vanished, bumping into one of the other boys. Their elbows tangled.

“Hey—!”

I ducked low, letting their confusion hide my motion. My hand shot out, not for Ginzo’s wrist—that would be too obvious—but for the edge of the desk between us.

I hooked my fingers under it and gave the slightest jerk.

The desk’s front leg lifted just enough. Ginzo, caught mid-tear, had his weight on his toes. The shift unbalanced him. His arm swung out to compensate—

—and the paper slipped from his grasp, fluttering up and away in a brief arc.

I straightened into that arc. My fingers closed on the sheet just as it began to fall.

“What the—”

Ginzo swore, lunging for me, his face flushing dark with embarrassment and anger.

“I warned you,” I said softly.

I stepped back as he came forward. Not far. Just enough that when his foot, already off-balance from the tipping desk, landed, it came down on the strap of an abandoned satchel on the floor.

His heel slid.

Instinct screamed at me to steady him. Training whispered that accidents were sometimes permitted.

I pivoted aside.

His body twisted as he tried to catch himself. He went down hard on one knee, his hand slamming into the floor to break his fall. I heard a sharp intake of breath—pain, not serious, but not pleasant either.

Silence slammed over the room for a heartbeat.

Then the bell rang, shattering it. Break was over.

Chairs squeaked, voices rose in a sudden flurry as students scrambled for their seats. No one wanted to be caught standing when a teacher walked in.

They moved around us like water around stones, carefully not looking too closely. I saw the quick, sidelong glances anyway, the way they edged their desks a little farther away from mine.

Ginzo hissed through his teeth, cradling his wrist as he pushed himself up. A flush burned high on his cheekbones.

“You little—” he began.

The classroom door slid open with a brisk rattle.

Anko stepped through, scrolls tucked under one arm, a stack of graded papers in the other. Her gaze swept the room in a practiced scan, taking in the disarray—the half-standing students, Ginzo straightening with a wince, me a pace away with my books scattered at my feet.

Her eyes paused on us.

“Is there a problem?” she asked.

Her tone was even, but I knew her too well not to hear the steel under it.

I bent immediately, gathering my fallen notebooks into a tidy stack.

“No, Anko-sensei,” I said, straightening. “Ginzo stumbled over a bag. I was just about to help him up.”

Her gaze flicked to Ginzo. “Is that so?”

Color stained his ears. For all his bluster, admitting that he’d been nearly taken out by a skinny student with a misplaced satchel—and in front of a teacher, no less—would be worse than keeping quiet.

He clicked his tongue, looking away. “Tch. Floor’s uneven,” he muttered. “I’m fine.”

Anko held his eye a breath longer, then nodded once. “Find your seat before I decide to test your balance with a pop quiz.”

A ripple of relieved laughter moved through the room.

She turned to the front, setting the scrolls on her desk. “Assignments on my table, please. If you forgot yours, congratulations—you’ve just volunteered to write two extra essays by tomorrow.”

I stepped forward, smoothed my retrieved paper one last time to remove the worst of the creases, and placed it on the growing pile.

Her hand brushed mine briefly as she took it. A tiny shock went through me; for a moment, I thought she might say something, ask for an explanation.

She didn’t. Her face was as impassive as stone.

“Back to your seat, Tadashi,” she said.

“Yes, Sensei.”

As I walked down the aisle, I heard the whisper start up at the back.

“No wonder his grades are good,” someone murmured. “Anko-sensei is his cousin. Of course she goes easy on him.”

“Teacher’s pet,” another snickered. “Must be nice.”

I kept my expression neutral, my steps measured. Inside, something between a laugh and a sigh twisted in my chest.

If only they knew.

Anko did not go easy on me. Never had. If anything, she was harsher—a relentless, unyielding force who refused to accept anything less than perfection because “you’re Masamune; the world will always expect more.”

Late essays meant double work. Wrong answers in class meant extra drills after school. When I stumbled, she didn’t offer a hand; she handed me another weight and told me to keep climbing.

It wasn’t cruelty. It was… faith, in her own hard way.

I slid into my seat at the back. The chair creaked softly. The students on either side shifted their desks an inch farther away, as if my presence might be contagious.

Out of the corner of my eye, I saw Ginzo flex his wrist and glare at me, humiliation simmering in his eyes.

“This isn’t over,” he muttered under his breath as Anko began her lecture.

I didn’t look at him.

“Open your textbooks to page one hundred and twelve,” Anko said, chalk tapping briskly against the board. “We’ll be discussing the socioeconomic aftermath of the second trade war. Masamune, you’ll summarize last week’s reading.”

“Yes, Sensei.”

I stood, the familiar weight of expectation settling onto my shoulders like a cloak.

My back ached. My stomach was empty. My hands still remembered the shape of a collar long since removed.

It didn’t matter.

A Masamune endcures all trials, bears all burdens.

I drew a breath and began to speak.
