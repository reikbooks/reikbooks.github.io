The Going Rate for Fathers

Bonus Story · August 31, 2026 · from Masamune Clan Series [EN]

A Masamune Clan side story, set after Season 1

Tadashi Masamune had been dead for four months, which was why the knock on his door made no sense.

The dead did not get visitors. The dead got memorial stones, and lanterns lit in their names once a year, and the quiet, careful grief of siblings who were not allowed to know the truth. What the dead did not get was someone pounding on the door of their rented room above a tofu shop in a river town no one could find on a map, at an hour when respectable people were still buttering their rice.

He answered the door the way he answered most things: quietly, with his weight balanced and one hand free, and found the threat assessment came back alarming in an entirely unexpected direction.

The threat was six years old.

He stood on the landing in his best clothes, which were best in the way that a child's clothes are best: too formal, carefully brushed, with a collar that had been straightened by hands that loved him but could not sew. His hair had been combed with water and hope. He clutched a handkerchief bundle against his chest the way soldiers clutch wounds.

"Good morning," the boy said. He bowed so deeply he nearly toppled down the stairs. "Are you Tanaka Hiroshi?"

Tadashi glanced past him to the empty street. No handler. No watcher on the roofline. No killing intent. The boy radiated the opposite of killing intent, something so earnest it was almost difficult to stand near.

"I am," Tadashi said carefully. His alias. The name the clan had issued him along with his death. "You have the wrong house, I think."

"No, I don't." The boy straightened, took a breath, and recited: "I paid for a papa. One day. O-Tama-baachan said you were for rent. You're mine until the lanterns."

Tadashi waited for the mistake to resolve itself. Wrong door. Wrong town. Some other Tanaka Hiroshi, a mild man who did odd jobs and smiled at children, who had agreed to some festival arrangement and given the wrong directions home.

The boy held out the handkerchief bundle and unfolded it on the doorstep with the ceremony of a diplomat presenting treaties.

Inside lay eighty-four copper, mismatched coins saved one at a time. A wooden button. One candy, slightly melted.

And a folded piece of paper, covered in a child's best brushwork, each character wobbling like a boat in weather:

One papa. One day. Pay: eighty-four copper. From: Haru. To: Tanaka Hiroshi.

Below the terms, someone had drawn two stick figures holding hands under what was either a lantern or a very optimistic sun.

Tadashi had signed kill orders with less ceremony than it took him to read that paper twice.

"This must be a mistake," he said at last. It came out more gently than he intended.

"It's not," said Haru, with the absolute certainty of someone who had checked. "I counted it three times."

—

The story assembled itself in pieces over the next ten minutes, in the doorway, while Tadashi stood frozen in the wreckage of his quiet morning.

The festival was tonight — the night of the river lanterns, when the whole town walked down to the water and sent lights to their dead. And there was a custom, an old one, that fathers carried their children up the embankment steps and along the lantern road on their shoulders, so the little ones could see everything.

Haru's father had been a scaffolder. He had made a promise last autumn, kneeling in the genkan with his work coat still on: this year I'll carry you the whole way, even when you're heavy, even when you're six. Then spring came. Then the scaffolding came down wrong.

"He fell," Haru said, in the flat, careful voice children use when they have rehearsed a thing until it stops cutting. "So this year I'm too short for the lanterns."

A door opened down the walkway. A woman came out still tying her apron, dye stains on her fingers, her hair half-finished, moving with the panicked speed of someone who had turned her back for one hour. O-Tama, the landlady, shuffled behind her wearing the expression of a woman who had caused all of this on purpose and regretted nothing.

"Haru! I am so sorry, Tanaka-san, he's been talking all week about renting a papa, I thought he meant a toy, I thought he was saving for my birthday—" The words tumbled out of her like beads off a snapped string. She was already reaching for the handkerchief bundle to give it back.

"I'm not buying Papa back," Haru said, stepping behind Tadashi's leg as though a near-stranger's doorway were fortress walls. "I'm buying a papa. It's different."

And there it was. The whole tragedy of the thing, taxonomized by a six-year-old.

Tadashi looked at the coins. At the button; a year of small economies, New Year's money, festival money, coins that could have been candy. At the contract with his false name written in a child's hand, because the boy had asked around the neighborhood for the quiet man upstairs, the one who fixed things and didn't talk much, and old O-Tama had said, apparently, everything's for rent if you ask polite enough.

He had been appraised his entire life. Scored at six years old. Weighed against his sister, priced against the clan's debts, valued downward after every failure. He knew exactly what he was worth to the people who owned his name.

He searched this arrangement for the mistake and could not find one. That was the unbearable part. There was no mistake. A child had simply decided he was worth eighty-four copper, and Tadashi, who had been haggled over by kings and clan elders all his life, could not for the life of him argue with the only tender offer he had ever received.

"Haru." Miyo knelt and turned her son to face her, speaking to him for once instead of past him. "Tanaka-san is a stranger. We can't ask a man we don't know to carry your father's promise."

He held up one hand, and she stopped, as if she was trained to do that.

"The contract has been accepted and payment rendered," he said. "I don't renege on contracts."

Then he counted the coins back into the handkerchief, all but one, which he closed in his fist.

"A contract without payment is a favor," he explained, to the mother as much as the boy. "Favors can be rescinded. Payment makes it binding." He looked down at Haru. "Terms. You hold my hand at crossings. You tell me immediately if you are hurt. You eat dinner. You are home before the last lantern is lit."

Haru saluted. "Yes, Papa."

Something in Tadashi's chest performed a maneuver he had no training for.

Miyo looked at him for a long moment; at the calluses, the stillness, the way he had knelt to be at her son's eye level without seeming to decide to... and whatever she saw, she decided to trust it, because grief leaves no time for better options. "Back by the last lantern," she said. "And don't let him eat his weight in sweets."

"I will return him," Tadashi said formally, "in the condition received."

O-Tama made a sound like a kettle achieving enlightenment.

The sandal strap snapped in the second street.

Tadashi knelt, produced a cord from his sleeve, and had it mended in under a minute with the same knot he used on prisoners' wrists, adjusted for mercy. Haru watched with the awe usually reserved for fireworks.

"Are you a carpenter?"

"Something like that."

The market was worse. The tofu seller did a visible double-take. "Didn't know you had a boy, Tanaka-san!" And then, squinting up: "He's got your eyebrows."

Tadashi, who had worn three names and two faces this month and could no longer fully account for either, decided that today's face had Haru's eyebrows. He bowed and apologized to the tofu seller for existing. Haru bellowed, at a volume that turned four heads, "PAPA, THE CANDY MAN SAID WE CAN HAVE EXTRA IF YOU SMILE," and Tadashi apologized to the candy man as well.

"Papa says sorry too much," Haru informed the street at large.

At the goldfish stall, Tadashi held the paper scoop with the steadiness of a man who had been trained since childhood that his hand must never, under any circumstances, tremble. He swept the pond once. The vendor's face went through several stages of grief.

"Don't chase it," Tadashi said, handing over the bowl. "Wait for it to forget you're there."

Haru considered this. "That's sad."

"It's patient."

The goldfish was named Hiro, which Tadashi understood, several seconds too late, meant his cover identity had now been baptized by a six-year-old and would be defended to the death by a fish.

The bad moment came at the shrine steps, where older boys were congregating, and one of them saw Haru's face and remembered. "Rent-a-papa!" one called, sing-song. "How much for a minute?"

Haru's chin did the wobble. His fists curled.

Tadashi did not raise his voice. He did not, in fact, do anything visible at all; he simply turned his head, and something in the geometry of the afternoon rearranged itself, and the boys discovered they were needed elsewhere. Then he knelt in the dust to Haru's level.

"You didn't rent me," he said. "I accepted a contract. I read the terms. I chose it."

Haru studied him with wet, ferocious eyes. "Then they're wrong."

"They're wrong."

They ate lunch on the shrine steps: onigiri from Haru's cloth, slightly crushed, wrapped in the same handkerchief that had held the coins. Tadashi ate every grain, and thought, without meaning to, of a woman in another time who had fed a broken stranger and asked nothing.

He carried Haru through the lantern crowds as the light went amber, small arms around his neck, and his ribs — still knitting, still remembering — complained, and he adjusted his breathing the way he had been taught and paid the ache no mind. Some pains you budget for. This one he had chosen.

"Do you live all alone?" Haru asked, somewhere past the bridge.

"Yes."

"In that whole house?"

"It's one room."

"That's the saddest thing I ever heard."

And then, because children circle grief the way wasps circle sugar: "Where's your papa?"

"Far away."

"Do you miss him?"

Tadashi was quiet one beat too long. Haru, gently, the way one explains rain: "Missing him means he's real. Mama told me."

Tadashi looked at the road ahead and said, "Then he's very real."

⁂

The river at dusk was a slow procession of fire. Hundreds of lanterns, named and nameless, slid out onto the black water while fathers hoisted children onto shoulders all along the bank, and the whole town spoke to its dead in the only grammar the living are allowed.

Haru had drawn the two figures on his lantern himself. He made Tadashi do the letters.

"Gen-ta," he spelled out loud, helpful. "I can't do hard kanji. He was a scaffolder. Can you write scaffolder?"

Tadashi could. The same hand trained for death warrants and clan seals wrote a dead scaffolder's name in a child's best hand, in the smallest, most careful brushwork it owned.

Then Haru pushed a second, unlit lantern into his hands. "Everyone floats one."

"I don't have anyone to send it to."

"Everyone has somebody. Even far ones." A pause. "'Specially far ones."

So Tadashi sat at the water's edge with a brush that was really a nail of charcoal, and almost wrote names, and did not. A man officially dead does not write his family's names at a public river. So he wrote a single word instead.

Soon.

Then, because the night was already impossible, he took a third lantern, small and plain, and left it entirely blank, and set it on the water with the others.

"Whose is that one?" Haru asked.

"Someone who lost his name."

Haru watched the blank lantern drift in among the glowing, named ones, keeping easy company with all those burning griefs, and delivered his verdict with the calm of a magistrate: "The river knows everybody. You don't need a name."

"No," Tadashi said quietly, watching it go. "I don't suppose you do."

It was on the walk back down the bank that Haru finally broke; not at the river, but after it, safe past the worst of it, the way children break when the grown-ups have stopped watching. A man had lifted his son overhead, and the boy had screamed with joy, and something in Haru simply sat down inside.

"Papa Genta was supposed to carry me the whole way," he said, and then he was crying, the terrible silent kind, and Tadashi picked him up without deciding to and held on.

He knew how to hold a wounded comrade under fire. Nobody had taught him this. He counted the boy's shuddering breaths the way he counted enemies, and waited, and did not say it gets better, because he did not lie, and children can smell a lie from farther than adults can.

"He kept the promise," Tadashi said instead.

Haru hiccuped against his collar. "How?"

"You're seeing all the lanterns," Tadashi said. "From very high up."

Haru was quiet for a long time. Then, small, muffled, into Tadashi's shoulder: "...Tell him thank you."

"I will," said Tadashi, who knew a great deal about speaking to the dead, and meant to keep that contract too.

⁂

Miyo was waiting under the lamp outside the dyer's house, and the worry on her face folded, when she saw them, into something older and more tired; relief with grief still living in it. She started to reach for real coins, proper payment, and Tadashi shook his head once.

"The fee was one copper. I was paid in full."

But Haru, half-asleep and therefore invincible, would not allow it. He dug a single coin out of the returned handkerchief and pressed it back into Tadashi's palm and closed his fingers over it with both small hands.

"You keep one," he ordered. "So next year I hire you again and you have to come. It's the rule. Contracts."

Tadashi, out-lawyered by a six-year-old using his own argument, bowed his head. "It's the rule," he agreed.

"Next year, will you—"

"I can't promise," Tadashi said, before the hope could get all the way out. It was the one honest thing he had. "But if you knock, and I am home, I will answer."

Haru considered this with the gravity of a general reviewing a bad treaty position. "Then I'll knock every single day," he concluded. "Then you'll always be home."

Tadashi had no counterargument. He reached out instead and straightened the boy's collar, A small, severe, unconscious gesture, — and discovered in his own hands a thing he had never once been on the giving end of.

Then Haru fetched the fishbowl from the step and pushed it into his arms. "Hiro's yours now. Papas keep the goldfish." A pause; a correction, borrowed from somewhere safer: "Mama says families share."

⁂

The room above the tofu shop had been a hiding place for four months: one cot, one window, one man the world was not allowed to know was alive.

Tadashi set two coins on the windowsill: the fee he'd kept that afternoon, and the retainer Haru had pressed into his palm for next year. He set the fishbowl on the desk, where it caught the last of the lantern light off the river and scattered it, faintly and absurdly, across the walls.

He thought of the compound he could not enter, and the father he could not visit, and the siblings who might, at this very hour, be floating a lantern with his name on it. I hope it's a good one, he thought, and had to sit down on the edge of the cot for a moment, hands loose in his lap.

He had watched a hundred families through their windows in his line of work: dinner tables, birthdays, ordinary lamps, and had never once been inside one. Tonight he had been, briefly, the inside of one. Eighty-two copper returned. Two kept; one for the day he'd been paid for, and one already standing for the day he hadn't been yet. A broken sandal, a lopsided goldfish, a word on the water.

The oath said his heart was a vessel for the clan's legacy. It said nothing about what else a vessel could hold.

He thought he might tell his brother, one day, when the debt was paid. Shirasu would laugh himself hoarse, and then go quiet, and then say something unbearably kind.

Tadashi lay down in the dark and listened to the small sound of water breathing in the bowl, and the room, for the first time all year, did not feel like a hiding place.

It felt like a place someone knew how to find.

If the knock came again... small, certain, at an indecent hour, he already knew what he would say.

I'm home. Come in.
